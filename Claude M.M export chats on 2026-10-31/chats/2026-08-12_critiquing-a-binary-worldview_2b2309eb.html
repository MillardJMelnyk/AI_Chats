CLAUDE2026-08-12 16:05 UTC

It destroys the distinction it's pointing at. Take "cultist" broadly — believing on group allegiance rather than evidence, holding the belief immune to revision, treating dissent as betrayal. That names a real failure mode. But it's a set of behaviors that shows up in degrees, per topic, per person, per week. Binning humanity into two boxes converts a useful variable into a useless label.

The categories are neither exhaustive nor exclusive. Most people are indifferent to most questions, not inquiring or dogmatic about them. And the same person is genuinely curious about his trade and utterly tribal about his politics. Every real case sits in both boxes or neither.

It's built so that disagreeing with it proves it. Anyone who objects has self-identified as the other kind. A claim that converts its own critics into confirming instances isn't a claim; it's a loyalty test with the grammar of a taxonomy. The statement's form is the thing it condemns.

It has no way to distinguish trust from credulity. Nobody understands more than a sliver of what they act on. Deferring to a cardiologist, a structural engineer, or a compiler is division of cognitive labor, not submission. Without a criterion separating warranted from unwarranted deference, "cultist" absorbs nearly everyone, and the term stops sorting anything.

It compares a desire to a habit. "Wants to understand" is a motive; the cultist failure is a practice. The two aren't opposites — motivated reasoning feels exactly like inquiry from the inside, which is why sincere truth-seekers produce dogma routinely. That's the interesting case, and the dichotomy renders it invisible by assuming the wanting is sufficient.

Nothing follows from it. It makes no prediction and licenses no test. It sorts people by an unobservable motive, and in practice the sorting runs backward: agreement gets read as understanding, disagreement as cultism.

CLAUDE2026-08-12 16:12 UTC

Three of my six points are dead. Here's what survives once the statement is treated as a hypothesis.

Conceded: trust/credulity was off-frame — deference is orthogonal to wanting. "Unobservable motive" was wrong: seeking out disconfirming sources, passing an ideological Turing test, asking questions whose answers could cost you, saying "I don't know" instead of guessing, changing position when the data changes — all observable, all codeable, all countable. And self-sealing is a defect in an argument, not in a hypothesis; a hypothesis doesn't owe its critics anything.

What's left:

Under your definition the partition is analytic, so the empirical content has to be bimodality. "Cultist = doesn't want to understand" splits the population by excluded middle. True by construction, tells you nothing. The hypothesis only says something when it says the distribution has two humps and a gap — that people cluster at the ends rather than smearing across the middle. That's the testable core, and it's the part most likely to fail. Score people on those observable markers and the honest prediction from what's known about motivated reasoning is a single fat middle, not two peaks.

It predicts a trait; the evidence points to a state. "Kinds of people" claims stability across time and topic. Test it: score the same person on his trade, his politics, and his marriage. If the mechanic who reasons carefully about a misfire refuses to look at data on the thing his in-group has already decided, the score is topic-conditional and "kinds" is the wrong ontology — like sorting humanity into hungry people and full people. Same test across six months catches the time axis. This is the one measurement that could kill it outright, and it's cheap to run.

The label contaminates the instrument. Not a philosophy objection — a coding-reliability one. Nobody self-reports as a cultist, so measurement has to be observer-coded, and observers reliably score people who agree with them as inquiring and people who don't as closed. Blind the coders to the content of the position or the effect size you measure is your own priors.

No pre-registered decision rule. Someone scoring high on three markers and low on three others — third kind, boundary case, or measurement error? Decide before you look, or the hypothesis survives every result.
