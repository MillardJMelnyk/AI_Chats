i am having trouble germinating bell pepper seeds, i have them in a wrapped in damp napkin in a plastic bag and between 2 pieces of cardboard, i read they need heat so i put them under my laptop but they are still not germinating

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:17 UTC

Peppers are slow — 7 to 21 days at 27–29 °C, and they stall completely below about 21 °C. Most people quit too early.

Likely problems, in order:

  1. Seed source. If they came out of a green bell pepper, they're immature and mostly dead. Red/yellow/orange fruit seeds are riper but still hit-or-miss. Packet seeds are the reliable route.
  2. The laptop. It idles cool and cycles, and the cardboard insulates the seeds from it. You're probably sitting at room temp. Better spots: on top of the fridge (the warm patch near the back), near a water heater, or a cheap seedling heat mat.
  3. Cardboard. Drop it. It wicks moisture out of the napkin and blocks heat. Damp napkin, sealed zip bag, that's it — no cardboard, no air gap.
  4. Moisture. Damp, not wet. Soaking seeds rot before they sprout.

Check the bag every couple of days; open it briefly for air, re-dampen if the napkin is drying out. If nothing by day 21 at proper heat, the seeds are the problem, not the method.

what else should i know?

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:20 UTC

Once they sprout:

Move to light immediately. The moment you see a root, plant it. Seedlings under weak light stretch into useless leggy stems within days. Bright window or grow light.

Transplant technique. Plant the germinated seed root-down, 6mm deep, in a small pot of seed mix. Don't handle the root — move it with the paper if you have to.

Seed leaves vs. real leaves. The first two are cotyledons, not real leaves. Don't fertilize until the second set (true leaves) appears, then feed at quarter strength.

Don't over-pot. Small container until roots fill it, then step up. Too much soil holds water and rots young roots.

Peppers are slow all the way through. Roughly 60–90 days from sprout to first fruit. They also drop flowers above about 32 °C or below 16 °C at night — Querétaro's spring heat and cool nights both matter, so aim to have plants ready to set fruit before the worst of the pre-rain heat in May.

Sun. At 1,800 m the UV is brutal. Harden seedlings off gradually over a week before they go outside full-time or they'll bleach and burn.

Water. Deep and infrequent. Peppers hate soggy feet, and the dry air means containers dry fast — check by weight, not schedule.

what about tomatoes, i have some sprouts, they are cherry tomatoes, when should i plant them in bigger pots? can i put more than 1 sproutin a pot? are there tricks like cropping the tops to increase yeild?

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:23 UTC

When to pot up: at the first set of true leaves. Bury the stem deep — up to the cotyledons. Tomatoes grow roots from buried stem, and that extra root mass is free vigor. Do it again at every step-up: 5cm cell → 10cm pot → final container. Each time, plant deeper.

One per pot. Cherry tomatoes get huge — a single indeterminate plant wants 20+ liters and will fill a 1m² trellis. Two in a pot means two half-starved plants competing for the same water and nitrogen. You can start multiples in one cell, but separate them at the first pot-up (tease apart when the mix is wet, hold by a leaf not the stem).

Topping: don't, on indeterminate cherries — you'd cut off the growth point that produces all future fruit trusses. Topping is a late-season trick only: in September, cut the growing tip to force the plant to ripen what's already set instead of making new flowers.

What actually raises yield:
- Suckering. Pinch the shoots that grow in the 45° angle between stem and branch. Left alone the plant becomes a bush; pruned to one or two main stems it puts energy into fruit and gets airflow. Cherry types are forgiving — some people let them run — but in a pot, prune.
- Stake before they need it. Put the support in at pot-up. Driving a stake in later tears roots.
- Consistent water. Wet-dry-wet swings cause fruit split and blossom-end rot. Mulch the pot surface.
- Feed high-potassium once flowering starts. Nitrogen before that, potassium after.
- Hand-pollinate. Flick the flower trusses or shake the stem daily around midday. Indoors or in still air they set poorly without vibration.
- Strip lower leaves as the plant grows — anything touching soil or below the lowest fruit truss. Cuts disease.

Cherry varieties are the most forgiving tomato there is. Yours will likely produce more than you can eat.

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:31 UTC

In pots, well-managed: 2–4 kg per plant over a season. In the ground with good soil and full sun, 4–7 kg.

So four plants in containers gets you roughly 10–15 kg spread over about three months of picking — call it 100–150g a day at peak, which is a handful or two.

The biggest levers, ranked by how much they actually move kilos:

  1. Container size. 20L is the floor, 40L doubles your ceiling. Nothing else you do compensates for a small pot.

Elaborate on each of the following

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:34 UTC

Container size

Tomato roots want depth more than width. An indeterminate cherry will send a taproot down 60cm+ if allowed, and side roots spread as far as the canopy. In a pot, whatever you give it is all it gets — root volume is the hard cap on how much water and nutrient the plant can access per day, and that translates directly into how many fruit trusses it can support at once.

Practical numbers: 10L gives you a stunted plant and under a kilo. 20L is the working minimum — you'll get 2–3 kg but you'll be watering twice a day in May. 40L gives you 4+ kg and buffers you against a missed watering, which in Querétaro's dry season is the difference between a healthy plant and a stalled one.

Depth over width if you have to choose: a 30cm-wide × 40cm-deep pot beats a 45cm-wide × 25cm-deep one of the same volume.

Fabric grow bags are the best value — they air-prune roots (roots hit the fabric, stop, and branch instead of circling), and they breathe, which matters less in your climate than in a humid one but still helps. Downside: they dry out faster than plastic. In Querétaro that's a real cost. Plastic pots hold moisture better; if you use them, make sure of drainage holes and don't let them sit in saucers of water.

Dark pots in full sun at 1,800m cook the root zone. Roots suffer above about 35°C. Shade the pot itself — tuck it behind something, wrap it in light cloth, or group pots so they shade each other's sides. The leaves want sun; the roots don't.

Sun

Six hours direct is the minimum for fruit. Eight is better. "Bright shade" or "lots of indirect light" produces leaves and almost no tomatoes — the plant photosynthesizes enough to grow but not enough surplus to fill fruit.

Direct means the sun physically strikes the leaves, no glass, no shade cloth, no wall in the way. Measure it before you commit: pick a day, check the spot every hour, count the hours it's actually in sun. People overestimate this by two or three hours routinely.

Morning sun beats afternoon sun. Morning dries the leaves early (disease control) and gives you photosynthesis during the cooler part of the day. Harsh afternoon sun in May, at your altitude, is closer to a stress than a benefit.

Your specific complication: above roughly 32°C daytime or 21°C night, tomato pollen goes sterile and flowers drop without setting fruit. Querétaro's April–May pre-rain heat hits that. So the play is timing — get plants big and flowering early, so a first wave of fruit sets before the heat peak. Then the plant idles through the hot weeks and starts setting again when the rains cool things down in June/July. Two harvest windows, not one continuous one. This is why potted plants are actually an advantage for you: you can move them into afternoon shade during the worst weeks.

Water consistency

The mechanism behind both the classic container problems is the same: the plant can only move calcium into fruit while water is flowing steadily through it. Let the pot dry out, and calcium delivery stops for a few hours — the fastest-growing tissue, the blossom end of a developing fruit, doesn't get its share and the cells collapse. That's blossom-end rot. It's not a calcium deficiency in your soil; adding calcium won't fix it. It's a watering-rhythm deficiency.

Splitting is the reverse of the same problem: fruit sits at a certain size while dry, you soak the pot, the plant floods water into the fruit faster than the skin can stretch, and it cracks. Cherry tomatoes are especially prone because the skin-to-volume ratio is high.

How to actually do it:

Water deep. Apply until water runs from the drainage holes. Half-watering only wets the top layer and trains roots to stay shallow, where they dry out first.

Judge by weight, not schedule. Lift the pot or tip it. Heavy = wet, light = dry. A calendar schedule fails because water use varies enormously with temperature and plant size — a big plant on a hot May day can drink 4L; the same plant in cloudy July might need nothing.

Mulch the surface. 3–5cm of straw, dry grass clippings, bark, whatever's cheap. In your dry air this is not optional; it cuts evaporation from the soil surface by more than half and stops the top layer from crusting. It also stops soil splashing onto lower leaves, which is how most fungal disease starts.

In peak heat, expect twice daily for a 20L pot. Once daily for 40L. This is the main argument for the bigger pot.

Feeding

Potting mix comes with fertilizer that lasts roughly six weeks. After that, in a container, the plant is eating whatever you give it and nothing else — there's no soil reserve to draw on, and every watering flushes nutrients out the drainage holes. This is the single most common reason container tomatoes look great in month one and mediocre in month three.

Phase one — from transplant to first flowers: balanced fertilizer, every two weeks, at label strength. You want leaves and stems now; that's the factory that will build the fruit later.

Phase two — first flowers onward: switch to high-potassium. Potassium is what fills and sweetens fruit. Excess nitrogen at this stage gives you a beautiful green monster with almost no tomatoes — the plant keeps choosing vegetative growth over reproduction. Look for a tomato-specific fertilizer, or anything where the last number (K) is clearly the biggest. Every 10–14 days.

Liquid over granular in pots — you control the dose and it's immediately available. Diluted and frequent beats concentrated and occasional.

Skip epsom salt and gypsum. Both are internet gardening folklore. Epsom is magnesium sulfate; unless you have a diagnosed magnesium deficiency (yellowing between leaf veins on older leaves), adding it can actually block calcium uptake and cause blossom-end rot. Gypsum is calcium sulfate, sold as a BER cure — but BER isn't a soil calcium problem, so it does nothing.

Compost worked into the top of the pot every month is a good slow supplement and improves water retention. Free if you make it.

Pruning

A sucker is the shoot that emerges from the 45° crotch between the main stem and a leaf branch. Left alone it becomes a full second plant — its own leaves, its own flower trusses, its own water demand — growing off your one root system. In the ground with unlimited roots that's fine and even desirable. In a 20L pot, you're asking a fixed root volume to feed three or four plants' worth of foliage. Everything gets less.

Pinch them at 2–5cm, with your fingers, in the morning when the plant is turgid — they snap off cleanly and the wound seals fast. Waiting until they're 20cm means a large open wound and wasted plant energy you can't get back.

One or two main stems for a container. One stem is easier to manage and gives you the earliest fruit; two gives more total fruit if the pot is 40L. Pick your second stem deliberately — the sucker immediately below the first flower truss is the strongest one — and pinch everything else from then on.

Strip leaves below the lowest fruit cluster. Those leaves are shaded, contributing almost nothing, and they're the closest to the soil, which is where fungal spores splash up from. Remove them progressively as the plant climbs.

Don't over-prune. This is the counterweight, and it's where people go wrong after watching pruning videos. Leaves are the engine. A plant stripped bare has nothing left to build fruit with, and exposed fruit sunscalds — a real risk at your UV levels. Remove suckers and lower leaves; keep the healthy canopy above the fruit. If you're taking off more than a few leaves in one session, stop.

Stake at pot-up time. A 2m stake or cage, in the pot before the plant needs it. Driving one in later shears through the root ball. Tie loosely with soft twine or cloth strips, figure-eight around stem and stake so the loop doesn't strangle the stem as it thickens.

Harvesting

The plant's goal is seed, not your dinner. Ripe fruit hanging on the vine signals "reproduction accomplished" and it eases off flowering. Strip it and the plant keeps pushing new trusses. At peak that means picking daily or every other day — with cherry types, a plant can put out a hundred fruits and they'll come in waves.

When to pick: full color and a slight give under gentle pressure. Days-to-maturity numbers on seed packets are averages and mean little; go by look and feel. Cherries ripen unevenly within a single truss, so pick individual fruits rather than waiting for the whole cluster.

How: grip the fruit and twist upward against the little joint on the stem — it snaps cleanly. Or use scissors. Pulling straight down tears branches off.

Pick just before full ripeness if birds are a problem. A tomato picked at first full color ripens fine on a counter, and beats one pecked open on the vine.

Don't refrigerate. Below about 12°C destroys the aromatic compounds and turns the texture mealy — permanently, so it doesn't recover on the counter. Room temperature, out of direct sun, stem-side down. For genuine surplus, freeze whole as mentioned, or halve and slow-roast at low heat then freeze in oil — that's the version that keeps for months and tastes better than fresh in winter.

u have some 10L jugs i buy here for water, would those be ok? they are maybe 20-25 cm in diameter and 40-50 cm in height

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:37 UTC

Workable, but they're at the bottom edge. Your dimensions actually compute to more than 10L — 20cm diameter × 45cm tall is about 14L, and 25 × 50 is closer to 24L. Measure by filling one with water if you want to know. Anything at 15L or above is fine for a cherry tomato; 10L genuinely is not.

The good news is the shape. Tall and narrow is exactly right for tomatoes — that 40–50cm of depth is worth more than the same volume in a wide shallow tub.

To convert them:

Cut the top off. Not just the neck — cut the shoulder off too, so you have a full-diameter opening. A narrow neck makes it impossible to fill properly, impossible to top-dress with compost, and impossible to get the root ball out at season's end. Serrated knife or a jigsaw; score a line first so the cut is straight.

Drainage. Six to eight holes of about 1cm in the bottom, plus another ring of holes in the sides about 2cm up from the base. The side holes matter — bottom-only holes go blind when the jug sits flat on a patio.

Raise them off the ground. Two bricks, a pallet slat, anything. Full contact with hot concrete cooks the roots and blocks drainage.

Shade the sides. These jugs are thin translucent plastic, which is the worst case at your altitude — sun goes straight through and heats the root ball, and light on roots causes algae. Wrap the outside in cardboard, foil, cloth, or paint them light-colored. Group them so they shade each other.

They'll be top-heavy. A 1.5m tomato plant in a narrow jug tips in the first real wind. Either tie the stake to a wall or fence rather than relying on the pot, or set the jugs into a crate or against something solid.

One per jug. At that volume you're firmly in one-plant territory, and expect to water daily — twice on the hottest May days.

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:38 UTC

Those are a true 10L — the marked volume is the fill volume, so once you cut the top off you'll have slightly less than that in soil. That's below the floor for a cherry tomato.

Better use for them: your bell peppers. Peppers have smaller root systems and are genuinely productive in 10–12L. Same jug, same conversion, no compromise. And they're perfect as intermediate pot-up containers for the tomato seedlings before the tomatoes go into something bigger.

If you want to use them for tomatoes anyway, it's possible but you're accepting maybe 1–1.5 kg per plant and daily-or-twice-daily watering with no margin for a missed day. Two things make it survivable: mulch heavily, and set the jug inside a shallow tray with 2–3cm of water during May so there's a reserve (remove the tray in the rainy season or you'll rot the roots).

Two specific problems with this bottle shape:

The waist. That pinched middle means the root ball is narrower halfway up than at top and bottom. Not a growing problem, but at season's end you cannot pull the root ball out — you'll be cutting the bottle off. Fine, they're free.

The wall thickness. This PET is much thinner than a proper pot. Filled with wet soil it bulges and the sides flex when you move it. Set them inside a milk crate or box, or tie a couple together — that solves both the flexing and the tipping.

Where to find something bigger, cheaply, in Querétaro: 19L cubetas from a hardware store or paint shop run about 60–100 pesos, or free if you ask at a restaurant or bakery — they get cooking oil and frosting in exactly that size. Drill holes and you've got the right volume. That's the move for the tomatoes.

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:41 UTC

Yes, the technique is real — tomatoes root from buried stem, and that's why you plant them deep. But doing it progressively in a 10L jug doesn't buy you what you're hoping for.

The problem it doesn't solve. More roots in the same 10L of soil doesn't mean more water or more nutrients. It means the same 10L gets drained faster. Root mass is only an advantage when there's untapped soil to reach into. In a fixed container the water reserve is the water reserve, and you're already at the limit of it. The plant's ceiling is set by soil volume, not by root density.

What it costs. Every burial redirects the plant into making roots instead of growing up and setting flowers. Each round sets you back one to two weeks. Three burials and you've pushed first fruit a month later — which in your case means missing the pre-May window and running straight into the heat that makes flowers drop.

Practical problem. The waist on that bottle. Soil added above the pinch will bridge and leave voids at the narrow point, and water will channel down the sides instead of soaking through evenly.

Do this instead: let the seedling get tall and leggy in a small pot — they do it naturally under weak light, which is the one time legginess helps you. Then one transplant, filling the jug near full, with 20–25cm of stem buried in a single go. Same root benefit, no repeated stalls, soil settles as one uniform column.

The deep-burial idea is worth keeping. Do it once, at transplant, with a plant that's already tall.

ok i have the plants sprouting in an old egg carton, so the soil is shallow, at what height should i transplant?

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:45 UTC

Don't wait for height — get them out of the egg carton now, or as soon as the first true leaves appear.

Egg cartons hold maybe 2–3cm of soil. Roots hit the bottom within days of germinating and start circling or stalling, and cardboard cartons wick moisture out fast, so they swing wet-to-dry constantly. A root-stalled seedling never fully recovers its vigor.

Move them to the 10L jugs as the intermediate step — those are perfect at this stage, and later they become your pepper pots. Fill about a third, plant the seedling with the stem buried up to just below the cotyledons, and give it room.

Then grow it on until it's 20–30cm tall with a stem about as thick as a pencil — usually 4–6 weeks. That's when it goes into the final large container (the 19L cubeta), with 20–25cm of stem buried in one go.

Getting them out of the carton: water thoroughly an hour first so the soil holds together. Tear the individual cup away from the carton, then peel the cardboard off the root ball rather than pulling the plant out. Handle by a leaf, never the stem — a crushed stem kills the plant, a torn leaf doesn't.

Don't bury cardboard in the soil, incidentally. It doesn't break down as fast as people claim and it wicks water away from the roots.

CLAUDE2026-08-26 06:59 UTC

Two things differ from tomatoes, and they matter:

Don't bury the stem deep. Peppers do not root along their stem the way tomatoes do. Bury a pepper stem and you get rot, not roots. Plant at the same depth it was growing, or a centimeter deeper at most.

Wait longer. Pot up at about four true leaves, not the first set. Peppers are slower and resent being moved, so fewer moves is better. Work fast once you start — get roots out of the air and into soil quickly.
