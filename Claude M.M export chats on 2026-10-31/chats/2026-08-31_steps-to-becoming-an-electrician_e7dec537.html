steps to becoming an electrician

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:39 UTC

Apprentice → journeyman → master, roughly 4–6 years:

  1. High school diploma/GED (or Mexican equivalent), basic algebra.
  2. Get into an apprenticeship — union (IBEW/JATC), non-union contractor, or a trade school program that places you.
  3. Work 4–5 years, ~8,000 paid on-the-job hours plus ~600–900 classroom hours.
  4. Pass the journeyman exam (state or municipal), get licensed, work unsupervised.
  5. Another 2 years plus a master exam if you want to pull permits and run your own shop.

Ambiguity: this is the US/Canada path. Mexico has no equivalent licensing ladder — it's CONOCER competency certification (EC0086 etc.), CFE/NOM-001-SEDE knowledge, and DRO or Unidad Verificadora registration if you want to sign off on installations. Say which country and I'll give the real steps.

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:39 UTC

US path, licensing is state-level:

  1. Prerequisite — HS diploma/GED, 18+, algebra. Some states want a drug test and clean driving record.

  2. Get an apprenticeship — three routes:
    - IBEW/NECA JATC (union): apply, aptitude test, interview. Paid from day one, best benefits, waitlists can be long.
    - IEC or ABC (non-union merit shops): easier entry, apply directly to contractors.
    - Trade school first (6–12 months), then apprentice. Costs money and doesn't skip the hours — only use it if you can't get placed directly.

  3. Register as an apprentice with your state's licensing board before or right when you start working. Most states require it.

  4. Log the hours — typically 8,000 OJT hours over 4–5 years plus 576–900 classroom hours. Some states count a fraction of school hours toward OJT.

  5. Journeyman exam — apply to the state board with signed hour affidavits from your employer. Exam is open-book on the NEC plus theory and calculations. Pass it, you work unsupervised.

  6. Master electrician — 2 more years as journeyman (some states 4), second exam covering code, business law, and planning. Lets you pull permits, sign off on plans, run a shop.

  7. Continuing education — most states require CE hours every renewal cycle, heavier in NEC update years.

Which state you'd license in changes the hour requirements and whether the state licenses at all — a handful (NY, PA, IL, etc.) leave it to cities and counties. Tell me the state and I'll give you the actual board, hour counts, and fees.

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:40 UTC

That's the normal starting point — apprenticeships are built for people with zero experience. Nobody expects you to know anything on day one.

What no experience actually means for you:

  • JATC applications don't require experience. They require the aptitude test (algebra and reading comprehension), an interview, and a high school transcript. Study for the test — NJATC publishes a practice guide, and Khan Academy's algebra is enough.
  • Non-union contractors hire green helpers constantly. Walk in, call, or apply online. You start pulling wire, digging trenches, and carrying material. Pay is low — $16–22/hr in most markets — but the hours count.
  • A pre-apprenticeship or trade school is the fallback if applications aren't landing. It signals commitment and teaches enough to be useful on a jobsite. It does not shorten the 8,000 hours.

What helps you get picked with nothing on your resume: a reliable vehicle, showing up on time, basic hand tools, willingness to do the grunt work, and any construction-adjacent work history at all.

One thing worth flagging: you're in Querétaro. The 8,000 hours have to be worked in the US under a licensed electrician, so this means relocating before you start, not after. Where in the US would you be doing this?

what are the 8000 hours for?

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:42 UTC

To prove you can actually do the work before the state lets you do it unsupervised.

Electrical work kills people — the apprentice, the homeowner, the next crew. Classroom teaches the NEC and theory; the hours teach the part that can't be taught in a book: bending conduit that fits, terminating panels correctly, troubleshooting a circuit that isn't behaving, recognizing when something is energized that shouldn't be. Four years is the trade's estimate of how long it takes to see enough situations that you stop being dangerous.

Practically, the hours are also your résumé. Signed and logged, they're the evidence you submit to the state board to sit for the journeyman exam. No hours, no exam, no license.

They're spread across categories too — most states require minimums in specific areas (residential, commercial, industrial, service work, conduit, motor controls) so you can't spend four years only pulling romex in tract houses and call yourself a journeyman.

MILLARD2026-08-31 11:42 UTC

how long would ittake to complete 8k hours?

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:42 UTC

Four to five years at normal full-time.

The math: 8,000 ÷ 2,000 hours/year (40/week × 50 weeks) = 4 years exactly, if you're on the clock every week with no gaps.

Reality pushes it to 4.5–5 years:
- Winter slowdowns and between-job gaps in most markets
- Union apprenticeships are structured as 5-year programs regardless, with classroom terms
- Weather days on outside work

Faster: heavy overtime. 50-hour weeks put you at 8,000 in about 3.2 years, but most state boards and union programs cap what you can log per year or refuse to let you finish ahead of the program's term schedule. So overtime pays you more, it doesn't usually shorten the clock.

Slower: part-time or seasonal work stretches it indefinitely — the hours don't expire in most states, but a few require you to complete within a set window.

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:44 UTC

Then a full 4–5 year journeyman apprenticeship is the wrong tool. It's aimed at licensure — pulling permits, working on other people's buildings for pay. Most of what you'd learn (commercial conduit runs, motor controls, three-phase service, code compliance for occupancy) doesn't touch an off-grid homestead.

What you actually need is narrower:

Solar and battery — NABCEP PV Associate is a ~40-hour course plus an exam, no experience required. That's the entry credential. Real skills come from a solar-specific course or a season working for a residential PV installer. DC wiring, array sizing, charge controllers, battery bank configuration and safety (LiFePO4 vs lead-acid), grounding and bonding, disconnects. Grid-tie needs a licensed electrician and a utility interconnection agreement; fully off-grid usually doesn't, though it still needs a permit and inspection in most US counties.

Watering systems — not electrical. That's hydrology, pumps, and pressure math. Irrigation Association certifications, or just Brad Lancaster's rainwater harvesting books plus a pump sizing course. The electrical part is a well pump circuit and a controller, which is a weekend of learning, not four years.

Permaculture design — a PDC (72-hour Permaculture Design Certificate) covers water earthworks, swales, keyline, and site layout. Cheaper and faster than any of the above.

The honest ordering: a PDC and a solar course get you building in under a year. An apprenticeship gets you a trade and a paycheck, which is a fine reason to do it — just not a prerequisite for the farm.

Two things change this answer: whether the farm is in the US or Mexico, and whether you want this as a livelihood or only for your own land. Which?

That's a legitimate reason and it changes the calculus. Middle path — apprentice with a contractor who does residential solar and battery storage. You log your 8,000 hours toward the license while the daily work is exactly the skillset you want. Those shops exist in every sunbelt market and they're hiring.

do i need credential to work on my own land?

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:48 UTC

No.

In the US, most states have a homeowner exemption: you can do electrical work on a home you own and occupy without a license. You still pull a permit and pass inspection. The exemption usually doesn't cover rentals, structures you plan to sell soon, or commercial buildings on the property — and a few jurisdictions restrict it further.

In Mexico there's no license to have. Nobody stops you. Practical limits are CFE's rules if you ever want a grid interconnection, and your insurance company if you have any.

Either way: no credential needed for your own land. The credential is for working on other people's property for money — which is the fallback-income part of your plan, not the farm part.

in mexico do i really need credentials to work? i mean it seems like the electical work here is shawty at best

CLAUDE2026-08-31 11:49 UTC

No, you don't need credentials to do electrical work in Mexico. There's no journeyman license, no state board, no exam. Anyone can call themselves an electricista and take paid work.

Where it does bite:

Grid interconnection. If a solar array feeds back to CFE, the contract application needs a Unidad de Verificación (UVIE) certificate confirming the installation meets NOM-001-SEDE. That's a third-party inspector, not you, and they'll fail sloppy work. Same for a new service drop or increasing your contracted load.

Commercial and permitted construction. Anything requiring a municipal permit needs a DRO (Director Responsable de Obra) to sign, and larger jobs need a Corresponsable en Instalaciones. Not you — an engineer you hire.

Insurance and liability. Fire from an uncertified installation, and the insurer walks. Injure someone and unlicensed doesn't shield you.

Getting hired. CFE, industrial plants, and serious contractors want CONOCER certification (EC0086 for LV installations) or an ingeniero eléctrico degree. Casas particulares don't ask for anything.

Your read on the quality is correct and it's the actual opportunity: the bar for competent, code-aware residential and solar work in Querétaro is low, and doing it properly is a differentiator, not a barrier. No paper required to start — CONOCER certification is cheap and fast if you later want doors that ask.

Which brings back the question I keep asking: is the farm in Mexico or the US? Because if it's Mexico, spending four years in a US apprenticeship for a license that's void here is a bad trade.

CLAUDE2026-08-31 12:01 UTC

Here's the sequence, with what each thing actually is and why it sits where it does.

Stage 0 — Free, before you spend anything (week 1)

NFPA free access — nfpa.org, "Free Access" under Codes and Standards. Register a free account and open NFPA 70 (the NEC), 2026 edition. Read-only in a browser, no download or print. Use it to see what the code book actually looks like before you buy one.

Mike Holt free material — mikeholt.com. Three sections: "Free Stuff," the Code Forum, and Charts & Calculators. Watch a few free videos. His style is slow, heavily illustrated, repetitive by design. An hour tells you whether it works for you.

Purpose of this stage: decide whether self-study suits you before spending a dollar. Some people need a classroom.

Stage 1 — Electrical theory (months 1–4)

Theory before code. The NEC assumes you already understand voltage, current, resistance, and AC behavior. Skipping to code is the most common self-study mistake.

Buy: NEC 2026 (NFPA 70) — nfpa.org store, or Amazon. Get the softbound or spiral. Buy index tabs separately (Mike Holt sells them, so does anyone on Amazon). Tab it yourself — the act of tabbing teaches you the layout.

Buy: Mike Holt Electrician Foundation Video Program (2026 NEC) — mikeholt.com, under Training Solutions → Apprenticeship. This is his entry-level theory program: Ohm's law, series and parallel circuits, power, AC fundamentals, transformers, motors, basic wiring methods.

How to work it: one unit per week. Watch the video, do the practice questions, then go find the corresponding thing in the code book and read it even if you don't understand it yet. Repetition against the physical book is what builds the navigation instinct that the journeyman exam actually tests.

Shipping to Querétaro: check whether each program is available as streaming/online before ordering a boxed set. Physical media through Mexican customs gets slow and expensive.

Stage 2 — Code fluency (months 4–8)

Mike Holt: Understanding the NEC — the guided walk through the code book's structure. Articles 90 through 250 are the ones that matter most for what you're building.

Mike Holt: Grounding vs. Bonding — take this as a standalone. It's the single most misunderstood topic in the trade and the one that most often makes solar installations dangerous. Article 250. Do not skip it, do not rush it.

By the end of this stage you should be able to open the code book and find an answer in under a minute. That's the skill, not memorization.

Stage 3 — Solar PV (months 6–12, overlapping stage 2)

Correction to what I told you earlier: NABCEP PV Associate eligibility through SEI requires two courses, not one. The exam covers both grid-direct and battery-based systems.

Solar Energy International — solarenergy.org. The deepest training available and the strongest on off-grid. Their online courses (PVOL prefix) are self-paced with real instructors grading work and answering questions in forums.

Order:

  1. PVOL101 — Solar Electric Design and Installation (Grid-Direct). The foundation course, 40 hours. Prerequisite for everything else. Includes the Solar Electric Handbook textbook.
  2. PVOL203 — PV System Fundamentals (Battery-Based). This is your course. Load profiles, load estimates, battery bank design, overcurrent protection and disconnects for battery systems, voltage drop in DC. Requires PVOL101 first, or a placement quiz if you can demonstrate experience.
  3. Sit the NABCEP PV Associate exam — nabcep.org, Associate Program. Roughly $125–180 through a registered provider, plus a $75 international surcharge for testing outside the US and Canada.

Optional later, if you go deeper:
- PVOL202 — NEC-focused design for grid-direct systems. Useful code reinforcement.
- PVOL304 — Advanced PV Stand-alone System Design. Charge controller types, MPPT vs. non-MPPT array sizing, DC load control, DC-only systems. Requires PV101 and PV203 first. This is the genuinely off-grid design course.

Cheaper alternative if SEI is out of budget: the university continuing-ed bundles (careertraining.ccny.cuny.edu, careertraining.cmich.edu, careertraining.unm.edu, careertraining.gulfcoast.edu — all the same underlying program) include the NABCEP exam fee. Weaker on off-grid, fine for the credential.

In-person option: NC State Clean Energy Technology Center, nccleantech.ncsu.edu — five-day Fundamentals of Solar PV Design and Installation, ends with a live residential install, exam fee included. Worth it if you want hands-on early.

One note on the 2026 code cycle: Article 690 (solar PV) got a list of changes, and energy management systems moved from Article 750 to Article 130. Make sure whatever course you buy is on the 2026 NEC, not 2023 — SEI's older material was written against 2020.

Stage 4 — Water (months 9–18, parallel)

Not electrical, and cheap.

Books: Brad Lancaster, Rainwater Harvesting for Drylands and Beyond, volumes 1 and 2 — harvestingrainwater.com. Written for dryland climates, which is exactly Querétaro.

Irrigation Association — irrigation.org. Training and certification in design and auditing. Their Certified Irrigation Designer track is more than you need, but their entry courses on hydraulics and design are the right level.

What you need to actually understand: static head, dynamic head, friction loss in pipe, and duty point. Get these before you buy a pump — pump mismatching is the most common and most expensive homestead mistake.

Stage 5 — Site design (do this early, months 3–9)

Permaculture Design Certificate, 72 hours. This determines where everything goes before you wire any of it, so earlier is better than later.

  • Geoff Lawton's online PDC — discoverpermaculture.com
  • Oregon State University online PDC — through OSU's professional and continuing education
  • Local Mexican PDCs — worth more than either, if you can find one in a dryland climate. Ask around Querétaro and Guanajuato; there are active networks.

Avoid temperate-climate PDCs. Water strategy for Oregon will actively mislead you in the Bajío.

Stage 6 — Credential (year 1–2, after you decide where you live)

Mexico:
- CONOCER EC0586.01 — PV installation, residential/commercial/industrial. conocer.gob.mx for the standard document; fide.org.mx for training, evaluation and certification. CONOCER suggests a year of electrical work plus 40 hours of PV training before attempting it — which your SEI courses cover.
- EC0086 — low-voltage electrical installations, the general electrical standard.
- EC1181 — supervision and technical documentation, if you want to sign off on projects.

United States:
- IBEW/JATC — electricalapprenticeship.org, find your local. Aptitude test, interview, transcript.
- IEC — ieci.org. ABC — abc.org. Non-union, apply to member contractors directly.
- State licensing boards — mikeholt.com keeps a directory. Register as an apprentice when you start.
- Mike Holt Journeyman Exam Prep Library — theory, NEC, calculations. Buy this in year 3–4, not now.

The parallel track that matters most

Starting month 2, build things. A small 12V system on the bench. Then a real array. Then batteries. Wire your own place. Help friends. Take apart what fails and find out why.

Every hour of coursework above is worth roughly nothing until it's been tested against something you built with your hands. Mexico lets you do that without asking permission — that's the real advantage of being where you are.
