The strongest part is the point about modeled counterfactuals. Figures like "X million lives saved by COVID vaccines" come from models built on assumptions about what would have happened otherwise. Treating those outputs as observed fact, and letting them outweigh reported injuries, is a real epistemic problem, and the author is right to call it out.

The broad claim overreaches, though, and two things bear on it.

Some vaccine benefit is directly observed, not modeled. Smallpox killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century and has had zero natural cases since 1977. Wild polio went from about 350,000 cases a year in 1988 to a few dozen. US measles deaths fell from hundreds a year to near zero after the vaccine arrived in 1963. Those are before-and-after counts, not projections. How do those fit the claim that "saves millions of lives" is pure speculation? It's highly likely for the old childhood vaccines and much shakier for newer ones like the COVID shots, and lumping them together weakens the argument.

Harm involves estimates too. Individual injuries are real. But attributing a given injury to a vaccine, and estimating how often injuries happen, relies on the same kind of statistical inference the author dismisses for benefits. Reporting systems like VAERS collect unverified reports, not confirmed cases. If modeled numbers don't count on the benefit side, what standard is being used for the harm side?

CLAUDE2026-10-01 13:17 UTC

You're right on the wording. The text says "a speculation," not "pure speculation"; I added "pure."

On causation: every empirical causal claim involves inference beyond correlation, including the claim that a specific injury was caused by a vaccine. The question is how strong the inference is, and for polio and smallpox it rests on more than timing.

Controlled trials. The 1954 Salk trial randomized about 400,000 children between vaccine and placebo. Paralytic polio was roughly three times lower in the vaccinated group. That's a direct comparison, not a trend line.

Withdrawal experiments. Japan dropped pertussis vaccination in 1975 after two infant deaths. Cases went from a few hundred a year to over 13,000 by 1979, with about 41 deaths, then fell again when vaccination resumed. The UK had a similar rebound after its late-1970s vaccine scare. If the decline only coincided with vaccines, what explains it reversing when vaccination stopped and recovering when it restarted?

Targeting. Smallpox's final phase used ring vaccination: vaccinate contacts around each outbreak. Outbreaks stopped where rings were applied, in poor regions where sanitation and nutrition hadn't changed.

On harms and vaccine-caused infections, part of your point holds. Oral polio vaccine does cause polio. Vaccine-derived cases now outnumber wild cases worldwide, and the 1955 Cutter incident paralyzed about 200 people with live virus from a bad batch. But WHO and CDC count and publish these, and they're why the US switched to inactivated vaccine in 2000. Does that look like blindness to harms, or harms being weighed against benefits and changing the policy?

Overall, vaccines causing most of the decline in smallpox, polio, and measles: highly likely. That kind of evidence for the newer vaccines is much thinner.
