Areas of study, ranked by what actually gets you hired:

  1. Risk management and position sizing — this is what separates people who get hired from people who blow accounts. Kelly criterion, drawdown control, correlation risk across a book, not just a single pair.

  2. Macro and rates — forex and gold move on interest rate differentials, central bank policy, and inflation expectations. You need to understand carry trades, yield curves, and how DXY, real yields, and gold correlate (usually inverse, but that breaks during crisis periods — know why).

  3. Market microstructure — order flow, liquidity, how ECNs and interbank markets actually work, slippage, spread behavior around news. Firms care whether you understand execution, not just direction-calling.

  4. Statistics and quant methods — regression, time series (stationarity, cointegration), Monte Carlo for testing strategy robustness, and enough probability to not fool yourself with backtest overfitting.

  5. Programming — Python is the industry standard for research; C++ if you want to go toward HFT/execution roles. MQL5-only skills don't transfer to institutional desks — Python and increasingly C++/Rust do.

  6. Options and derivatives basics — even pure spot FX/gold traders at firms are expected to understand vol, skew, and how options flow affects spot.

Books, roughly in order:

  • Trading in the Zone — Mark Douglas (psychology, read this first, not last)
  • Market Wizards series — Jack Schwager (pattern-match across many successful traders instead of one style)
  • A Complete Guide to the Futures Market — Jack Schwager (technicals + fundamentals, most of it applies to FX/gold)
  • Currency Trading and Intermarket Analysis — Ashraf Laidi (gold/dollar/rates relationships specifically)
  • Advances in Financial Machine Learning — Marcos López de Prado (if you're going quant route, this is close to required reading at prop/quant shops)
  • The Predictors — Thomas Bass (good narrative on how a quant shop actually got built, useful for understanding the culture)
  • Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives — Hull (reference text, not cover to cover, but firms assume you've been exposed to it)

Where to put your energy, practically:

  • Build a verifiable track record. Firms don't hire on theory. A funded/prop-firm evaluation pass (FTMO, The5ers, etc.) or a documented live/demo track record with real risk stats (Sharpe, max drawdown, win rate, R-multiple) is worth more than any certificate.
  • Learn to write a research note, not just a signal. Institutional trading roles want you to explain why a trade works, with data, not just that your backtest was profitable.
  • CFA or CMT if you want a credential that opens doors at traditional firms — CMT (Chartered Market Technician) is more directly relevant to trading than CFA, which skews portfolio management.
  • Network through prop trading communities and quant finance forums (QuantConnect, Elite Trader, local trading meetups) — most trading jobs aren't posted publicly, they come through referral.
  • Target prop trading firms first, not banks. Banks want elite pedigree (top math/CS programs, PhDs for quant roles). Prop firms (Jane Street, DRW, Jump, XTX, and smaller regional prop shops) hire more on demonstrated edge and will take someone with a strong track record and quant chops over a pedigree-only candidate.

The single biggest lever: a documented, statistically sound track record beats every book and certificate on this list combined. Everything above should be in service of building that.

For working toward a trading firm, here are the real course options, split by what they actually get you:

University-level / structured programs
- Financial Markets (Yale, via Coursera, Robert Shiller) — free, broad, good foundation but not technical enough alone.
- Quantitative Finance — MIT OpenCourseWare has full course materials (18.S096 Topics in Mathematics with Applications in Finance) free, no certificate but real MIT problem sets.
- CQF (Certificate in Quantitative Finance) — paid, ~$20k+, respected in industry, heavy math/programming, most directly aimed at quant trading roles. This is the closest thing to an industry-recognized "quant trader" credential short of a master's degree.
- Master's in Financial Engineering / Quant Finance (e.g., Baruch MFE, CMU MSCF, NYU) — the actual pedigree path banks and top prop firms recruit from, but expensive and time-intensive; only worth it if you want the traditional pedigree route.

Practical / trading-specific
- CMT Association curriculum (Level I–III) — this is coursework, not just an exam; it walks through technical analysis, market indicators, and behavioral finance systematically. Ties directly into the credential already in the PDF.
- QuantConnect Bootcamp — free, hands-on, teaches algorithmic strategy building in their platform using Python; good for building the track record from Section 7.1.
- Udemy/Coursera algorithmic trading courses — low cost, uneven quality, mainly useful for filling specific gaps (e.g., a pandas course, a specific stats course) rather than as a primary path.

Standalone subject courses worth taking individually
- Time series econometrics (many universities post free lecture series on YouTube — search for a graduate econometrics course with an ADF/cointegration unit)
- A proper linear algebra + probability course if your math is rusty (Khan Academy or MIT 18.06/18.05 OCW) — this underlies almost everything in Section 4 of the PDF.
- Python for data science (any of DataCamp, Coursera's "Python for Everybody," or just working through McKinney's book from the PDF directly)
