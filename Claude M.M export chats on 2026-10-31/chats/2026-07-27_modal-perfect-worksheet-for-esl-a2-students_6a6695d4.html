CLAUDE2026-07-27 21:45 UTC

Confirmed — my worksheet wasn't wrong. Both are standard grammar.

"Can't have" and "couldn't have" are both correct for deduction about the past (negative certainty) — they're interchangeable in that use: "It's possible to use either couldn't have or can't have to speculate about the past."

The one place they're not interchangeable is when the meaning is past ability rather than deduction — there, only "couldn't have" works: "To describe ability in the past couldn't have is the only possibility" (e.g. "I couldn't have finished it without your help" — this is about ability, not a guess, so "can't have" is wrong there).

Check the sentence I used: "That's impossible! He can't have finished already." — that's a deduction, so "can't have" is correct. "Couldn't have" would also be correct in that exact sentence.

One honest note: some teachers/resources lean toward "couldn't have" as more common in everyday speech, with "can't have" taught more in British grammar books, treating can't have as a form that "exists and is taught in some grammar books, but it's probably a lot less common than couldn't have." Nothing in the PDF was factually wrong — I'll leave "can't have" as is unless you want me to switch to "couldn't have" for consistency with what you teach.
