The Freud part is accurate. He proposed the seduction theory in 1896 — hysteria caused by real childhood sexual abuse, based directly on what his patients told him. He dropped it a year later and replaced it with the idea that those reports were fantasies driven by infantile sexual desire (the Oedipal theory). Jung doesn't fit the same charge. His break with Freud was over libido theory and the nature of the unconscious, not a parallel campaign to reclassify women's abuse memories as fantasy. Lumping him in with Freud on this specific point isn't supported by the record.

"The disciplines are bullshit" doesn't follow from the accurate half either. Freud built an error into the foundation of psychoanalysis, and that error did real damage — but a founder's specific bad call isn't the same as everything downstream of it being worthless. Plenty of clinical practice since has corrected for exactly this problem.

CLAUDE2026-08-07 17:01 UTC

There's a specific theoretical answer here.

Jung's early theory (complex theory, from his word-association work) did minimize real exogenous trauma as a cause of psychic disturbance, favoring "endogenous trauma" — conflict arising from fantasy and drive, not actual events. He held that all unconscious material was psychically real, meaning fantasy carried the same weight as event — a stance that structurally lets a real memory and an invented one collapse into the same category, functionally.

Where he diverges from Freud theoretically: Freud built a specific mechanism — the Oedipal replacement of the seduction theory — that required actively reclassifying reported abuse as wish-fulfillment fantasy. Jung's theory doesn't have that mechanism. His move was different: trauma gets absorbed into "complex formation," sourced from archetypal/collective material rather than personal history, so the question "did this happen" becomes less central to his framework than "how did this get symbolized." That's not the same operation as Freud's, but it produces a comparable effect — the literal, external reality of what a patient reported becomes secondary to its symbolic/psychic content.

So: theoretically, Jung didn't run Freud's specific reversal, but his framework had its own way of de-prioritizing external reality — through the primacy of the collective unconscious and archetypal material over lived event.
