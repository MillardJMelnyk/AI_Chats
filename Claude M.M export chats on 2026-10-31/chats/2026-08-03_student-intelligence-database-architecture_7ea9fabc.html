  5. Context/template library — roughly 200 templates × 6 CEFR levels. Zero server cost, and by far the largest amount of actual work in the document. Authoring and QA is the real bottleneck, not infrastructure.

Three boxes:

1. Querétaro — real-time. LiveKit SFU + whiteboard sync server. 8 vCPU / 16GB. This is the box that scales worst and costs most.

2. Querétaro — app + Postgres. 4 vCPU / 8GB, NVMe, real backups. Same region as box 1 so the classroom engine isn't reaching across an ocean for state.

3. Hetzner EU — NLP workers. spaCy + self-hosted LanguageTool. CX33 or CAX with 8–16GB. Latency irrelevant, buy the cheapest RAM.

Three things that will bite you on box 1:

  • Bandwidth, not CPU, is the constraint. An SFU forwards streams; a 6-student group room at 720p is ~10 Mbps out per participant. Get is*hosting's egress cap and overage price in writing before you sign.
  • You need a TURN server. Students behind corporate or mobile NAT can't do peer-to-peer; their media relays through you, on the same bandwidth budget.
  • Whiteboard state is not Postgres data. Keep CRDT docs in their own store with periodic snapshots. Writing every stroke transactionally to the main DB will take box 2 down.

One flag and I'll drop it: group + breakouts at launch is the most expensive and slowest possible starting configuration, and it delays proof that the core loop works. You asked, that's the fleet.

Teacher-authored answer keys are the right call at this stage, but exact string match will fail badly with language students. Build in from day one, it's an afternoon of work:

  • Normalize before comparing: trim, collapse whitespace, lowercase, strip trailing punctuation, normalize curly quotes and accents.
  • Let teachers enter multiple accepted answers per item, not one. "I have got" / "I've got" / "I have" are all correct and a single key marks two of them wrong.
  • Support answer types beyond free text: multiple choice, matching, gap-fill with a word bank. Those have no ambiguity and cover most of what A1–B1 teachers actually assess.
  • Flag near-misses for teacher review instead of auto-failing. One-character-off gets queued, not marked wrong.

Store every response verbatim regardless of scoring. When you can afford the NLP tier, that corpus is what you replay through it — and it's what tells you which items the keys are getting wrong.

MILLARD2026-08-03 15:20 UTC

right i could do like a string comparison with regex and if the difference is huge evaulatae from there

CLAUDE2026-08-03 15:20 UTC

Regex is the wrong tool for the "how different" question — it answers yes/no on a pattern, not how far apart two strings are. Use Levenshtein distance for that. Ratio of edit distance to answer length gives you a clean three-way split: near-zero is correct, small is teacher review, large is wrong.

Where regex does earn its place is in the teacher's answer key, not the comparison — let them write I(f| ha)ve got style optional groups if they want. Most won't. Multiple accepted answers gets you 90% of it without asking teachers to learn syntax.

One caution: Levenshtein doesn't know meaning. "I do a decision" is one edit from "I make a decision" and completely wrong; "we didn't have any" versus "we had none" is far apart and correct. Set the auto-fail threshold loose and let the review queue absorb the middle — teacher time is cheap at your volume, wrong grades are not.

MILLARD2026-08-03 15:22 UTC

ok, honestly there will be a prompt in which ai will be returning a json for these answer groups

CLAUDE2026-08-03 15:22 UTC

That's the better version — the AI generates the accepted-answer variants at authoring time, the teacher approves or edits them, and grading itself stays offline string comparison. No LLM call when a student submits, so cost stays fixed per test rather than per attempt.

Three things to nail down:

  • Generate once, store the result. The variant list lives in the test object in Postgres. Never regenerate at grade time — same test must grade identically today and in six months.
  • Teacher approves before the test goes live. The AI will produce variants that are wrong for the level: accepting a B2 phrasing on an A1 gap-fill teaches the wrong thing. Show the list, let them delete.
  • Validate the JSON on the way in. Schema check, reject and retry on malformed output. A test that fails to parse at 8am with a class waiting is the failure mode you care about.

Ask the model for level-appropriate variants explicitly — pass the CEFR level and grammar focus into the prompt. Without it you'll get every synonym in English.

CLAUDE2026-08-03 15:35 UTC

Virginia is usable. The latency case against it is weaker than I implied earlier — here are the actual numbers.

Querétaro → Ashburn is roughly 50–60ms RTT. Through an SFU that means student-to-student mouth-to-ear picks up ~50–60ms extra each way. Total conversational delay lands around 200ms, which is noticeable on interruption and turn-taking but sits inside the range people run classes on every day. Whiteboard sync is even less of an issue: CRDT renders your own strokes locally and instantly, so you only see the delay on other people's strokes.

So the $34/mo delta buys a real improvement, not a necessary one.

The reason I'd still steer you off CloudingHost isn't milliseconds:

  • No track record for sustained WebRTC egress, and "unlimited" on a $8.95 plan almost always has a fair-use clause that video trips first.
  • Port speed unspecified per plan.
  • Core-tier CPU isn't labelled dedicated in the specs even though the marketing copy claims it — you'd be on oversubscribed cores during class hours.
  • Mexican ISP routing to US-East isn't always direct; Telmex in particular can hairpin through Dallas or Miami, so your real latency may be worse than the 50–60ms figure and will vary by student.

If you want US-East cheap, use a provider whose bandwidth terms and AUP are actually documented — Hetzner's Ashburn location or DigitalOcean NYC. You'd pay more than $8.95 and less than $42, with terms you can hold them to.
