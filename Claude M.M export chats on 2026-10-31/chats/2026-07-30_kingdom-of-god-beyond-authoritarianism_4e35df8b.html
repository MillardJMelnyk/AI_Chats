You want the basileia material examined as evidence, not as doctrine. How I propose to go about it, in order:

  1. The word. Basileia, and behind it Aramaic malkuta / Hebrew malkut. These carry reign-as-activity at least as strongly as realm-as-territory. Step 2 — What the texts actually say about it

I sorted every kingdom saying by what it predicates. The split is real, but it doesn't fall where I predicted.

Things it's compared to: a seed nobody watches grow (Mk 4:26–29), a mustard seed, leaven worked into dough, buried treasure stumbled on, a pearl worth everything, soil receiving seed. No commands in any of them. Nothing is enforced. The farmer in Mark 4 explicitly doesn't know how it works — it just does.

Things it's said to be or do: near, arrived, upon you, in your midst and not observable (Lk 17:20–21), not food and drink but rightness, peace and gladness (Rom 14:17), not talk but power (1 Cor 4:20).

Who it belongs to: the poor, the persecuted, children. Not the obedient, not the qualified.

How you get in: become like a child, be born from above, sell everything, be poor. Hard for the rich. Never once "obey and you're in."

Rank inside it: inverted, explicitly and repeatedly. Greatest = child, greatest = servant (Mt 18:1–4, 20:25–28).

And then the other pile. A king settling accounts and handing a debtor to torturers (Mt 18:23–35). A guest bound and thrown out over clothing (Mt 22:11–14). A servant cast into outer darkness for burying money (Mt 25:14–30). A net sorting fish, a fire for the weeds, a door shut with "I don't know you." Keys handed to Peter with binding and loosing power (Mt 16:19). In Revelation: throne, crowns, an iron rod.

So my prediction was half right and I want to be precise about which half. The organic material has no juridical structure — that held. But the juridical material is not marginal, and it is not late. Mark, the earliest, already sets the kingdom against gehenna (9:47). You cannot date the sanctions away. They're in the oldest layer we can reach.

One thing I'll note and then not lean on: several organic parables have judgment endings that many scholars read as Matthew's own additions to the parable he received — the weeds and the net especially. That may be true. It's contested, it conveniently favors the answer you want, and I'm not building on it.

Step 3 — Your test: authority of or authority over

This is where it breaks open, and your distinction did the work.

The material sorts almost perfectly by whether it has a scene.

Everything that describes imposed authority comes with a stage set: a throne room, an audit, a doorway, a courtroom, a master returning. Someone occupies a position, and the position is outside and above the thing being ruled. Command, sanction, no appeal.

Everything that describes intrinsic authority has no scene at all. It's a description of how something behaves. Seed grows because that's what seed does — there's no enforcement because there's nothing outside the process to enforce against. Leaven doesn't rule dough. Treasure doesn't command the man who finds it; it draws him, and he goes willingly and gladly. Authority of existence, not over it — exactly your line.

Three passages then go further than I expected, and they're the strongest things in the file:

Luke 22:25–30. Jesus names Gentile kings lording it over people and says flatly: not so among you. Then, in the same breath, confers a kingdom on them. He puts the anti-model and the word side by side deliberately. Whatever he means by the word, he has just excluded domination from it by name.

John 18:36. Asked if he's a king, he says his kingdom isn't from this world, and gives the test: if it were, his people would be fighting. He defines it by the absence of coercive force. That's a structural definition, not a poetic one.

1 Corinthians 15:24–28. The end of the whole arrangement is that Christ hands the kingdom over, all rule and authority and power are abolished, and God is all in all. The apparatus of rule terminates. It is not eternal. It's a temporary measure that ends in something with no positions in it.

Run those against your criterion: a monarchy whose sovereign serves rather than is served, whose ranks are inverted so the least is greatest, whose force is renounced as the thing that would prove it false, and whose stated endpoint is the abolition of all rule — has had every defining property of a monarchy removed. What's left is the word.

Step 4 — The sources diverge, and the divergence is the story

Mark: kingdom is sudden, near, mostly organic, with a hard edge on judgment.
Matthew: uses it most, and monarchizes it most. Kings, accountings, expulsions, keys, a church with binding power. Matthew is where the throne room gets built.
Luke: keeps the organic material, adds the sharpest anti-domination statement in the corpus.
John: nearly drops the phrase. Twice, total. Replaces it with life, abiding, indwelling, vine and branches, and "I no longer call you servants but friends" (15:15). Where John needed the concept, he refused the vocabulary.
Paul: uses it rarely, mostly in exclusion lists, and writes its abolition.
Revelation: thrones and iron rods throughout — but also says the redeemed are the kingdom (1:6, 5:10). The kingdom is the people, not a structure above them.

The term was already being pulled two directions while the New Testament was still being written. It is not a single witness saying one thing.

Step 5 — The ancient overlay

Your point 1 was half right, and the important half is later than you placed it.

The writers' own political air matters less than what came after. The decisive capture is fourth-century: Eusebius mapping one emperor onto one God as matching halves of a single order; Christ enthroned in imperial iconography borrowed directly from the emperor's own portraiture; Dominus and Kyrios carrying the ruler's title. Latin didn't help — regnum is more territorially concrete than basileia, and the West read the Vulgate for a thousand years. Then feudal Christendom, where lordship was the only available shape for any relationship at all.

And the sharpest re-monarchization is modern, not ancient: the feast of Christ the King was created in 1925, explicitly to reassert sovereign kingship against secular states. That's not inherited residue. That's a deliberate act inside living memory.

Step 6 — The modern overlay

Shortest, as expected, and your point 2 stands. Current institutional usage runs on lordship, submission, authority, covering, headship, obedience — a vocabulary of chain-of-command that the sayings material barely supplies. "King Jesus" as the dominant worship idiom is doing something the parables of seed and leaven never did.

Step 7 — Residue

There is residue, and it is not a monarchy.

What survives your test is a description of how reality actually behaves when it isn't being fought: growth from something small and unwatched, hiddenness, working from inside a thing rather than upon it, attraction rather than command, and belonging that runs to the poor and the young — the ones with the least standing under any actual sovereign. Authority intrinsic to existence and action, which needs no enforcement because there's no outside from which to enforce.

But I won't tell you this is what the New Testament says, because the New Testament also says the other thing, early and unambiguously, and I'd be doing to the text what the tradition did if I edited that out. Two incompatible things sit in the same collection. The one you can't reconcile is genuinely there. It is not all overlay.

So the honest verdict on your question: no legitimate remainder in the monarchy image itself. What's worth keeping got named with royal vocabulary — probably because in that world it was the only word available for ultimacy — and the vocabulary then swallowed the content. John saw the problem and switched words. Paul mostly stopped using it. Jesus, if Luke 22 is close to him, used it while explicitly excluding what it means. That's either subversive genius or a fatal choice of word. Given the next seventeen centuries, the word won.

Your over/of distinction is the key, and it held under load. It's the only test I ran that sorted the material cleanly, and it did it by a criterion the texts themselves don't state — which is usually the sign of a real one.

MILLARD2026-07-30 17:20 UTC

So clarify the meaning and usage of the Greek word, briefly.

CLAUDE2026-07-30 17:20 UTC

Basileia is an abstract noun built off basileus, king. It names the kingship itself — the exercise of it — not the territory. Greek had other words for territory.

Range of use: the office or rank of king; the exercise of ruling; the period of a reign; and, secondarily, the domain. Ordinary Greek used all four. Which one is meant is context, not the word.

Behind it, Aramaic malkuta. In the Aramaic targums the phrase already exists — God's malkuta is revealed, appears, comes near. Kingship as an event that shows up, not a place with borders.

Two things about its use in the gospels that ordinary Greek doesn't predict:

It takes verbs of arrival and proximity — comes, has drawn near, is upon you. You don't say that about an office or a reign. You say it about an event.

And you can enter it. That's spatial and it fights the abstract sense. Though "enter" in Semitic idiom often means entering a state — entering life, entering rest, entering joy.

So: the word means kingship-in-action, and the gospels use it as a name for something arriving that you can be inside of. Neither the office sense nor the territory sense covers that. The vocabulary was being stretched to hold something it wasn't built for — which is roughly what we found by every other route.
