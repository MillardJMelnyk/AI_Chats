CLAUDE2026-09-01 23:09 UTC

I'll translate it in full and then flag the problems.

Full translation is in the file below. I'm not a lawyer, and Querétaro's civil code governs some of this — the items marked check locally are worth a consulta with a Mexican abogado before signing.

What this document is

A standard landlord-side Mexican lease built around a póliza jurídica — a legal service policy that funds fast eviction. SBI Comercializadora is the policy provider, named in Clause 6. Nothing here is a scam. It is heavily one-sided in the landlord's favor, and several clauses are aggressive even by Mexican standards.

Real problems

Clause 18 + 16 — the two-month penalty trap. This is the worst clause. A two-month penalty applies not just to early termination, but to any grounds for rescission in Clause 16 — and 16(c) is "breaches any of the clauses." One late water bill, one noise complaint from a neighbor, and the landlord can rescind and bill two months' rent. Negotiate this: the penalty should attach only to voluntary early termination, and ideally reduce to one month or prorate after month six.

Clause 18 cites the wrong clause. It references the Sixteenth Clause for early-termination notice; early termination is the Seventeenth. Sloppy drafting, but it creates ambiguity in the clause that costs the most money. Get it corrected before signing.

Clause 10 — 48 hours to report a hidden defect. Absurdly short, and it's paired with Declaration II(c), where your son pre-certifies everything works and waives any right to condition rent on repairs. Together these mean: if the water heater dies in month three and he doesn't send a written notice within 48 hours, the repair is arguably his. Push for five business days minimum, and add an express landlord obligation to make necessary/structural repairs at landlord cost within a fixed window.

Clause 8 — the utility cascade. Unpaid utilities mean rent is legally "not paid." So a disputed CFE or CEA bill turns into unpaid rent → late penalties → póliza jurídica activation → eviction. That chain runs from a bill he might not even have received.

Clause 6 — eviction machinery starts on day one. The penalty schedule tolerates payment through the 11th and beyond, but the same clause says SBI is notified "from the first day of default." Internally contradictory, and the version that gets used will be whichever suits the landlord. Ask for a written cure period — 10 days' notice before the póliza is triggered.

Clause 15 — he pays for the weapon aimed at him. The póliza jurídica exists to evict him, he funds it, and the landlord can renew it without his authorization and charge him whatever it costs. No cap. Get the cost quoted in writing now and a cap written into the clause.

Clause 12(VI) — eviction on suspicion. Grounds for rescission if the property is merely investigated or linked by any authority, for crimes committed inside or outside the property, by the tenant or anyone he let in. No conviction required. Combined with Clause 18, that's suspicion → eviction → two months' penalty. Standard post-2009 boilerplate in Mexico, but this version is broader than most.

Clause 16(g) — "disturbances or inappropriate attitudes with the neighbors." Undefined, subjective, and carries the two-month penalty. A foreigner in a condominio is exposed here.

Clause 16(i) — death of the tenant. Under Mexican civil law a lease generally survives the tenant and passes to heirs. Check locally, but this is likely unenforceable.

Clause 16 — "without the need for any judicial intervention." This does not let the landlord change the locks. Eviction in Mexico requires a court. If anyone tells him otherwise, that's the moment to call a lawyer.

Ordinary, don't fight it

  • Deposit of one month, not usable as last month's rent, held 30 days after move-out, returned without interest — normal in Mexico.
  • Joint obligor requirement. He needs a co-signer, usually someone who owns property in Querétaro. That person is liable for everything, including the two-month penalty.
  • INPC-indexed renewal, 30-day notice, no subletting, Querétaro jurisdiction — all standard.
  • Maintenance included in rent (Clause 5) — that's in his favor; keep it in writing.

Before he signs

  1. No blanks. Rent, deposit, address, dates, bank details all filled in on the copy he signs. Never sign a document with empty lines.
  2. Photo inventory is his best protection. Clause 9 requires one. Insist it happens, get a signed copy, and attach a written list of every existing defect as an annex. Clause 20 voids all verbal promises — if the landlord says "we'll fix the shower," it goes in the annex or it doesn't exist.
  3. CURP and RFC. If your son isn't a resident he won't have these. Sort out how that blank gets filled before signing day.
  4. Ask the póliza cost upfront. Typically a percentage of annual rent or roughly one month. It's a real line item.
  5. Nothing in here covers pets, parking, or guests. If any of those matter, add them.

CLAUDE2026-09-01 23:09 UTC

Two things change now that it's a house rather than an apartment, plus Querétaro state law adds a couple of points.

What "house" changes

Clause 16(h) — condominium regulations. A standalone house shouldn't be subject to a reglamento interno del condominio. If it's in a gated fraccionamiento, that reglamento exists and he's bound by rules he hasn't read. Ask for a copy before signing. If it's a free-standing house on a public street, strike 16(h) — otherwise he's agreeing to comply with a document that doesn't exist, which is a rescission ground someone could invent later.

Clause 5 — "rent includes maintenance." For a house this is ambiguous. It probably means the HOA cuota in a fraccionamiento. Get it stated explicitly: whether there's a cuota de mantenimiento, how much, and confirmation the landlord pays it. If the HOA bills the resident directly and goes unpaid, Clause 8's cascade could pull it in as "rent not paid."

Gas. Clause 8 charges him for gas. A house usually means a tanque estacionario — check whether it's full at handover and note the level in the photo inventory, or he'll be topping up someone else's gas.

Garden, pool, roof, boiler. Clause 9 makes him responsible for the property's condition and Clause 10 gives him 48 hours on hidden defects. A house has far more that can fail than an apartment — cistern, pump, water heater, roof waterproofing (impermeabilizante, which needs redoing every 2-3 years in Querétaro). None of these are assigned. Get a written split: landlord for structure, roof, plumbing, electrical, water pump; tenant for consumables and normal wear.

Querétaro state law

He has a statutory right to a one-year extension. Article 2378 of the Querétaro Civil Code gives a tenant who is current on rent the right to have the contract extended for one year on expiration, with the landlord permitted to raise the rent by the annual legal interest rate. Clause 3 of this contract says renewal is at the landlord's discretion via a new contract at INPC. That's weaker than what the code gives him. Don't rely on Clause 3's framing — the statutory right exists regardless. Check with a local abogado, because asserting it requires a specific procedural step in response to an eviction demand, not just a letter.

Repair obligation is the landlord's by default. A Querétaro firm cites the framework as: the landlord is obliged, even without express agreement, to keep the property in the same condition throughout the lease by making necessary repairs; the tenant must notify the landlord of the need for repairs as soon as possible; and if the landlord fails to make necessary repairs, the tenant may elect to rescind. Those article numbers are from the Federal Civil Code — the Querétaro state code has its own numbering with equivalent provisions. The practical point: Clause 10's 48-hour deadline and Declaration II(c)'s advance waiver are the landlord trying to contract out of a default duty. That's exactly why the pre-signing defect annex matters — it's his evidence.

One year is the legal minimum for residential, so the 12-month term is normal, not the landlord being generous.

Priority before signing

  1. Get the fraccionamiento reglamento and the maintenance cuota answer in writing.
  2. Written repair-responsibility split as an annex.
  3. Photo inventory plus a defect list, both signed, both parties keeping copies — this is the single highest-value thing he does.
  4. Fix Clause 18's wrong cross-reference and push the two-month penalty to voluntary termination only.
  5. Two hours with a Querétaro abogado before signature is cheap next to a two-month penalty.

Are there concerns not mentioned that my son should write into the contract, either requirements for the landlord or to protect himself?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 23:15 UTC

Yes — several. Grouped by what they protect.

Money and the deposit

Deposit return mechanics. Clause 4 says returned after 30 days with deductions, but there's no requirement the landlord itemize them. Add: any deduction must be supported by written quotes or receipts delivered to the tenant within the 30 days, and undeducted balance returns by bank transfer to a named account. Without this, "we kept it for repairs" is unanswerable.

Walk-through at move-out. Nothing requires a joint inspection when he leaves. Add: a joint inspection with photographs on the delivery date, signed by both, and the deposit deduction limited to items documented in that inspection.

Receipts. Clause 7 makes it his job to chase receipts. Add: landlord must issue a payment receipt (or CFDI if he needs one for tax purposes) within 5 days of each payment. If your son ever needs to prove he paid, transfer records alone can be argued around.

Landlord's obligations if he doesn't deliver. No clause covers the landlord failing to hand over on the start date. Add: if possession isn't delivered on the agreed date, rent is prorated and the deposit is refundable in full after X days' delay.

Habitability and repairs

Response deadline for the landlord. Clause 10 binds the tenant to 48 hours but binds the landlord to nothing but "will repair." Add a mirror: landlord must respond in writing within 5 business days and complete necessary repairs within 15, and if he doesn't, the tenant may make the repair and deduct the documented cost from the following month's rent. That deduction right is the only thing that makes the obligation real.

Water supply. Querétaro has pipa deliveries and pressure problems. Add: if municipal water fails for more than X consecutive days for reasons not attributable to the tenant, rent abates proportionally, and cistern/pump/tinaco repair is the landlord's cost.

Pre-existing conditions annex. Already flagged, but make it explicit in the contract body: "The Annex of Existing Conditions dated ___ forms part of this contract, and no item listed therein may be charged against the tenant." Otherwise Clause 20's entire-agreement language can be used to say the annex is outside the deal.

Access and privacy

There is no clause limiting landlord entry. The only entry right in the whole document is showing the house during early termination. That silence cuts both ways — add a clause requiring 24-48 hours' written notice for any entry except genuine emergency, and no entry without the tenant present unless he agrees. A house with a landlord who has keys and no rules is a problem waiting.

Showings near the end of the term. Cap it: showings only in the final 30 days, by appointment, maximum X per week.

Continuity of the deal

Sale of the property. Nothing addresses what happens if the owner sells. Under Mexican law an unregistered lease can be vulnerable to a new owner. Two fixes: add a clause obliging the landlord to make any buyer respect the lease through its term and assume the deposit, and consider registering the lease in the Registro Público de la Propiedad. Check locally — registration costs something but it's what makes the lease enforceable against a purchaser.

Proof of ownership. The landlord declares ownership in I(c). Require it as an annex: copy of the escritura and current predial receipt. If the person signing isn't the registered owner, he needs a poder notarial. This is the most common way rental fraud works in Mexico — someone rents out a house they don't control.

Predial and structural taxes. Silence favors the landlord's later argument. Add one line: predial and any structural or ownership-related charges are the landlord's.

Notices. Clause 19 gives physical domiciles only. Every deadline in this contract runs on written notice. Add email as a valid notice channel with named addresses and confirmation of receipt, or he'll be serving papers by courier to meet a 30-day deadline.

Practical life items

Pets, parking, home office, guests. All absent. Clause 20 kills any verbal assurance. If any of these matter, they go in writing now.

Keys and locks. Add: number of key sets delivered, tenant may change locks provided he gives the landlord a copy at move-out.

Furniture and appliances. If the house comes with anything — stove, refrigerator, boiler, garden equipment — it needs an itemized annex with condition noted, plus a statement of who repairs or replaces each item on failure.

Subletting vs. roommates. Clause 13 bans subletting outright. If your son might share the house or have a partner move in, get an express carve-out naming permitted occupants — otherwise adding a housemate is technically a rescission ground carrying the two-month penalty.

The one to insist on hardest

A cure period. Nowhere in this contract does the tenant get a chance to fix a breach before rescission and penalty attach. Add a general clause: no rescission or penalty may be invoked without prior written notice specifying the breach and a 10-business-day period to remedy it. That single clause defuses most of the sharp edges in Clauses 6, 16 and 18 at once. If the landlord refuses everything else, get this.
