CLAUDE2026-08-19 20:04 UTC

Yes — you have real options, and her being in Spain doesn't block any of them. Not a lawyer, but here's the landscape.

What the law says

  • The landlord is obligated, even without an express clause, to keep the rented property in the same condition throughout the lease, making the necessary repairs. Pre-existing plumbing was hers to pay. If she refuses, you can do the repair and demand reimbursement, and a civil judge decides on damages caused by her failure to repair promptly.
  • Water money collected for consumption that turned out to be her own leak is pago de lo indebido — recoverable.
  • The 1,000-peso extra-occupant charge is only enforceable if it's written in the contract. If it isn't, it's not owed.
  • Verbal leases are valid in Mexico. No written contract doesn't kill your case; transfer records and WhatsApp messages prove the relationship.

Fastest lever: Protección Civil Municipal

The awning and structural water damage are a safety report, not a lawsuit. Call the municipal Protección Civil, they inspect free, and can formally order her to repair. That inspection report then becomes your evidence in everything else.

Free mediation (do this next)

The Centro de Justicia Alternativa of the Poder Judicial de Querétaro provides free mediation for civil matters including breach of lease contracts. You request it by emailing mediacion@tribunalqro.gob.mx with the service request form and valid ID attached. The resulting convenio is enforceable by a judge — it's not just a chat. The Municipio de Querétaro also has its own Dirección de Mediación.

If she won't mediate

Civil claim for reimbursement in the local courts. The property is in Querétaro so Querétaro law and courts apply regardless of where she lives. Amounts this size go through the low-value/oral civil track. The state Defensoría Pública provides free civil representation if you qualify.

Practical

  • Go collectively. Twelve rooms with the same pattern is far stronger than one tenant, and it costs each of you almost nothing at the mediation stage.
  • Gather now: contract, every payment receipt or transfer, water bills before and after the leak, dated photos of the awning and water damage, all messages with her.
  • Do not just stop paying rent to force the issue. That hands her grounds to evict you and flips who's in breach.

CLAUDE2026-08-19 22:16 UTC

She doesn't need to come back, and refusing to appear helps you, not her.

Service of process

She can be served at the property or through whoever manages it for her — collecting rent on her behalf makes that person a de facto representative. If she genuinely can't be located, the court authorizes emplazamiento por edictos (published notices). That adds a few months, it doesn't stop the case.

If she ignores it

The case proceeds en rebeldía — in absentia. She loses the right to contest your evidence, and the judgment is issued anyway. A landlord who never shows up is the easiest opponent you can have, provided your documentation is clean.

Enforcement — this is the real answer

She owns a 12-room building in Querétaro. That's a physical asset inside the court's jurisdiction. A money judgment against her is enforced by embargo on the property: a lien is recorded against the title, and if she still doesn't pay it can go to auction. Living in Spain protects her personally; it protects none of her assets here. She also can't sell or refinance cleanly with a lien recorded.

Consequences she faces

Repayment of what she took, plus legal costs, plus a recorded encumbrance on the building. On the safety side, a Protección Civil finding can bring municipal fines and an order to repair independent of any lawsuit — that one doesn't need her participation at all.

Deposit specifically

She must return it unless she can prove actual damage or unpaid rent. Protect it now:

  • Photograph and video every room and fixture, dated, before you leave.
  • Ask for a written acta de entrega signed on move-out day. If she or her manager refuses to sign, note the refusal in writing and have another tenant witness it.
  • Never let her deduct the plumbing or the water overcharge from it — that's the same money you're already claiming.
  • If she keeps it, the deposit rolls into the same claim. Don't file separately.

Practical reality: mediation exists because a full civil suit for a few thousand pesos costs more in time than it returns. The leverage isn't the lawsuit — it's twelve tenants, a Protección Civil file, and a credible threat of a lien on her building. Most absentee landlords settle at that point.

That changes almost everything. She's a sublandlord, and there's a real chance she has no right to sublet at all.

Why it matters

No building in her name means no asset here to attach. A judgment against a person living in Spain with nothing in Mexico is close to unenforceable. The lien leverage is gone.

Where the leverage moves: the actual owner

Most Mexican leases prohibit subletting without written consent. If she doesn't have it, she's in breach with the owner, and her whole operation — twelve rooms, 12 rents, an extra 1,000 pesos per head — is exposed. Find out:

  • Who owns the building. The Registro Público de la Propiedad in Querétaro allows a search by address for a small fee.
  • Whether the owner knows and consented. Ask them directly.

If the owner didn't authorize it, telling them is your single strongest move. It also means the owner — not her — is the one legally responsible for structural repairs and habitability, and the one Protección Civil will pressure.

Your position doesn't collapse

You're still a tenant with a valid contract against her personally, and it still stands regardless of her title. What you lose is easy enforcement, not the right.

Risk you need to weigh first

If the owner voids her lease, everyone in the building could be told to leave. Talk to the other tenants before you go to the owner. The better play is often to approach the owner together and offer to rent directly — you keep housing, the owner keeps twelve paying tenants, and she's cut out.

Deposit

Realistically, money already in her hands and her in Spain is money you may not see through a court. Practical routes:

  • Negotiate it off your final months' rent, in writing, agreed in advance — not unilaterally withheld.
  • Or settle at mediation, where she has an incentive to close cleanly rather than have the owner learn what she's running.

Before anything else: does your agreement with her name her as owner or as arrendataria, and is there anyone here collecting rent for her?

Two cautions

And "I think the owner lets her" is worth verifying rather than assuming. Verbal permission to occupy is not the same as written authorization to sublet twelve rooms commercially. What to ask for

A certificado de gravámenes (or certificado de libertad de gravamen) for the property. It names the registered owner and shows any liens.

Whether you're allowed

Yes. It's a public-access document — anyone can request it, you don't need to be the owner.

The folio real problem

Normally you need the folio real or clave catastral, which you get off the deed or the predial receipt — neither of which you have. You can go to the counter with just the property address and staff will search for it. That's the route for you. If they push back, the fallback is Catastro Municipal, which converts an address to a clave catastral, then you return to the IRC with that.

Steps at the window

  1. Take your residency card (valid photo ID) and the full street address, including number and colonia.
  2. Ask them to locate the folio by address, then request the certificado.
  3. They generate the payment slip; you pay at the window, a bank, or online. Keep the receipt.
  4. You get a tracking folio. Expect roughly 3 to 5 business days.

Before you go

Call ext. 1850 or use the WhatsApp number and ask two things: the current fee, and whether they'll do an address-only search without a clave catastral. Ten minutes on the phone saves a wasted trip. If they say no to address-only, go to Catastro first.

One caveat

The registered owner may not be the person she rents from either — buildings get managed through relatives or intermediaries. The certificate gives you the legal titleholder, which is the person whose interests actually matter here, but don't assume that's who she deals with day to day.
