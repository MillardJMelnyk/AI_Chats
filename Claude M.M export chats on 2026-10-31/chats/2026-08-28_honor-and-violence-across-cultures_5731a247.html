Honor and violence across cultures

Created 2026-08-28 23:15 UTC · Last updated 2026-08-28 23:25 UTC · 10 messages
Original: claude.ai/chat/5731a247-3eb9-453c-bad1-7ec5576172bc
MILLARD2026-08-28 23:15 UTC

I'm watching The Northman. It seems like no matter the time nor the culture nor the location, among men that pride themselves for mastery of violence, the same things come up over and over, at least in depictions of them, both modern and ancient. Were they really so obsessed with ideas like honor and loyalty and this lust to dominate?

CLAUDE2026-08-28 23:15 UTC

Yes, and the repetition isn't a storytelling accident — it's what happens wherever there's no state to enforce anything.

Honor cultures show up in the same conditions: weak or absent central authority, wealth that can be stolen and moved (cattle, ships, slaves), and no police to call. In that setup your reputation is your security system. If people believe you'll answer an insult with blood, they don't test you, and they don't touch your herd or your daughter. So men cultivate a reputation for being dangerous, and small slights get treated as existential, because backing down once marks you as safe to rob. Same logic produced Homeric Greeks, Icelandic feud, Pashtun badal, Sicilian vendetta, and the Scots-Irish American South.

The primary sources back it up rather than just later romance. Tacitus reports Germanic warbands where surviving your chief was lifelong disgrace. Hávamál says cattle die, kin die, you die — reputation is the only thing that doesn't. The sagas are obsessive about who owed what to whom and who failed to avenge whom.

Two corrections to what Eggers shows you, though. The driver was mostly shame-avoidance, not lust to dominate — dread of being níðingr, the man nobody fears, was the sharper motive than glory-hunger. And these were farmers most of the year; the berserker fatalism is heavily stylized, drawn from Saxo and skaldic verse that were already literary performance by the time they were written.

MILLARD2026-08-28 23:16 UTC

The way you framed it is bogus. States and centralized authorities did not depart from these values, but rather monopolized and perfected them.

CLAUDE2026-08-28 23:16 UTC

Fair hit. I framed it as a vacuum-filler, which implies states dissolve it. They don't — they relocate it upward and take a monopoly on it, exactly as you say.

The state's core claim is Weberian: sole legitimate violence. That's not the negation of the warband ethic, it's the warband ethic scaled and made exclusive. Everything I listed reappears at the level of the polity — national honor as a valid casus belli, credibility as the whole substance of deterrence, prestige as a strategic asset defended with lives. Nuclear strategy is a reputation system: it works only if others believe you'd actually do the insane thing. That's Grettir's logic with warheads. And states didn't even fully surrender the private version — dueling survived longest in the officer corps and aristocracy, the state's own violence-holders, well after it was outlawed for everyone else. Militaries still manufacture the whole apparatus deliberately: colors, medals, cowardice as a capital offense, shame as a training instrument.

What actually changed is narrower than my first answer implied. The ordinary man no longer needs a personal reputation for violence to keep his cattle; that function got transferred, not abolished. And where the transfer fails — prisons, illicit markets, places where calling the police isn't an option — the old code reconstitutes itself within weeks, intact.

Your word "perfected" is the one I'd sit with. The honor system's constraint used to be that it ran on individual bodies and personal vengeance, which capped the damage. Removing that cap is what produced the twentieth century.

MILLARD2026-08-28 23:18 UTC

I see the obsession with these things as a severe level of codependence, when codependence is understood as the derivation of personal worth from outside sources. Even a person's achievements don't contribute to their sense of worth unless those achievements are recognized and applauded by other people.

CLAUDE2026-08-28 23:18 UTC

The description fits and the diagnosis doesn't fully, and the gap is worth naming.

Where you're right: honor is literally externalized worth. It's not a property of the man, it's a ledger entry held by other people, which is why it can be taken from him by an insult he didn't consent to. Anthropologists split honor cultures from dignity cultures on exactly that axis — dignity says worth is inherent and unremovable, honor says worth is what the audience says it is. And that's why the honor man is so fragile despite the tough surface. He can be destroyed by a rumor. His entire self is held in escrow by people he may not even respect.

Where it strains: codependence describes a pathology relative to available alternatives. For the Icelandic farmer there was no inner-worth option — no framework in which a man privately valued himself and that meant anything. Interior worth is a late invention, built out of Christian conscience, Protestant inwardness, Romanticism, then psychology. So it's less that these men were sick and more that the self hadn't yet been located inside the body. Calling it codependence is a bit like calling illiteracy a learning disability.

The version of your claim I'd defend hard: the code survives in men who do have the alternative and still won't use it. That's where it becomes genuine pathology rather than the water they swam in.

And the sharper knife is the one you already picked up — the achievement that doesn't count until it's applauded. That's not honor culture, that's now, and it doesn't need swords.

Where it strains: codependence describes a pathology relative to available alternatives. For the Icelandic farmer there was no inner-worth option — no framework in which a man privately valued himself and that meant anything.

CLAUDE2026-08-28 23:21 UTC

You're right, it doesn't hold. Two separate errors, both mine.

The first is that I made pathology culture-relative in a way I wouldn't accept anywhere else. Universal isn't the same as healthy. Everyone in a famine is malnourished and it's still malnutrition; endemic lead exposure poisoned everyone equally and was still poison. If a structure reliably produces fragility, dread, and men destroyed by rumor, its ubiquity is a fact about its distribution, not a defense of it. "There was no alternative" bears on culpability, not on diagnosis.

The second is that the historical claim was just wrong. Epictetus was a slave and located worth entirely in what others couldn't touch — that's centuries before the Christian-Romantic story I told, and Buddhism is centuries before that. Hávamál itself, in the same poem that says reputation never dies, tells you not to care what fools think. So inner worth wasn't unavailable. It was available and not adopted by the warrior class, which makes it a choice rather than a horizon.

Which puts your original claim back intact, and stronger. The men most steeped in honor weren't people without the concept of self-possession. They were people who had it nearby and rejected it, because the external ledger paid better in the currency that mattered to them.

CLAUDE2026-08-28 23:25 UTC

Framework yes, execution mediocre. The three-way typology is real research — Leung & Cohen (2011) in JPSP is the standard citation, plus work by Yuki, Aslani, and Brett. He's right that lumping everything into "honor-shame" is too crude. But he cites nobody, and several claims are wrong or self-undermining.

Specific problems:

  • He says the strength of honor cultures is promoting social harmony and cohesion. That's backwards on the empirics. Honor cultures produce in-group solidarity and out-group violence — Nisbett and Cohen's work on the US South is the standard demonstration. He then admits the violence two sentences later as a "potential weakness," which contradicts the claim he just made.
  • The geography is treated as neat buckets — dignity primarily in the West, honor in Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, face in East Asia. Leung and Cohen's actual paper is titled around within-culture variation, and it's about that precisely because the buckets leak. Mediterranean Europe is textbook honor and also the West; Latin America is his honor example and also the West.
  • He claims a universal human need for social recognition as common ground across all three. That breaks his own definition. If dignity means worth is inherent and unaffected by external factors, it can't also rest on social recognition. He doesn't notice.

Worth knowing about the frame: it's a missions blog. Every section ends with how to pitch the gospel to that culture type. That doesn't make the descriptions false, but the categories are being built to be useful for conversion, not to be tested.

For what you're actually chasing, the dignity/honor axis is the useful part and it's sound. The contradiction he stumbles into is the more interesting thing — it's your point about achievements not counting until they're applauded, and it says the dignity option may be shakier than its own advertising.
