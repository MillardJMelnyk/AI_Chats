Other post-2010 material

  • No additional peer-reviewed studies were located that systematically measure VAERS capture rates across a broad set of “all categories” (mild + serious, multiple vaccines, multiple outcomes) after 2010.
  • General statements in CDC/VAERS documentation and reviews continue to say underreporting “varies widely,” is higher for serious/unexpected events, and cannot be reduced to one number.
  • Activist or secondary analyses (e.g., calculations using V-Safe data or other proxies claiming factors of 26× or higher) exist but are not peer-reviewed sensitivity studies of the type above.

On averaging
There is no set of post-2010 studies that supply rates “for all categories” that can be averaged into a single meaningful number. The only quantified sensitivities after 2010 are the event-specific ones listed above for anaphylaxis and GBS.
If you simply take the midpoint or arithmetic mean of the published ranges in that one study:

  • Anaphylaxis mid-range ≈ 44–45% capture → underreporting factor ≈ 2.2×
  • GBS mid-range ≈ 38% capture → underreporting factor ≈ 2.6×

That arithmetic exercise is not scientifically valid as a general VAERS underreporting rate—it mixes different vaccines, different background rates, and only two serious outcomes. The study itself does not compute or endorse any overall average.
Bottom line from the available post-2010 quantified evidence: for the serious events that have been studied, VAERS capture has ranged from roughly 12% to 76%. No broader, averaged figure for “all categories” exists in the peer-reviewed literature after 2010.

CLAUDE2026-08-28 18:49 UTC

No single validated number exists for either, and the two differ by an order of magnitude, so pick which one you want:

Modern global (recent, defensible): CDC/WHO reported 205,153 measles cases worldwide in 2022 against a modeled 9,232,300 actual cases and 136,200 deaths — that's a ~45× case underreporting factor from the current WHO/CDC estimation model. Conservative floor: 40×.

US pre-vaccine era (what your chart argument needs): ~500,000 reported cases vs an estimated 3 to 4 million infected each year — 6–8×; conservative 6×.

Deaths are the different animal: US measles deaths came from death certificates via vital statistics, not from notifiable-disease reporting, so their capture is far better than case capture — there is no published multiplier in the modern literature putting US measles death underreporting anywhere near the case figure. That asymmetry is why the pre-1963 death curve and the case curve behave differently, and it's a real limit on how far a case-underreporting number can be pushed onto the death numbers.

Which frame do you want the number for — US pre-1963, or current global?

CLAUDE2026-08-28 18:56 UTC

The gap is narrower than that framing suggests. Death reports to VAERS are individually reviewed by CDC and FDA clinicians with medical records and autopsy reports pulled, and vaccine-mortality analyses have been run in the Vaccine Safety Datalink. What doesn't exist is a sensitivity study — a measured capture fraction — and that's because the method requires a known true rate to divide into, and for vaccine-caused death there isn't one to measure against. That's a real epistemic limit, and it does mean nobody can tell you what fraction of vaccine-caused deaths reach VAERS. Whether the limit is negligence or arithmetic depends on whether you think the denominator was obtainable and skipped.
