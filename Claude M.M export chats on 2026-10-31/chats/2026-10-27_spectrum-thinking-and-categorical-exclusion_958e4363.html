The field that tests exactly this is taxometrics, started by Paul Meehl: it checks whether a trait is a true category (different in kind) or a continuum (different in degree), and treating a continuum as a category is usually called false dichotomization or reification, driven by what psychologists call psychological essentialism. For psychopathy, Edens et al. (2006, "Psychopathic, not psychopath") found it is a continuum, and for cults, Steven Hassan's "Influence Continuum" and the "high-control group" framing make the same correction.

Okay identify the essential traits of the move and the key motivations for making it

CLAUDE2026-10-27 15:46 UTC

At the structural level, the move's essential traits are:

  1. A trait that varies by degree across a whole population.
  2. A cutoff placed near one extreme, with everything past it given its own name (cult, psychopath).
  3. That name treated as a difference in kind, so anyone below the cut counts as lacking the trait, not having less of it.
  4. The named zone becomes the marked exception, making everything else the unmarked normal by default.
  5. The cut placed by those doing the naming, almost always where they fall outside it.

The key motivations, strongest first:

  1. Self-exoneration: the namer and their group land on the clean side.
  2. Protecting institutions that carry the trait in moderate degree, such as mainstream churches, states, and corporations.
  3. Boundary maintenance: the named extreme supplies the contrast that defines and binds the in-group, per Erikson.
  4. Control: whoever sets the cut decides who gets named.
  5. Mental economy: a yes/no category is easier to think and talk with than a gradient.

The specific move you're describing—drawing a cutoff near the extreme of a dimensional trait, labeling everything beyond it as a distinct category, and thereby treating the moderate range as "not that thing at all"—has been analyzed most directly under the framework of "concept creep," coined by Nick Haslam (University of Melbourne).

🎯 The Closest Match: Nick Haslam's "Concept Creep"

Haslam's 2016 paper, "Concept Creep: Psychology's Expanding Concepts of Harm and Pathology," describes exactly the boundary-drawing dynamic you outline, though he focuses on how concepts expand rather than how they are used to exonerate the middle .

· The Specific Mechanism: Haslam identifies "vertical expansion," where concepts stretch "downward to capture quantitatively less extreme phenomena" . This is the inverse of your observation: as the labeled category expands downward, the unlabeled "moderate" zone shrinks, making it easier for people to say, "I'm not that" and ignore the lower-level traits they do possess.
· The Examples You Cited: Haslam's framework explicitly covers "mental disorder" (relevant to psychopathy) and "abuse" (relevant to high-control groups) as concepts that have undergone this semantic dilation .
· The "Exoneration" Effect: Haslam argues that this creep risks "pathologizing everyday experience" and creating a sense of "virtuous but impotent victimhood" . While he doesn't use the word "exonerate," the implication is that the binary label (disorder vs. normal) obscures the dimensional reality.

🔍 Other Adjacent Analyses (But Not Exact Matches)

· Psychopathy as a Dimensional Construct: Legal and forensic scholarship notes that psychopathy exists on a spectrum, but the PCL-R test imposes a hard cutoff (often a score of 30) to label someone a "psychopath" . This creates the exact dynamic you describe, but the literature focuses on the stigma of the label rather than naming the rhetorical move of exonerating the middle.
· The "Cult" Label: Sociologists note that "cult" is a pejorative label often applied to high-control groups, while "new religious movement" is the neutral term . However, the specific argument that labeling only the extreme "cult" exonerates merely "controlling" mainstream groups is a critique found in survivor communities, not a formally named academic concept in the search results.

Bottom line: If you are looking for the author who analyzed the semantic move of drawing a sharp line on a continuum to separate the "extreme" from the "normal," Nick Haslam's "Concept Creep" is the established framework, even though his focus is on the expansion of the label rather than the exoneration of the middle.
