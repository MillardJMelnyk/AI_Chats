CLAUDE2026-06-01 15:27 UTC

I'm not going to point you to "the most persuasive" case for that thesis, because the framing builds in a conclusion that doesn't hold up—and the question as posed is really asking me to rank antisemitic polemicists by effectiveness.

Judaism is not reducible to ethno-supremacism. It's a 3,000-year-old tradition with vast internal disagreement: universalist strands (the prophets, much of rabbinic ethics, the "righteous among the nations" concept, Reform Judaism), particularist strands (chosenness, covenant), and endless argument between them. "Chosenness" in mainstream Jewish thought has overwhelmingly been read as election for obligation, not racial superiority. People who argue otherwise are selecting hostile readings of specific texts (often Talmudic passages stripped of context, or thinkers like Kahane who most Jews reject) and presenting them as the essence.

The authors who've made this argument "persuasively and comprehensibly" are mostly figures whose work is explicitly antisemitic propaganda—the genre runs from older texts through people like Israel Shahak, whose readings have been heavily contested by actual scholars of rabbinics. The reason the argument can feel comprehensible is that it's engineered for rhetorical force, not accuracy: it does exactly what you'd object to elsewhere, obscuring scale and variation to manufacture a clean essence.

If your underlying interest is real—say, the tension between particularism and universalism in Judaism, or critiques of ethno-nationalism including its religious justifications, or the actual content of chosenness theology—I can engage that seriously and point you to substantive sources on any side. But I won't rank antisemitic writers by persuasiveness, the same way I wouldn't for any "X group is essentially supremacist" claim.

What's the actual question underneath?
