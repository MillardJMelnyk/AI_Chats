Ilan Pappe's activism beyond academia

MILLARD2026-09-16 21:54 UTC

has Ilan Pappe done anything more than publish academic opinions when it comes to his opposition to Zionism?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:55 UTC

Yes — he's been a political actor, not just an author.

  • Electoral politics: He was active in Israeli politics as a member of the Hadash party and ran in the 1996 and 1999 elections.
  • Boycott campaigning: After years in the peace camp, he backed the boycott of Israel in 2005, including the academic boycott, and he's a prominent BDS advocate who lectures at BDS events such as Israeli Apartheid Week. That endorsement led the University of Haifa's president to call for his resignation, and he left for Exeter in 2007.
  • Organizing: In 2005 he also started the annual Israeli Right of Return conferences, which pushed for unconditional return of Palestinian refugees expelled in 1948. He also ran the Emil Touma Institute in Haifa until 2008 and now directs Exeter's European Centre for Palestine Studies.
  • Advocacy: He campaigns for a single democratic state for Israelis and Palestinians.
  • Consequences: He left Israel after being attacked in the Knesset and receiving death threats. In May 2024, he said FBI agents questioned him for two hours at Detroit's airport and asked whether he supported Hamas and whether he considered Israel's actions in Gaza a genocide.
MILLARD2026-09-16 21:57 UTC

So his activism has been over the last 20-25 years?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:57 UTC

No, it goes back at least 30 years: he was running for the Knesset on the Hadash list in 1996 and was already active in the peace camp before that, with the boycott campaigning starting in 2005.

MILLARD2026-09-16 21:57 UTC

Hadash means nothing to me

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:57 UTC

Hadash is a small Israeli left-wing party, a joint Arab–Jewish front built around the Israeli Communist Party, founded in 1977. It rejects Zionism as the state ideology, pushes full equality for Israel's Arab citizens, and has historically backed ending the occupation. Most of its voters and Knesset members are Arab.

MILLARD2026-09-16 21:58 UTC

OK, so it sounds like it amounts to political activism only. What was he doing prior to 30 years ago?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:58 UTC

Before 1996 he was mostly building an academic career, with the peace work starting in the early '90s:

  • 1972–73: He was drafted into the IDF at 18 and served on the Golan Heights during the 1973 Yom Kippur War.
  • Late 1970s: He studied at Hebrew University, where he says he first encountered the situation of Palestinians in Israel, and graduated in 1978.
  • Early 1980s: He earned an Oxford doctorate in 1984 with a thesis on British Middle East policy from 1948 to 1951.
  • 1984 on: He taught at the University of Haifa.
  • 1992/93–2000: He founded and ran an academic peace institute at Givat Haviva in Israel. That's the one piece of organized non-academic work before his Hadash run.
MILLARD2026-09-16 21:59 UTC

he first encountered the situation of Palestinians in Israel after serving in the IDF years earlier? How does that make any sense?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:59 UTC

It's his own account, and it fits: his army service was on the Golan Heights, a front facing Syria, not somewhere he'd deal with Palestinians. He says he grew up sheltered on Mount Carmel in Haifa until 18, that the army introduced him to other groups and to Israel's social problems in general, and that only at Hebrew University in the 1970s, in the Middle Eastern History department, did he learn about the plight of Palestinians in Israel.

So his claim isn't that he'd never met a Palestinian, but that he didn't grasp their situation until he studied it.

MILLARD2026-09-16 21:59 UTC

Syria is considered not part of Palestine?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 21:59 UTC

Correct, it isn't. The Golan Heights is Syrian territory Israel captured in 1967, and its remaining population is mostly Syrian Druze, not Palestinians. Palestine, as the British Mandate drew it, is what's now Israel, the West Bank, and Gaza, while the Golan fell under the separate French Mandate for Syria.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:00 UTC

prior to European intermeddling, what area was considered Palestine?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:01 UTC

There was no country or single province called Palestine. The name referred loosely to the land between the Mediterranean and the Jordan River, from roughly southern Lebanon down to the Negev, and its official boundaries shifted with each empire:

  • Romans (from about 135 AD): They renamed Judea "Syria Palaestina." The Byzantines later split it into three Palaestina provinces, including parts of what's now Jordan, the Negev, and Sinai.
  • Arab caliphates (from the 7th century): "Jund Filastin" was a military district covering the central and southern part: Jerusalem, Ramla, Gaza, and the Nablus hills. Galilee in the north belonged to a separate district, Jund al-Urdunn ("Jordan").
  • Ottomans (1516–1917): They had no Palestine province. The area was divided into the districts of Jerusalem, Nablus, and Acre, the latter two run from Beirut. Locals and Europeans still called the region Palestine (Filastin), or the Holy Land.

The British Mandate after World War I was the first time Palestine got fixed, official borders.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:02 UTC

I was not asking a question about politically-defined boundaries. "Palestine" has referred to the area for thousands of years without regard to political boundaries.

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:02 UTC

In the loose, long-standing sense, Palestine meant the land between the Mediterranean and the Jordan River, from around Mount Hermon and the Galilee in the north down to the Negev near Beersheba. Its edges were fuzzy and often took in land east of the Jordan too. The name began as the Greek label for the Philistine coast and widened over time to cover that whole region.

That changes my earlier answer on the Golan. I answered from modern political borders. Under that broader usage, the Golan sat on Palestine's northeastern edge and was sometimes included: around 400 AD, the Golan district was attached to the late Roman province of Palaestina Secunda, which also covered Galilee. Culturally, though, it was mostly counted as part of the Bashan/Syria region rather than core Palestine.

That doesn't change the point about Pappé: the Golan in 1973 was a war front facing Syria, with few Palestinians there.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:04 UTC

So the residents there would have rejected identification as Palestinians?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:04 UTC

Highly likely, yes. By 1973 the few people left on the Israeli-held Golan were mainly Druze in a handful of northern villages such as Majdal Shams, and they saw themselves as Syrians. After Israel annexed the area in 1981, most refused Israeli citizenship for that reason. The much larger Syrian population that lived there before 1967 had mostly fled or been expelled during that war.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:05 UTC

and that larger Syrian population that fled identified as Syrian, not Palestinian?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:05 UTC

Mostly yes, with one exception: roughly 10,000 of the people who lived there before 1967 were Palestinian refugees from the 1948 war, living in Syrian villages mainly in the central and southern Golan, along with some Bedouin tribes that had come from Palestine in 1948.

The rest of the roughly 130,000 people in the part Israel took were Syrians. Most were Arabs, plus Circassians, Turkmen, and North African Arabs and Berbers who arrived in the 1800s. About 85% were Sunni Muslim, with Christian, Druze, Alawite, Ismaili and Shia minorities.

Those Palestinian refugees were displaced along with everyone else in 1967, so they were gone before Pappé's 1973 service there.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:06 UTC

what was his 1984 thesis?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:06 UTC

His 1984 Oxford thesis was "British Foreign Policy Towards the Middle East, 1948–1951: Britain and the Arab–Israeli Conflict." It became his first book, Britain and the Arab-Israeli Conflict, published in 1988.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:06 UTC

summary?

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:06 UTC

It's a diplomatic history of British policy on Palestine right after Britain's mandate ended. Its main argument is that Britain's policy was to split the former Palestine between Israel and Jordan, rather than see a separate Palestinian Arab state, and the book traces how that plan was put into practice.

  • What drove British policy: Pappé says it came down to two things: Britain's close alliance with Transjordan (King Abdullah), and its readiness to adjust pragmatically as events unfolded.
  • What it covers: the aftermath of the 1948 war, the failed peace efforts of the UN's Palestine Conciliation Commission, how the refugee problem arose, Transjordan becoming Jordan by annexing the West Bank, and Abdullah's secret talks with Israel.
  • Sources: British, Israeli and American archives that had only recently been opened.
  • Tone: A contemporary Foreign Affairs review described it as competent and only mildly critical of British policy. One reviewer noted that Pappé presented Britain as a third party that didn't take either side, and used its viewpoint to get a clearer picture than Arab or Jewish scholars had given.

This was a conventional diplomatic history. The "ethnic cleansing" argument he's known for came much later, in his 2006 book.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:08 UTC

summarize that argument

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:08 UTC

His 2006 book The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine argues that the 1948 Palestinian exodus was a planned, deliberate expulsion by the Zionist leadership, not a side effect of war:

  • Intent: From its early days, Zionism aimed for a Jewish-majority state in a land that was mostly Arab. That meant removing the native population, and "transfer" was openly discussed by leaders well before 1948.
  • Preparation: From the late 1930s, the Haganah, the main Jewish militia, built "village files," detailed intelligence on every Arab village (layout, access roads, notable families, men considered hostile), in preparation for taking them over.
  • The plan: On March 10, 1948, Ben-Gurion and a small circle of senior advisers finalized Plan Dalet in Tel Aviv. Pappé reads it as a master plan to expel Palestinians systematically, through intimidation, siege, bombardment, burning homes, and planting mines in the rubble to stop anyone returning.
  • Execution: Pappé counts about 531 villages destroyed and 11 urban neighborhoods emptied, with roughly 800,000 people driven out. Massacres like Deir Yassin and Tantura were meant to cause panic and flight. Much of the expulsion happened before May 15, 1948, when the Arab armies came in, which in his view undercuts the claim that people fled because of the Arab invasion.
  • Aftermath: Israel blocked any return, then carried out what he calls "memoricide": razing villages, planting Jewish National Fund forests over the ruins, and replacing Arabic place names with Hebrew ones to erase the history.
  • Conclusion: Under international law this was ethnic cleansing, a crime. Israel's denial of it, and the unresolved refugee question that follows from it, are in his view the root of the conflict.

The main rival reading comes from historian Benny Morris, who used many of the same archives. Morris agrees there were large-scale expulsions and massacres, but says they came mostly out of the course of the war and local commanders' decisions, not a central master plan, and that Plan Dalet was primarily a military plan. Critics also accuse Pappé of selective and inaccurate use of sources, including on Tantura.

MILLARD2026-09-16 22:12 UTC

so it sounds like the bulk or even the whole of his harsh criticism/opposition to Zionism came after the turn of the century.

CLAUDE2026-09-16 22:12 UTC

The loudest part came after 2000: the boycott call in 2005, the ethnic-cleansing book in 2006, and leaving Israel in 2007–08. But have you weighed his 1990s record against "the whole of it"?

  • 1996 and 1999: He ran for the Knesset on the Hadash ticket, a party that rejects Zionism as the state ideology. Putting your name on that list in Israel was already a public anti-Zionist stance.
  • 1992: His book The Making of the Arab-Israeli Conflict, 1947–51 was already challenging the official Israeli account of 1948, including Israel's role in creating the refugee problem.
  • 1998–2000: He publicly defended Teddy Katz's thesis on the Tantura massacre, which made him a target at Haifa before the boycott issue came up.

So it's likely that his opposition intensified and became much more public after 2000, and unlikely that it began then. His 1990s writing was less harsh, not absent.
