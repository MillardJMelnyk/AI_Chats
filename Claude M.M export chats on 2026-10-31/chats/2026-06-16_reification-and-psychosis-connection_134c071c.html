CLAUDE2026-06-16 12:34 UTC

No, not as a general claim. Here's why the framing is off:

Reification (treating abstractions as concrete things) is ubiquitous and mostly functional. Language itself requires it. "The economy is suffering" — that's reification. It doesn't produce psychosis; it produces communication.

Psychosis involves a breakdown in the ability to distinguish internal states from external reality — not just misclassifying the ontological status of abstractions.

The closer claim — one worth taking seriously — is:

Reification becomes pathological when the reified abstraction is then experienced as an agent with authority over the subject.

That's a narrower and more defensible thesis. The institution becomes real, then becomes the one doing the experiencing, judging, and demanding — and the actual subject gets hollowed out. That's your territory, given your manuscript's argument.

But "seminal move towards psychosis" overshoots. It makes reification sound like a necessary precursor to a clinical phenomenon, which it isn't. What reification is seminal to is alienation — the subject becoming estranged from their own experience by attributing it to the abstraction.

If you're building toward a claim that institutional thought is structurally alienating (not psychosis-inducing), that holds up better and doesn't require the clinical overreach.

What's the actual thesis you're driving at?
