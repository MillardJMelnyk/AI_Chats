Good question, and one with a lot of well-trodden ground. Let me lay out the main structural approaches, then give you my take on what fits algorithmic intraday work best.

The core structures

1. Weighted linear score (the workhorse)

Each measurement produces a normalized signal in [-1, +1] (short to long) or [0, 1] (confidence in "act now"). You sum weighted contributions:

S_direction = Σ wᵢ · sᵢ      where sᵢ ∈ [-1, +1]
S_timing    = Σ vⱼ · tⱼ      where tⱼ ∈ [0, 1]

Trade if |S_direction| > θ_dir AND S_timing > θ_time, with sign giving direction. Simple, transparent, easy to backtest weight sensitivity.

2. Two-factor decomposition (direction × timing)

Treat the two questions as orthogonal axes — which is exactly how you framed it, and that's the right instinct. Direction signals (trend, regime, momentum bias, structural levels) feed one score; timing signals (trigger conditions, volatility expansion, microstructure, session phase) feed the other. You only enter when both clear independent thresholds. This prevents a strong directional view from dragging you into a bad entry, and prevents a great trigger from firing into noise.

3. Hierarchical / gated (filter cascade)

Signals are organized in tiers. Lower tiers are veto gates (regime filter, spread acceptable, news blackout), upper tiers contribute weighted score. A failed gate kills the setup regardless of score. This matches how discretionary traders actually think and avoids the "score barely passed because everything was mediocre" failure mode.

4. Bayesian / log-odds aggregation

Each signal contributes a log-likelihood ratio: log(P(signal | long) / P(signal | short)). Sum them, convert back to probability. Mathematically principled, handles signal correlation poorly unless you model it, and requires you to actually estimate the conditional distributions from data — which for M1 XAUUSD you have plenty of.

5. Voting / ensemble with confidence

Each measurement votes long/short/neutral with a confidence weight. Aggregate via weighted majority. Less expressive than linear scoring but robust to a single signal going haywire.

Practical points worth being explicit about

Normalization is where these systems live or die. Each sᵢ needs to map raw indicator output to a comparable scale. For bounded indicators (slope angle, percentile rank) this is easy. For unbounded ones (price distance, spread), use rolling z-score or empirical percentile against a trailing window. Don't mix raw scales into a weighted sum.

Weights should be derived, not guessed. Once you have the structure, fit weights against historical labeled outcomes (logistic regression on win/loss, or grid search over a profit metric). Equal weights is a fine starting prior but a bad final answer.

Correlation between signals will inflate confidence. Three momentum indicators agreeing isn't three pieces of evidence — it's roughly one. Either group correlated signals into a single composite before weighting, or use a method (Bayesian with covariance, PCA, or just hand-grouping) that accounts for it. This is the single biggest reason naive weighted scores overfit.

Keep the score auditable. When a trade fires, you want to log each sᵢ, wᵢ, and contribution. When it loses, you want to see which component carried the score and whether that component has been reliable lately.

Threshold hysteresis. θ_dir for entry should be higher than the threshold for staying in / not reversing. Otherwise you get whipsaw at the boundary.

Understood. Direction is fixed exogenously, every measurement is already on a 0–100 scale expressing confidence in that direction at the present moment, and you want an aggregation function smarter than a weighted sum, feeding a single gauge.

Here are the structures that fit, ranked by how well they match what you described.

1. Weighted geometric mean (or weighted power mean)

C = (Π mᵢ^wᵢ) ^ (1 / Σwᵢ)

Behaves like a weighted average but penalizes disagreement. One measurement at 10 drags the composite down hard even if everything else is at 90 — which is usually what you want for entry confidence: any single component screaming "no" should matter more than five components mildly nodding "yes."

Generalize to the weighted power mean:

C = (Σ wᵢ · mᵢ^p / Σwᵢ) ^ (1/p)
  • p = 1 → arithmetic mean (sum-like)
  • p → 0 → geometric mean (penalizes weakness)
  • p = -1 → harmonic mean (penalizes weakness harder)
  • p → -∞ → min (single weakest link decides)
  • p → +∞ → max (single strongest dominates)

One parameter, p, lets you tune how unforgiving the composite is toward laggards. For an entry gauge I'd start around p = 0 to p = -1.

2. Noisy-OR / probabilistic accumulation

Treat each mᵢ/100 as an independent probability that condition i supports entry. Composite is the probability that at least one strong-enough piece of evidence exists, weighted:

C = 1 - Π (1 - (mᵢ/100)^(1/wᵢ))

Opposite character from #1: this rewards accumulation. Several mediocre signals can collectively push the gauge up. Useful if your philosophy is "enough small pieces of evidence = a real edge." Less useful if you want unanimity.

3. OWA (Ordered Weighted Average)

Weights aren't tied to specific measurements but to their rank after sorting. You decide the shape:

sort m₁..mₙ descending → m₍₁₎..m₍ₙ₎
C = Σ wₖ · m₍ₖ₎
  • Front-loaded weights → "best few signals decide" (optimistic)
  • Back-loaded weights → "weakest signals decide" (conservative)
  • Middle-loaded → trimmed mean (robust to outliers)

This is the structure to use if you want to say things like "the top 3 strongest signals carry 70% of the gauge" without hard-coding which signals those are.

4. Choquet integral

The serious answer when measurements are correlated and you don't want to double-count. Instead of weights per measurement, you assign weights to subsets of measurements (a fuzzy measure), so you can express "TEMA slope and ZZ direction together add less than the sum of their parts because they're correlated" or "SCR + shape-chain agreement is worth more than either alone."

Heavier to specify (2ⁿ subset weights, though usually parameterized) and heavier to reason about, but it's the mathematically honest way to handle correlated evidence on a 0–100 scale without bolting on a separate decorrelation step.

5. Compensatory + non-compensatory hybrid

Two-part composite:

C = G · A

where G ∈ [0,1] is a soft gating function (product of sigmoids over critical measurements that must be above some level) and A is one of #1–#4 over the rest. Lets you encode "this measurement isn't just weighted — it has to be at least 40 or the whole gauge collapses" without making it a hard boolean veto. The gauge degrades smoothly as critical signals weaken.

My recommendation

For what you described — single gauge, pre-assigned weights, smarter than sum, with the directional question already settled — weighted power mean with p between -1 and 0 is the sweet spot. It's:

  • A single closed-form expression
  • One interpretable tuning knob (p = how unforgiving)
  • Reduces to familiar means at boundary values, so you can sanity-check
  • Naturally penalizes weak components, which matches the "all systems go before redline" mental model of a gauge
  • Trivial to implement in both Python and MQL5

If you later find that certain measurement pairs are double-counting, wrap it in a Choquet-style adjustment or pre-collapse the correlated pair into a single composite measurement before it enters the power mean.
