Tomato seedling leaves turning yellow

MY TOMATO SEEDLINGS LEAVES ARE TURNING A SLIGHT YELLOW COLOR AND NOT DARK GREEN

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:00 UTC

Nitrogen deficiency — seedlings have used up what was in the seed and the starting mix has no fertilizer.

Feed them a diluted balanced liquid fertilizer (quarter to half strength) every watering or two.

Two things that would change that answer: if only the bottom two round leaves (cotyledons) are yellowing, that's normal and you ignore it; if the mix stays soggy and the whole plant is pale, it's overwatering drowning the roots, not lack of food — let the top dry out before watering again.

MILLARD2026-08-30 21:01 UTC

you sure? they are yellowing but new leaves have barely started to show at all

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:01 UTC

Not fully sure, no. Cotyledons yellowing before true leaves are established is early.

At that stage it usually means the seedling ran out of stored food ahead of schedule, and the common causes are low light (leggy, stretched stem) or waterlogged mix.

Check two things: is the stem long and thin, and is the mix wet all the time. If yes to light, get them closer to strong light. If yes to water, let the surface dry between waterings. If neither, feed a quarter-strength balanced liquid fertilizer and they'll green up.

MILLARD2026-08-30 21:02 UTC

what can i use for natural fertilizer? just pee and smashed chicken bones is ok?

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:02 UTC

Pee yes, bones no.

Urine is real nitrogen fertilizer, but it's strong and salty. Dilute it at least 15:1 with water for seedlings — 10:1 or stronger will burn them. Use it fresh, same day, and don't get it on the leaves. Skip it if you're on medication.

Smashed bones do almost nothing — bone is phosphorus locked in a hard mineral matrix that takes years to break down at that size. To make it usable you have to burn them to white ash and grind them to powder. Even then it's phosphorus, not the nitrogen your seedlings are short of.

Other options you probably have: coffee grounds steeped in water, or water from boiling vegetables (cooled, unsalted).

MILLARD2026-08-30 21:03 UTC

i meant bone meal i was going to make my own boiling, drying in the overn then asmashing and pulverizing the bones

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:03 UTC

That works — that's essentially how bone meal is made.

Boil to strip fat and tissue, dry them out hard in the oven (200°C for an hour or two until they're brittle and snap rather than bend), then crush. The finer the powder the faster it releases; coarse chips can sit in the soil for years doing nothing.

Two things: it's slow-release phosphorus and calcium, so it won't fix the yellowing you're seeing now — that's a nitrogen problem. And it's better mixed into the potting soil when you transplant into the jugs than sprinkled on seedlings. The calcium is worth having for tomatoes, it helps against blossom end rot later.

MILLARD2026-08-30 21:04 UTC

so water from boiling vegetables works?

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:04 UTC

Yes, but it's weak — mostly potassium and trace minerals, very little nitrogen, which is what your seedlings actually need. It won't burn them, it just won't fix the yellowing.

Diluted urine is the strongest nitrogen source you have on hand for that.

MILLARD2026-08-30 21:04 UTC

ok anything else they might need?

CLAUDE2026-08-30 21:05 UTC

Light is the big one — cherry tomato seedlings want 8+ hours of direct sun or they stretch thin and never recover their vigor. Querétaro sun is plenty, just get them outside or in the brightest window you have.

Air movement helps too. A fan or a daily hand-brush across the tops thickens the stems.

Beyond that: don't transplant into the 10L jugs until they have two or three sets of true leaves, and when you do, bury the stem deep — tomatoes root along the buried stem and you get a stronger plant. Make sure those jugs have drainage holes cut in the bottom.
