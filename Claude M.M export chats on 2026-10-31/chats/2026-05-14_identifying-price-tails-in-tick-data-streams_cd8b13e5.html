CLAUDE2026-05-14 16:23 UTC

The detection logic works but has a few structural weaknesses worth considering.

The window-start price as reference is the main issue. Extension is measured from whatever tick happens to sit at the left edge of the sliding window. As left advances tick-by-tick, the reference price jumps around. The same $0.50 spike can pass or fail the extension threshold depending on where left lands. What you actually want to measure is "how far did price travel to reach this extreme" — the window-start price is a proxy for that, not the thing itself.

A more robust approach would be to track the running min (for up-tails) or running max (for down-tails) within the window and measure extension from that, rather than from the single tick at position left. That way you're asking "did price move at least E from its lowest point to its highest point within T seconds, then retrace R?" — which is closer to the visual definition of a wick.

Fixed dollar thresholds don't adapt to volatility. $0.50 means very different things during Asian session vs. London open vs. NFP. An ATR-scaled or rolling-percentile threshold would catch proportionally significant tails regardless of regime.

Global argmax/argmin shadows smaller tails. If two spikes happen within the same window, only the larger one is visible. The smaller one can be permanently hidden.

These are refinements, not show-stoppers. The core idea — time-bounded extension-then-retracement on the raw tick stream, ignoring bar boundaries — is sound. The ZigZag work you've already done is actually a natural alternative framing for the same problem: a ZigZag leg is an extension, and the next leg is a retracement, with no arbitrary window-start reference to worry about.

CLAUDE2026-05-14 16:33 UTC

The feature set captures the average character of the lead-up but misses the shape of it. That's the main gap.

What's well covered: overall velocity, range, activity level, spread conditions, and whether the recent window is hotter than the longer-term window (the acceleration ratios). These establish "the market is moving and active."

What's missing:

Momentum exhaustion. A tail extremum is where a directional move dies. The script measures mean_abs_move and bias over each sub-window, but those are flat averages. Splitting a sub-window into first-half vs second-half and comparing (are tick-to-tick moves shrinking? is directional bias fading?) would capture deceleration — the signature of a move running out of steam.

Directional persistence. Consecutive same-sign tick-to-tick moves vs reversals. A strong extension has long runs; exhaustion shows increasing chop. A simple runs-ratio or longest-run metric would capture this.

Price position within recent range. How close is the current price to the edge of its N-second range? A reading near 1.0 (or 0.0) says "we're at an extreme" — which is literally what you're trying to detect.

Spread trajectory. Mean and max spread are captured, but whether spread is widening or narrowing matters. Liquidity thinning (widening spread) near a directional extreme is a classic exhaustion tell.

The existing features would likely separate tails from controls to some degree. The additions above would sharpen that separation specifically around the moment you care about — the peak, not just the run-up.
