Origins of property according to its critics

Created 2026-05-27 18:55 UTC · Last updated 2026-05-27 19:05 UTC · 8 messages
Original: claude.ai/chat/c6f6af55-f887-4c6f-92b6-82f685c43292
MILLARD2026-05-27 18:55 UTC

what did the foremost critics of ownership/property have to say about the origins of ownership?

CLAUDE2026-05-27 18:56 UTC

The dominant thread among the major critics is that property did not arise from anything resembling the Lockean fable of mixing labor with unowned nature, nor from any freely entered social contract. It arose from seizure — and was sanctified afterward by the very people who did the seizing.

Rousseau put it most memorably in the Second Discourse (1755): the first man to enclose a plot of ground, say "this is mine," and find people simple enough to believe him was the real founder of civil society. From that act flowed inequality, dependence, and law itself — which the rich subsequently invented to defend what they had grabbed.

Proudhon, in What Is Property? (1840), examined every classical justification — first occupation, labor, civil law — and demolished each. Occupation is arbitrary (why the first comer?). Labor justifies at most ownership of the improvement, not the land itself. Civil law is circular: property is whatever the law says it is, and the law was written by property-holders. "Property is theft" was a literal claim about origins, not a metaphor.

Marx, in the chapters on so-called primitive accumulation at the end of Capital Vol. 1, dismantled the bourgeois origin myth in which capitalists patiently saved while workers squandered. The actual origin of capitalist property was enclosure of the commons, expropriation of the peasantry, colonial plunder, and the slave trade. Capital, he wrote, comes dripping from head to foot, from every pore, with blood and dirt.

Engels, in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884), took the longer view: private property emerged with pastoralism and agriculture, the accumulation of surplus, and the need to transmit it through inheritance — which required subjugating women and breaking down older clan-based communal forms. Property, class, and patriarchy were a single historical event.

Kropotkin argued every increment of wealth is the deposit of countless generations' collective labor; what gets called private property is a state-protected monopoly on that common inheritance, originating in conquest and sustained by force.

Winstanley and the Diggers, more than a century before Rousseau, located the origin of English property specifically in the Norman Conquest. Landlords were the descendants of robbers; their titles were paper laid over a sword.

Henry George — less radical on movable goods — held that land in particular can have no honest origin in ownership, because no one made it; every land title traces back to a grant from a conqueror or king.

Stirner came at it sideways: he didn't mind ownership as such, but mocked the moral and legal robes draped over it. All property, stripped of its mystifications, is what someone has the power to take and hold; the "rights" attached afterward are spooks used to make seizure look sacred.

The convergent picture across the tradition: property begins as an act of force or enclosure that excludes others from what was previously common, and is then dressed in the language of natural right by jurists, theologians, and economists in the service of those who benefited from the original taking.

MILLARD2026-05-27 18:58 UTC

is the origin of ownership in seizure and deprivation pretty well recognized in the relevant fields?

CLAUDE2026-05-27 18:59 UTC

Broadly yes, with caveats — and the caveats are worth knowing because they sharpen rather than weaken the thesis.

In economic history, the empirical case is essentially settled. The Marxist account of primitive accumulation — enclosure of the English commons, expropriation of peasants, colonial plunder, slavery as foundational to early capital formation — is no longer a heterodox claim. Mainstream economic historians from Tawney through E.P. Thompson and into present work on the Atlantic economy treat the violent origins of modern property regimes as documented fact. You can disagree about what to make of it, but not whether it happened.

In legal scholarship, the violent origin is also widely conceded, even by relatively conservative property theorists. Carol Rose's much-cited "Possession as the Origin of Property" essentially admits that legal title is a story we tell about a prior taking. American property law courses now routinely teach Johnson v. M'Intosh and the Discovery Doctrine as the seizure-foundation of US land title; this used to be embarrassing and is now openly acknowledged. Critical legal studies and indigenous-law scholarship treat dispossession as the unquestioned starting point.

In anthropology, the picture is more contested but in a way that helps the critics rather than the defenders. The old Engels-style linear story (foraging → agriculture → surplus → private property → state) has been complicated by Sahlins, Clastres, and more recently Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything (2021), which shows pre-agricultural and seasonal societies with deliberate refusals of permanent property. The upshot isn't that property has innocent origins — it's that humans have repeatedly chosen against it, which makes its eventual imposition look even more like an imposition.

Where the seizure thesis is weakest, or rather least relevant: personal effects (the bow you made, the cloak you wove) plausibly emerge from something like labor-mixing without seizure of anyone. Almost no critic in the tradition you asked about denies this; Proudhon explicitly distinguished property from possession. The seizure account is about land, capital, and the kinds of ownership that produce dependence — and there it is the historical consensus, not a fringe position.

Who still resists it: libertarian philosophers in the Nozick/Epstein vein, who work in a normative register and bracket history; some neo-institutional economists (Douglass North and successors) who focus on property rights as productive institutions without dwelling on origins. Notably, Nozick himself conceded that if original acquisitions were unjust, his whole framework requires a principle of rectification he couldn't supply — which is closer to admitting the critics' premise than refuting it.

So: across history, law, anthropology, and settler-colonial studies, the seizure-and-deprivation account is the working consensus for the property forms that matter. The Lockean origin story survives mainly in political philosophy classrooms and libertarian polemics, not in the fields that study what actually happened.

so if I tell someone "You need to look for people who study these things and see if anyone supports your folktale grasp of ownership. In academia, the support for that Lockian concept is pretty much dead." it's fairly accurate?

CLAUDE2026-05-27 19:04 UTC

Directionally accurate, with one phrase I'd tighten.

"Pretty much dead in academia" overshoots if read literally. Locke is not dead — he's actively taught and defended in political philosophy, and there's a live cottage industry of Lockean and neo-Lockean theorists (Nozick's heirs, some natural-rights legal scholars, parts of the law-and-economics world). They exist, they publish, they have chairs.

What is essentially dead is the Lockean story as history — as an account of how property actually came to exist. Almost nobody in economic history, legal history, anthropology, or settler-colonial studies thinks property regimes originated by peaceful labor-mixing on unowned land. That folktale survives as civic mythology and in introductory libertarian arguments, not in the fields that study origins.

So the sharper version of your line would be something like: "If you want to know where ownership actually came from, look at what historians, anthropologists, and legal scholars who study it have found. The Lockean origin story is folk mythology — it survives as a normative argument among some philosophers, but as history it's been abandoned by the fields that study the question."

That version is defensible and hard to wriggle out of. The original phrasing invites a pedant to point at a living Lockean and say "see, not dead" — which dodges your actual point. Separating the normative Locke (still kicking) from the historical Locke (a fairy tale) closes that exit.
