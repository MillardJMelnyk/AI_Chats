The study that the CDC decided to suppress at Simpsonwood Georgia in 2000 showed what exactly.

CLAUDE2026-09-07 12:05 UTC

It wasn't suppressed — it was published in Pediatrics in 2003 (Verstraeten et al.) as a null result. What Simpsonwood was about is what the earlier cuts of that same analysis showed before it got there.

Thomas Verstraeten ran the Vaccine Safety Datalink looking at cumulative ethylmercury from thimerosal-containing vaccines. His first-generation analysis (late 1999) found a dose-response association between mercury exposure at one month of age and later neurodevelopmental diagnoses — tics, speech and language delay, ADD — with relative risks in the highest-exposure group running roughly 1.4 to 2.5 depending on the outcome. Autism showed up in some cuts and not others. That's what the ~50 people at the Norcross retreat center in June 2000 were looking at.

Over subsequent revisions the associations shrank and mostly vanished: more confounders controlled, a third HMO (Harvard Pilgrim) added, outcome definitions tightened. The real fight is over whether those changes were legitimate epidemiology — kids who get more vaccines see doctors more, so they get diagnosed more — or motivated analysis to bury a signal. The transcript itself came out under FOIA and contains the quotes people cite both ways. RFK Jr.'s 2005 "Deadly Immunity" piece made it famous; Salon retracted it in 2011.
