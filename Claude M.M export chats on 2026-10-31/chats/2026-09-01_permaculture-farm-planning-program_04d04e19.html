CLAUDE2026-09-01 15:41 UTC

Straight answer on the science first, because it determines the architecture

Most published companion-planting tables are folklore. If you build the suggestion engine on a pairwise "friend/enemy" matrix copied from Carrots Love Tomatoes, you will have built a superstition generator. That book contains no scientific evidence for its claims and most of the combinations have no stated mechanism, and extension services state plainly that companion planting is not a proven science.

What is proven is a smaller set of mechanisms. Build the engine on mechanisms with an evidence rating attached, not on plant-pair opinions.

Tier 1 — replicated field trials, real effect sizes

  • Push–pull (stimulo-deterrent). Maize intercropped with desmodium, bordered by Napier grass. Stem borer counts dropped to 8–20 per 40 plants versus 39–57 in controls, and maize yields rose to 4–7 t/ha versus 2–5 t/ha in controls. The mechanism is documented: desmodium volatiles repel the moths, Napier attracts them, and Napier does not support full larval development so most larvae die before adulthood. Desmodium also suppresses striga weed and fixes nitrogen. Note the honest counter-evidence: one Ethiopian study found landscape complexity mattered more than the intercrop, and push-pull effects on maize yield were overridden by landscape effects. Same system, different context, weaker result. Your database has to be able to record that.
  • Nitrogen fixation and resource complementarity. Annals of Botany work on the Three Sisters found the three crops use different nutrient foraging strategies, which is what makes them complementary — you would not pair three root crops at the same growth rate.
  • Trap cropping and natural-enemy habitat. Timing is a documented variable: planting the companion earlier than the cash crop gives better pest control. That means your data model needs a relative planting date offset field, not just "plant these together."
  • Allelopathy (negative). Black walnut juglone, sorghum-sudangrass residue, rye. These are hard exclusions and belong in the engine as blocking constraints.
  • Family-level rotation to break soilborne pathogens and root-specific pests. This is the single highest-value rule and it needs no species-pair data at all — just plant family plus bed history.

Tier 2 — mechanism sound, field results patchy

Aromatic masking and "appropriate/inappropriate landings" — any non-host groundcover reduces brassica pest landing rates, regardless of species. This is why "plant thyme with your cabbage" appears to work; the thyme is not special. Encode it as a functional rule (ground cover present, yes/no), not a species pair.

Tier 3 — folklore. Keep it in the DB, flagged, greyed out in the UI. Users want it. Don't let it drive constraint checks.

The data problem is bigger than the code problem

There is no complete free source for spacing and sowing depth. Current state:

  • OpenFarm — had exactly what you want, seed spacing and depth, watering, soil, companion plants, sun/shade, but the servers were shut down in April 2025 and the repo archived. The data dumps are still on GitHub. Scrape them.
  • Trefle — gives minimum temperature, root minimum depth, fertility requirement, 417,000+ species records. Taxonomy is good, cultivation data is thin, and the issue tracker shows years of unresolved bug reports. Treat it as a one-time import, not a live dependency.
  • Permapeople — permaculture-oriented, has an API and CSV export, best free fit for perennials and guild species.
  • USDA PLANTS — authoritative for family, growth habit, nitrogen fixation, root depth, drought tolerance. No row spacing.
  • University extension planting guides — this is where real spacing and depth numbers live. Not machine-readable.

Realistic assessment: you hand-curate the first 100–150 species from extension guides, at maybe 4 minutes each. Ten hours of typing. Everything after that is bulk-imported and cleaned. Plan the schema so a human can fill it fast, because a human is going to.

Proposed structure

Stack: Python + SQLite + browser front end (SVG canvas). Rationale: your Python is solid, SQLite means no server and a single portable file, and drag-to-draw plot editing is a fraction of the work in SVG compared to any desktop toolkit.

Core tables

  • species — botanical name, common names, family, in-row spacing, between-row spacing, sowing depth, mature height, mature spread, root depth, root type, sun requirement, water need, pH range, hardiness range, days to maturity, perennial flag, N-fixer flag, layer (canopy / sub-canopy / shrub / herb / groundcover / root / vine).
  • bed — farm ID, geometry (polygon), type (annual bed / orchard / food forest / pasture / water), soil notes, sun exposure.
  • planting — bed ID, species ID, quantity, planted date, spacing override.
  • interaction — subject (species or family or functional group), object, effect type (repels / attracts-enemy / traps / fixes-N / allelopathic / shades / competes / hosts-shared-pest), mechanism text, evidence tier 1–3, source citation, effect size, required density, required spatial arrangement, planting offset in days.
  • bed_history — for rotation checking.
  • guild_template — named packages: Three Sisters, push-pull, apple guild, etc.

The critical design choice is that interaction keys on family and functional group as well as species. If it only keys species-to-species, the table needs millions of rows and you'll never fill it.

Engine outputs, in order of value

  1. Hard conflicts — allelopathy, shared soilborne pathogen, rotation violation.
  2. Spacing/geometry — given bed dimensions and species, compute plant count, row layout, and flag overcrowding. Show sowing depth per species.
  3. Shading — tall species placed on the sun side of short ones.
  4. Suggestions — N-fixers, insectary plants, trap crops, each shown with its evidence tier and the condition it depends on.

Build order

  1. Schema + seed 30 species by hand. Prove the data model before drawing anything.
  2. Plot editor — farm boundary, scale, north, draw and dimension beds.
  3. Assign species to beds; spacing calculator; depth display.
  4. Rules engine v1 — rotation and allelopathy conflicts only.
  5. Suggestion engine — mechanism-based, evidence-tiered.
  6. Guild templates.
  7. Sun/shade and layering.
  8. Calendar tied to your frost dates.
  9. Water and contour. This is where the housing add-on will eventually hook in, since siting a building and siting swales are the same problem. Deferred.

Before I write anything, five questions

  1. Where is the farm — Querétaro, or somewhere else? Drives the species list, frost dates, and whether water design is the dominant constraint.
  2. How big — house lot, or hectares? Determines whether you need contour and terrain or just a flat scaled rectangle.
  3. Just for you locally, or a web app other people will use?
  4. Metric?
  5. Blank scaled canvas, or satellite imagery underlay with real coordinates?

Answer those and I'll give you the exact change plan for step 1. No code until GO.

i mean its notproven cause its not an area of scientific interest right?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 15:44 UTC

Partly.

Funding bias is real — research money is tight and goes to commercial agriculture, not home gardens, so most gardener-facing pairings have simply never been trialled.

But "no scientific interest" is false at the mechanism level. Intercropping, biological control, allelopathy, trap cropping and agroecology are enormous, well-funded fields with thousands of papers. Push-pull alone got two decades of institutional funding. The science exists; it just isn't filed under the phrase "companion planting," which is why the popular tables and the literature never meet.

Two other reasons the folk pairs stay untested, both structural rather than disinterest:

Combinatorics. 200 species gives ~20,000 pairs, and each one needs testing across soil, climate, and pest pressure. Nobody can fund that, ever.

Signal-to-noise. These effects are small next to weather and site variation. Detecting them needs replication on a scale nobody will pay for to answer whether basil helps tomatoes.

And the part that cuts against you: some pairs have been tested and failed. That's a different state from untested, and it matters for the build.

So the schema needs four evidence states, not three:

  1. Supported by trials
  2. Mechanism plausible, untested
  3. Tested, no effect found
  4. Folklore, no mechanism proposed

Collapsing 2 and 3 into "unproven" throws away the most useful information you have. State 3 is what stops a user wasting a bed.

Right — and that changes the timeline, not the watering advice.

You're planting into the descending half of the year. Rough schedule from where that seedling is: transplant late September, first flowers early to mid November, first ripe cherry tomatoes December. That's the worst window. Tomatoes stop setting fruit reliably when nights drop below about 13°C, and Querétaro nights are heading there through November and December. Three things that decide whether you get fruit:

Frost. Querétaro gets occasional light frost December through February, not every year. One night is enough to end the plant. Keep the jugs somewhere you can move them, or have a sheet ready.

Night warmth. Against a south-facing wall that holds daytime heat. This is worth more than anything you can buy.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:00 UTC

will the plants survive till the summer to fruit again or only once?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:00 UTC

Yes, it can survive — tomatoes are tender perennials, not annuals. They're grown as annuals only because frost kills them. Your winter is mild enough that a protected plant on a warm wall will very likely make it through to spring and fruit again.

Two conditions decide it:

Variety type. Indeterminate varieties keep growing from the tip indefinitely and will keep producing for years. Determinate ones set one main flush, then decline and die back regardless of what you do. Most cherry tomatoes are indeterminate, so you're probably fine, but check the seed packet — this is the single fact that settles the question.

Frost. One night below zero ends it. That's the only real threat.

The catch: a second-year plant in a 10L jug performs worse than a fresh one. The root ball is bound, the stem goes woody and less productive, and a full year of accumulated leaf disease and pests carries over. You'd be nursing a tired plant through winter to get a mediocre spring out of it.

The better move, and it costs you nothing: take cuttings. Snap off a 15cm sucker, put it in water or damp medium, and it roots in one to two weeks. Tomatoes root from cuttings almost aggressively. So in December, take three or four cuttings from your plant, keep them small and sheltered over winter, and pot them up in February. You get genetically identical plants with brand-new root systems, timed exactly for the March–May fruiting window, for free — and you're not gambling the whole thing on one frost night.

Keep the mother plant alive as backup if you like. Just don't rely on it being the producer.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:03 UTC

will th suckers drop in quality as it ages?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:03 UTC

No. Cuttings don't age.

A rooted sucker is a fresh plant — new root system, juvenile growth, full vigour. Growers propagate tomatoes this way through multiple cycles without decline. There's no clock inside the cutting counting the mother plant's age.

What does degrade, and gets confused for aging:

Disease carryover. This is the real one. Anything systemic in the mother — viruses especially, since they move through the plant's tissue — goes into the cutting intact. Take cuttings from a plant that's visibly clean, not from one that's already tired and spotty. If your December plant looks rough, don't propagate it.

Cutting quality at the moment you take it. A sucker from a starved, root-bound, end-of-season plant roots slower and starts weaker than one from a healthy plant. That's the cutting's condition, not inherited age. It catches up once it's rooted and fed.

Cumulative selection. If you always take the most convenient sucker rather than the best one, over many cycles you're propagating from mediocre wood. Take from vigorous mid-plant growth, not the exhausted bottom.

So the plan holds: take your cuttings in December while the plant is still in decent shape rather than waiting until it's struggling in February. Cleaner material, better start.

Worth knowing for later — a cherry tomato you like can be kept going indefinitely this way, which matters if it turns out to be a hybrid whose saved seed won't come true.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:03 UTC

how long can i save cuttings for?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:03 UTC

Two different questions in there, so both:

Before rooting — days, not weeks. A detached tomato sucker has no roots and wilts fast. Same day is best. In water on a shaded windowsill you have maybe two to three days before it declines badly. There's no dormancy to exploit — tomatoes don't have a resting state you can store them in, unlike woody cuttings you can hold cold.

After rooting — as long as you keep it growing. This is the useful answer for your December-to-February plan. A rooted cutting is just a small plant; hold it in a small pot, bright and frost-free, water sparingly, and eight to ten weeks is comfortable. Keep the pot small on purpose — it restricts growth so the plant stays compact instead of turning into a leggy mess before you can plant it out.

Two things while holding:

Pinch the growing tip if it starts stretching. Forces it to bush rather than sprint upward.

Don't feed much. You want it ticking over, not growing. Feeding a held cutting just gives you a bigger plant to manage in a pot that can't support it.

If February slips, take a cutting from the cutting. That resets the clock indefinitely, and since cuttings don't inherit age, you can chain them as long as you like.

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:05 UTC

I can't play video, but here are two that cover it.

The whole procedure, since it's short:

Find the sucker — the shoot growing at a 45-degree angle in the "armpit" between the main stem and a leaf branch. Not the main vertical stalk, not the horizontal leaf branch. Take one 15–20cm long. Too small is weak, too big goes into shock.

Strip the lower leaves so the bottom of the stem is bare. In water, keep the remaining leaves clear of the surface or they rot.

Then either water or soil. Water roots in about a week and lets you watch progress; soil gives stronger roots and skips the transplant step, so it's the better choice for you. Bury it up to the first branch — tomatoes root all along the stem, so deeper means faster root development. Keep the soil evenly moist, warm and bright. Don't panic if the leaves droop or crisp; it rebounds once roots form, but only if the soil stays moist. Check for roots after a week or two by lifting it gently; if there are none but it looks healthy, put it back and wait.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:06 UTC

can you find me a video on soil mix for tomatoes? Also do most plants prefer simalar soil mixes like humus carbon and sand mixtures?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:06 UTC

The standard container recipe, so you can judge the videos against it: 2 parts coco coir or peat for moisture retention, 1 part perlite for drainage and aeration, 1 part compost or worm castings for nutrients and microbes. Tomatoes want pH 6.0–6.6.

Two things that apply directly to you. Don't use garden soil in pots — it's heavy, compacts more with every watering cycle, loses oxygen and water-holding capacity, and carries pathogens and pests. And watch the bark: excess pine bark pushes pH acidic and consumes extra nitrogen. Your current mix is bark-heavy, which is likely part of why that seedling looks lean.

On your second question: no, and the reason matters for the planner.

There isn't one universal mix, but the structure is close to universal while the chemistry is not. Nearly every container plant wants the same three functions — something to hold water, something to hold air, something to hold nutrients — which is why the coir/perlite/compost skeleton works for most vegetables. What changes between species is the dial settings:

pH. The real divider. Blueberries want 4.5–5.5, most vegetables 6.0–7.0, asparagus and brassicas tolerate 7.0+. Put a blueberry in your tomato mix and it starves surrounded by nutrients it can't take up.

Drainage. Mediterranean herbs — rosemary, thyme, lavender — rot in a mix tomatoes thrive in. They want far more sand or grit and far less organic matter. Your bark-heavy mix would actually suit them better than it suits the tomato.

Fertility. Tomatoes are heavy feeders. Carrots in rich mix fork and split. Legumes fix their own nitrogen and don't want it supplied.

Depth. Carrots and parsnips need deep loose medium free of stones; lettuce is happy in 15cm.

Your "humus, carbon and sand" framing is sound as a skeleton — organic matter, structure, drainage — you just adjust the ratios per plant.

For the planner, this is a schema point: soil isn't a per-species free-text field. It's four numeric fields — pH range, drainage requirement, fertility demand, minimum rooting depth — and the engine flags when you've put a rosemary and a tomato in the same bed. That's a genuine mechanism-based conflict rule, unlike most of the companion folklore.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:09 UTC

but i can make the soil the same and then fertilize and adjust soil after planting no?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:09 UTC

Yes for fertility. No for structure. pH sits in between.

Fertility — adjust freely after planting. This is what liquid feed is for. Same base mix for everything, then feed tomatoes heavily, feed carrots almost nothing. Correct within days. You're right, and this is most of what people mean by "different soil needs."

Structure — you cannot fix after planting. Once the plant is in and rooted, you can't change how much perlite or sand is in the mix. You'd have to bare-root and repot it, which sets the plant back weeks. If you put rosemary in a moisture-retentive tomato mix, it sits wet at the root all winter and rots, and there is no amendment you can add on top to fix that. This is the one that has to be right at mixing time.

pH — technically adjustable, practically slow. Sulfur to lower it, lime to raise it, both take weeks to months to move because they work through microbial and dissolution processes, not instantly. In a small container you can shift it faster than in ground, but you're still waiting a month while the plant sits in the wrong range. Cheaper to build it right.

So the practical rule, which is a real simplification worth taking:

Make one base mix. Vary drainage at mixing time. Vary everything else after.

Base: coir + compost + perlite. For the wet-hating plants — rosemary, thyme, lavender, sage — take a scoop of that base and cut in extra sand or grit before potting. That's one extra step at potting time, not a second recipe.

The exception you'll hit eventually is blueberries, which need pH 4.5–5.5 and genuinely need their own acidic mix from the start. Everything else in a normal vegetable garden lives in the 6.0–7.0 window and your one base mix covers it.

For your Ciel jugs right now: one mix, feed differently. You're overthinking it.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:10 UTC

what about tomoatoes? its more neutral, how do i know the ph of my soil wityhou a test?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:10 UTC

Tomatoes want 6.0–6.6. Slightly acidic, not neutral.

Without a test, you don't measure pH — you infer it from what you mixed. That's the real answer, and it works because container mix is made from known ingredients rather than found in the ground:

  • Peat moss — acidic, roughly 3.5–4.5
  • Coco coir — near neutral, roughly 5.5–6.8
  • Compost / worm castings — roughly 6.0–7.0
  • Perlite, sand — inert, no effect
  • Pine bark — acidic, and it also ties up nitrogen

So a coir + compost + perlite mix lands in the low-to-mid 6s on its own. That's tomato range without doing anything. A peat-heavy mix runs low and usually has lime added by the manufacturer to correct it. Your current bark-heavy medium is the one that's likely sitting below where you want it.

The things people suggest instead don't work. The vinegar-and-baking-soda kitchen test only detects extremes — strongly alkaline or strongly acidic — and tells you nothing in the 5.5 to 7.5 band, which is the entire range you care about. Weed indicator species are for ground soil and are weak evidence even there.

What the plant tells you, which is genuine but late: interveinal yellowing on new growth while old leaves stay green means iron lockout, which in a container almost always means pH too high. Purple leaf undersides after true leaves are established point to phosphorus lockout, which usually means pH too low or soil too cold. Both show up weeks after the damage started.

Honestly though, strips cost about what a bag of soil does and end the guessing permanently:

Any garden centre in Querétaro carries these, or Amazon MX. Mix a soil sample with distilled water, dip, compare to the chart.

Skip the cheap two-prong analog probes — the ones you stick in the ground with a needle dial. They're notoriously inaccurate and mostly read moisture regardless of what the pH scale says.

For your situation: mix coir, compost and perlite and you're in range by default. Test only if the plant starts showing symptoms.

MILLARD2026-09-01 16:14 UTC

should i add a small amount of wood ash, or bbq ash?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:14 UTC

No to BBQ ash. Probably no to wood ash in your containers.

BBQ ash — never, if it's briquettes. They contain binders, fillers and sometimes lighter-fluid residue, and the ash carries all of it. Same for anything painted, treated, glued, or plywood. Only ash from clean untreated hardwood is even a candidate.

Wood ash — right nutrients, wrong direction. It's genuinely useful stuff: high potassium, which tomatoes want for fruiting, plus calcium, which helps against blossom end rot. But it's strongly alkaline. You're at 6.0–6.6 and want to stay there. Ash pushes you up, toward the iron lockout I mentioned.

The container problem makes it worse. In ground soil, ash gets diluted into a huge buffered volume and a mistake is forgiving. In a 10L jug there's nothing to buffer it — a couple of tablespoons can swing pH a full point and the salts have nowhere to leach to. Ash is also alkaline enough to burn roots on direct contact.

If you want to use it anyway, the safe route is to put it in compost rather than in the pot. It mellows there, gets buffered, and comes to you already integrated at a sane concentration. That's where ash belongs.

If what you're actually after is potassium for fruiting, use a tomato feed with a high K number, or banana peels in the compost. Both give you the potassium without moving pH the wrong way.

Slightly low is not a problem worth treating. Tomatoes want 6.0–6.6. Bark might have you at 5.5–6.0. At 5.5 they grow fine. There's nothing to correct — you'd be spending risk to buy nothing.

If you do want to raise it, garden lime is the tool, not ash. Lime is calibrated, sold with application rates, buffers rather than spikes, and supplies calcium without potassium. Ash is potassium-heavy, dose-uncertain, and alkaline enough to burn roots. Using ash to adjust pH is using a shotgun where you need a dropper.

But here's the thing that makes this moot: your seedling isn't going to stay in that bark mix. It moves into a Ciel jug at two sets of true leaves, and that's when you make a fresh coir + compost + perlite mix that sits in the low 6s by itself. The pH question solves itself at transplant, for free, with no amendment and no risk.

So: don't add anything now. Fix it by mixing properly at transplant.

what should i feed my plants after 2 true leaves form?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:16 UTC

Quarter-strength balanced liquid feed, once a week.

Balanced means roughly equal N-P-K — something like 10-10-10 or 20-20-20, diluted to a quarter of what the label says. A seedling with two true leaves has a tiny root system and full-strength feed burns it.

Then step it up as it grows:

Two true leaves to transplant — quarter strength weekly. If you transplant into a compost-based mix, the compost feeds it and you can stop entirely for two or three weeks.

After transplant, vegetative growth — half strength weekly, still balanced. Some people push nitrogen here. Don't. High nitrogen gives you a big leafy plant that flowers late, which you can't afford given your timeline.

At first flowers — switch to high potassium. This is the real change. A tomato feed with a K number much higher than the N, or the classic tomato fertiliser you'll find locally. Full strength, weekly.

Two things specific to your setup:

Calcium matters more in containers. Blossom end rot is a calcium delivery failure, and it's usually caused by inconsistent watering rather than a lack of calcium in the soil. Get one that includes calcium, and water consistently — that does more than the feed does.

Liquid, not granular slow-release. In a 10L jug you want control, and you want to be able to stop if you see burn. Slow-release commits you.

One rule that overrides all of the above: if the leaves are dark green and the plant is growing well, don't increase anything. Overfeeding container tomatoes is far more common than underfeeding, and it costs you fruit.

i want to make my own how can I?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:17 UTC

The honest constraint first: homemade feeds have unknown NPK. You can't dose them precisely, so the rule is weak and frequent rather than strong and occasional. That's fine for a few jugs.

You need two feeds, matching the two stages.

Nitrogen feed, for growth

Alfalfa is your best option in Mexico — it's everywhere as fodder and costs almost nothing. Soak a couple of handfuls of alfalfa pellets in 5L of water for 3–4 days, strain, dilute that roughly 1:3, use weekly. Roughly 3-1-2, plus it contains triacontanol, a natural growth stimulant. It smells bad for the first few days. Outdoors only.

The free alternative is urine. It's a genuine fertiliser, roughly 11-1-2, sterile when fresh from a healthy person, and it's been studied properly. Dilute 1:10 for containers — 1:15 if you're nervous — use fresh, don't store it, don't get it on the leaves. Weekly at most. Nothing you make will beat it for nitrogen and it costs nothing.

Potassium feed, for flowering and fruit

Comfrey tea is the classic — leaves packed in a bucket, water added, three weeks, dilute 1:10. If you can't find comfrey in Querétaro, banana peels do the same job more slowly: chop 4–5 peels into 2L of water, steep a week, strain, use undiluted.

Base fertility and microbes

Worm castings, and this is the one worth actually building. A worm bin costs nothing, eats your kitchen scraps, and gives you both castings to mix into the potting medium and liquid to feed with. Steep a cup of castings in 4L of water overnight for the liquid. In your climate a bin runs year-round. If you're going to invest effort in one homemade input, make it this one.

Calcium

Skip eggshells crushed into soil — they take years to break down and do nothing on your timescale. If you want eggshell calcium, dissolve the shells in vinegar until fizzing stops, then dilute that liquid heavily, around 1:200. That gives you calcium acetate, which is actually plant-available. Otherwise just buy agricultural gypsum, which is cheap and supplies calcium without touching pH.

What this looks like in practice

Two true leaves to transplant: worm casting tea, very weak, weekly. Nothing stronger.

After transplant: alfalfa or diluted urine weekly.

From first flowers: switch to comfrey or banana, stop the nitrogen.

Throughout: work castings into the mix at potting time and you'll need less liquid feed at every stage.

Watch for the failure mode — homemade feeds skew heavily to nitrogen, because that's what's easy to make. A plant that's dark green, huge and leafy with no flowers means you overdid the nitrogen. If that happens, stop feeding entirely for two weeks and go straight to potassium.

can i use the 10l just placed inside one another to make a temp worm bin?

CLAUDE2026-09-01 16:20 UTC

Yes. That's the standard stacked-bin design, just smaller.

How it works: drill holes through the bottom of the upper jug so worms can migrate up through them, and set it inside the lower jug. Bedding and food go in the top. The lower jug catches leachate and stays sealed. When the top is full of castings, you add a third jug on top with fresh food, and the worms migrate up into it over a few weeks, leaving you finished castings below with no sorting by hand.

Drilling:
- Migration holes in the bottom of the upper jug — 6mm, twenty or so
- Air holes around the upper sides near the top — 3mm, plenty of them
- Nothing in the lower jug. It's the sump.

Two things that will bite you at this size:

Volume. 10L is small for a worm bin. Start with a quarter kilo of worms, not a full kilo, and feed lightly. An overloaded small bin goes anaerobic and stinks within days. If you get half a kilo, split it across two stacks.

Drying out. High surface area to volume, plus your dry season starting now. Bedding should feel like a wrung-out sponge, and in a container that small you'll be checking it every few days rather than weekly. Keep a lid on, in shade.

Setup: fill the upper jug about two-thirds with damp shredded cardboard or coco coir, add worms, let them settle a day before feeding, then bury small amounts of scraps in one corner at a time. No citrus, no onion, no meat, no dairy.

The leachate in the bottom jug is not the same thing as worm tea — it's runoff and can be anaerobic. Dilute it heavily, around 1:10, or just tip it out. The valuable product is the castings.

Cut the jugs so the upper one nests without sitting flat on the bottom of the lower one; you want an air gap of a few centimetres for drainage.

Two corrections to the mechanics:

The top jug goes on from day one, empty, as a lid. Not for the worms — for moisture and light. An open bin in Querétaro's dry season loses water fast, and worms flee light. You want it covered from the start. It just sits there empty until migration time, then you fill it.

Contact is what makes migration work. The bottom of the fresh jug has to physically rest on the surface of the castings below, not hover above it. Worms crawl, they don't jump. If there's an air gap they stay put. So when you load the top jug, press it down until it's touching.

One clarification on your middle jug — the bedding and the food are different things. Cardboard and coir are bedding: that's the habitat and the carbon, and it fills most of the volume. Food scraps are a small amount buried in one corner. New people load a bin with food and almost no bedding, and it goes anaerobic and sour. Ratio is roughly 3:1 bedding to food by volume.

Migration takes four to six weeks. Some stragglers stay below — don't wait for a perfect migration, just harvest and pick out the few you see.

When you rotate: the emptied castings jug becomes your new top jug. Three jugs cycling indefinitely.

Right. Empty means nothing to eat and nothing to hide in, so they won't move up. A few will wander through the holes, find bare plastic, and go back down. That's it.

Two things make them stay put rather than migrate:

No cover. Worms are photosensitive and want to be buried. An empty jug is exposed, dry plastic — the opposite of what they'll settle in.
