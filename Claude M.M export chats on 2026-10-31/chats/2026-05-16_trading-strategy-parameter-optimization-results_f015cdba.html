CLAUDE2026-05-16 13:12 UTC

Two separate problems. Thinking through both.

Better wick identification

The current system scores each tick independently against static thresholds. Things that could sharpen detection:

Tick-rate acceleration. Not just tick count in the window — the derivative of tick arrival rate. Wicks come with a burst. A sudden spike in ticks-per-100ms relative to the prior second is a strong filter. Calm markets don't wick.

Displacement-to-retracement ratio. Within the T_ms window, measure max displacement from the window's starting price versus where price is now. A wick that ran 80 pips and retraced 60 looks very different from a sustained 80-pip move. High retracement ratio = wick is already forming. Low ratio = it's just a fast move, not a wick yet.

Consecutive same-direction tick runs. The thrust phase of a wick produces long runs of ticks in one direction — 8, 10, 15 in a row. Then the reversal phase breaks the run. Longest-run and current-run-length are cheap to compute and directly characterize wick structure.

Bid-ask spread expansion. If your tick data carries bid and ask, spread widening is a near-universal wick signature. It precedes or accompanies the thrust.

Better signal price (closer to extrema)

This is the bigger lever for net P&L. Right now you fire the moment rules pass. That moment could be mid-thrust — before the tip.

Two-phase detection. Phase A: rules fire, detector enters a primed state for that direction. Phase B: wait for the first reversal evidence — price ticks back toward the expected profit direction, or tick-rate decelerates, or a same-direction run breaks. Signal price comes from Phase B, not Phase A. This catches the turn instead of the thrust.

Deceleration trigger. Once primed, watch tick-to-tick displacement size. During the thrust, displacements are large and one-directional. Near the tip, they shrink or start alternating direction. First tick where displacement drops below some fraction of the recent average = fire.

Micro-retracement entry. Simpler variant: once primed, don't fire until price retraces N pips (or N ticks) back from the running extremum. You sacrifice a few pips off the theoretical best but you confirm the turn is real. Trades that never retrace — the wick just kept going — get filtered out, which also helps winner rate.

Post-hoc extremum tracking. For tuning, not live use: after each signal, track where the actual extremum landed relative to your signal price. Build a distribution of "how many pips early/late." That distribution tells you how much room there is and whether the two-phase approach is calibrated correctly.

The two-phase approach is probably the highest-impact single change. It attacks both problems — the primed state is a better wick filter (rules matched and reversal confirmed), and the signal price is mechanically closer to the tip.

What's actually NOT covered, now that I've read the code:

One thing I notice: the feature window is split at the tick-count midpoint (mid = diffs.size // 2), not at the temporal midpoint or at the extremum position. In a wick where ticks cluster heavily during the thrust, the tick-count midpoint could land well past the temporal midpoint of the window. That means decel_ratio and spread_trajectory are measuring "first half of ticks vs second half of ticks," not "first half of time vs second half of time." That would be a more precise reversal-timing signal than the whole-window chop ratio, and could help tighten signal price toward the actual extremum.

The tail_id == 0 exclusion removes ticks from the control candidate pool if they fall anywhere inside a detected tail's window — not just the anchor tick, but every tick from window_start_idx through window_end_idx. That's a hard gate before matching even starts.

The audit is pointing out that an alternative design would keep those ticks eligible and let the overlap weighting downweight them instead. That's a softer approach — more candidates, statistically managed rather than binary-excluded.

My position: the hard exclusion is correct here. A tick sitting mid-wick — even if it's not the anchor — has wick-shaped features. Letting it into the control pool and then weighting it down still lets it contribute to the control distribution. That contaminates the "what does non-wick look like" baseline, which is exactly what the tail-vs-control comparison needs to be clean.
