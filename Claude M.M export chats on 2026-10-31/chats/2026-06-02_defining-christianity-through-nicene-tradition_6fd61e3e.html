CLAUDE2026-06-02 15:52 UTC

Here's a tightened version:

When I say "Christianity," I mean the traditions that derive their theological authority, doctrinal identity, and institutional legitimacy from the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) and subsequent ecumenical councils — including Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Protestant denominations, all of which accept the Nicene Creed as a foundational confession.

MILLARD2026-06-02 15:53 UTC

CLAUDE2026-06-02 15:53 UTC

Imperial auspices — convened and enforced under Roman imperial authority, beginning with Constantine.

MILLARD2026-06-02 15:54 UTC

CLAUDE2026-06-02 15:54 UTC

Yes — Nicaea was convened by Constantine directly. The later councils varied, but the pattern was set: ecumenical councils required imperial summons and, often, imperial enforcement of their outcomes.

MILLARD2026-06-02 15:59 UTC

what's the relationship between the so-called "Church Fathers" and those councils?

CLAUDE2026-06-02 15:59 UTC

The Church Fathers were the theological architects whose writings, debates, and rulings shaped the doctrinal positions the councils then codified and enforced. Many were direct participants — Athanasius at Nicaea being the obvious example. The councils didn't generate the theology; they selected winners from pre-existing disputes among the Fathers and made imperial law out of the result.

Later councils drew additionally on:

MILLARD2026-06-02 16:01 UTC

And what is the documented linkage between the "Church Fathers" and the Apostles? Notice I said documented -- not claimed after the fact.

CLAUDE2026-06-02 16:01 UTC

There isn't one. The linkage is entirely claimed, not documented. The earliest verifiable patristic writings postdate the apostolic period by decades at minimum, and the chain of "apostolic succession" is a retroactive construction — asserted most aggressively by Irenaeus precisely because it was contested. No contemporaneous documentation connects any Church Father directly to any apostle.

MILLARD2026-06-02 16:05 UTC

where/when was the decision made/finalized that the Jewish writings should be included in the "canon"?

CLAUDE2026-06-02 16:05 UTC

At the councils themselves — the canon as a fixed list was effectively finalized at the Synod of Hippo (393 CE) and ratified at Carthage (397 CE), both held under the same imperial-ecclesiastical framework. The inclusion of the Jewish scriptures (as "Old Testament") was never seriously contested by that point; the disputes were over which Jewish texts qualified and how they related to the new material. The decision was made by the same conciliar apparatus, not by any Jewish authority.

MILLARD2026-06-02 16:07 UTC

CLAUDE2026-06-02 16:07 UTC

Not cleanly. The Christian Old Testament derives largely from the Septuagint — the Greek translation — not the Hebrew Tanakh directly. This matters because the Septuagint includes additional books (the Apocrypha/Deuterocanonicals) that the Hebrew canon excludes. So the Christian Old Testament is a repackaged, reordered, Greek-mediated version of Jewish scripture, with additions, interpreted through a Christological lens — a fundamentally different thing presented as the same thing.

MILLARD2026-06-02 17:18 UTC

Dig into the Hebrew supporting "it has Yahweh himself claiming he commanded the offering of firstborns".

CLAUDE2026-06-02 17:18 UTC

Here's what the Hebrew actually says:

Ezekiel 20:25 — וְגַם־אֲנִי נָתַתִּי לָהֶם חֻקִּים לֹא טוֹבִים וּמִשְׁפָּטִים לֹא יִחְיוּ בָּהֶם

  • וְגַם־אֲנִי — "and also I" — subject is unambiguously Yahweh, first person
  • נָתַתִּי — "I gave" — qal perfect, active, no passive escape route
  • חֻקִּים לֹא טוֹבִים — "statutes not good" — the word is chuqqim, the standard word for divine law/ordinance throughout the Torah
  • מִשְׁפָּטִים לֹא יִחְיוּ בָּהֶם — "ordinances by which they could not live"

Ezekiel 20:26 — וָאֲטַמֵּא אֹותָם בְּמַתְּנֹותָם בְּהַעֲבִיר כָּל־פֶּטֶר רָחַם

  • וָאֲטַמֵּא — "and I made unclean / I defiled" — again first person active, Yahweh speaking
  • בְּהַעֲבִיר כָּל־פֶּטֶר רָחַם — "in causing to pass through every one that opens the womb" — ha'avir is the standard verb for passing children through fire in sacrifice contexts
  • לְמַעַן אֲשַׁמֵּם — "in order that I might devastate/horrify them"

The Hebrew admits no grammatical wriggle room. The subject of both verbs — giving the bad laws, defiling them through firstborn sacrifice — is Yahweh, stated explicitly in first person. The translations that soften it to "I let them defile themselves" are inserting a causative cushion that isn't in the Hebrew. The verb וָאֲטַמֵּא is not permissive — it's active.

MILLARD2026-06-02 17:20 UTC

Do rabinnical authorities admit this?

CLAUDE2026-06-02 17:20 UTC

Some do, reluctantly. The mainstream rabbinic response has been evasion rather than admission — the dominant move is to repoint or reread ha'avir as non-sacrificial ("passing through" as dedication, not immolation), or to read verse 25 as Yahweh withdrawing protection and allowing the people to follow bad laws rather than issuing them.

But several honest engagements exist:

Rashi acknowledges the difficulty of verse 25 and essentially concedes Yahweh gave defective laws as punishment — he just redirects the purpose to chastisement rather than admission of commanded sacrifice.

Maimonides, in the Guide for the Perplexed, takes a different evasion — arguing that sacrificial law itself was a concession to pagan habit, not intrinsically commanded. This implicitly concedes that the sacrificial system wasn't Yahweh's ideal, which creates its own problems.

Modern Orthodox scholars like Moshe Greenberg, who wrote the definitive critical commentary on Ezekiel, acknowledges the plain sense of the Hebrew is that Yahweh gave laws producing death, and that the text is genuinely theologically disturbing. He doesn't fully resolve it.

The honest bottom line: no mainstream rabbinic authority flatly says "yes, the text means Yahweh commanded child sacrifice." But the evasions themselves confirm the plain reading is the problem they're evading. If the text were clearly saying something else, no evasion would be necessary.
