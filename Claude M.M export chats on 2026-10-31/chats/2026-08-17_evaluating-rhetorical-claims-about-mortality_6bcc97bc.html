CLAUDE2026-08-17 20:08 UTC

Essentially true as stated, and it's not an exaggeration for the period it refers to.

Sweden kept preschools and schools open for ages 1–16 — 1,951,905 children as of end-2019. The Karolinska/NEJM study covering March–June 2020, the first wave with schools open, found 15 of those children admitted to ICU with COVID or MIS-C — about 1 in 130,000 — and no child with COVID died. All-cause deaths in that age group were 65 in the four pre-COVID months versus 69 in the four COVID months, so no hidden signal either.

Over the entire pandemic, not just that window: 21 people under 20 in Sweden died of COVID, mostly with underlying conditions — about 1% of deaths in that age group. So "none" is off by roughly 21 out of ~2 million across three years, and that count includes 17–19-year-olds who were on remote learning, not the 1–16 school cohort.

Two things the meme skips: upper secondary schools (16–19) were switched to distance learning, so it wasn't the whole system; and child mortality was never the main argument for closing schools — transmission to adults was. Sweden's teacher data cuts at that too — schoolteachers had a lower ICU risk than other occupations. The counterargument is that Sweden's overall death toll, concentrated in the elderly and care homes, was high relative to Norway and Finland, and critics attribute that to the broader light-touch approach rather than to schools specifically.
