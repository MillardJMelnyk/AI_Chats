CLAUDE2026-10-26 16:50 UTC

Monday, October 26, 2026, 10:50 AM CST — Thorium and Uranium are radioactive, and Bismuth is too, though so slowly (half-life about a billion times the age of the universe) that it's effectively stable. Several others, such as Potassium, Rubidium, and Samarium, have naturally radioactive isotopes, but they're normally counted as stable elements.

CLAUDE2026-10-26 16:54 UTC

Their central claim — that these elements are deliberate, undeclared ingredients pointing to nanotechnology — doesn't survive their own methods and numbers (Highly likely).

  1. Trace impurities aren't "undeclared ingredients." ICP-MS finds trace metals in virtually any injectable, saline, or water, and the regulatory question is whether they exceed safety limits, not whether they're present. Their own numbers are tiny: the highest arsenic they report, 28 µg/L, works out to about 0.014 µg per 0.5 mL dose, roughly a thousand times below the ICH Q3D daily limit for injectables (15 µg).

  2. No controls for the obvious contamination sources. They drew samples with a 5 mL Hamilton syringe, puncturing each rubber septum, which means a stainless steel needle through rubber into borosilicate glass. That combination fits their most frequent hits almost exactly: chromium in 100% of samples, boron and gallium in 88%. Their only blanks were ultra-pure water in plastic tubes, so they never ran saline or a non-COVID injectable from the same vial type through the same handling.

  3. Classic false signals in a salt-heavy sample. Chloride plus argon plasma produces a molecule at mass 75 that mimics arsenic, and carbon plus argon produces one at mass 52 that mimics chromium. Those two artifacts sit right behind their two headline "heavy metals." They used helium collision mode, which removes these, only for some elements, without saying which.

  4. Physically impossible values. They list selenium at isotope 79, but selenium has no stable isotope at mass 79; that mass is bromine. They also report potassium at 64,000,000 µg/L (64 g/L) in Pfizer, far beyond anything the formulation could contain. Values like these point to calibration or detector overload in a heavy matrix, which undermines the quantification across the board.

  5. The replicate data undercut "precise." Draws from the same vial differ by up to about sevenfold, for example Moderna 045C22A sodium at 1,300,000 vs 180,000 µg/L. The authors themselves concede the heterogeneity makes it impossible to quantify the elements precisely. Measurement noise and contamination explain this more simply than "self-assembly."

  6. The cross-brand inference runs backwards. They read elements common to all brands as evidence of shared technology. But shared contamination sources (glass, steel, reagents, instrument background) predict exactly that same cross-brand pattern, and they ruled them out only by judgment, not by any test.

  7. Weak sample base. The study used 13 vials supplied informally by health workers, with no documented chain of custody. Many vials were 1–2 years past expiration by the Nov 2023–Jan 2024 analysis dates.
