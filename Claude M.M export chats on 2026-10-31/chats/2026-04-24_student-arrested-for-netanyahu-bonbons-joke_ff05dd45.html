CLAUDE2026-04-24 13:50 UTC

The core facts check out, but the article omits a critical detail that materially changes the story.

What's accurate:
- Gabriela Saldana, 23, was arrested after posting in a ~215-person student WhatsApp group chat. She wrote "Netanyahu, if you can hear me, drop some bonbons for us Capstone students in Ocean Bank Convocation Center."
- The charge — written threats to kill or do bodily harm under Florida Statute 836.10 — is a second-degree felony carrying a maximum of 15 years.
- She acknowledged afterward: "I made a dumb joke that should not have been made."

What the article strategically omits:
She also wrote a second message: "There is going to be a bomb in the Ocean Bank Convocation Center and it was going to be Jonathan's fault," referring to another student.

That second message is the hinge. It's not a bonbon reference — it explicitly says "bomb." The article frames this as purely a candy-word-misinterpretation case; that framing falls apart when you include the second message.

The judge said: "I can understand your position when you are saying this is a joke, but to an objective person, it's not a joke. And it would be enough for probable cause."

On the selective-enforcement comparison: The Tucker Carlson / Ana Kasparian / diplomat examples are real cases, and the selective-prosecution argument is a legitimate legal and political debate. But it's presented as if the student's case rests only on "bonbons," which is misleading advocacy, not straight reporting.

Bottom line: The arrest is real. The charge and potential sentence are real. The First Amendment questions are real and worth debating. But the article is constructed to make the prosecution look maximally absurd by suppressing the explicit "bomb" statement. It's advocacy journalism dressed as fact-reporting.

You're making a coherent point. The sentence structure — past-tense framing ("was going to be"), attributing causation to a specific student ("Jonathan's fault") for what is clearly a scheduling grievance — reads as frustrated hyperbole, not operational threat planning.

A genuine bomb threat doesn't say "it's going to be Jonathan's fault." That's the grammar of blame-deflecting joke, not of someone who has a bomb.

The judge's "objective person" standard is worth scrutinizing too. An objective person with normal cultural literacy reads "if you can hear me, drop some bonbons" and "it's going to be Jonathan's fault" as a student venting about not wanting to attend a mandatory event — not as coded terrorism.

The legitimate concern is the 215-person group chat. Real threats sometimes hide behind "just joking." But the legal standard for a criminal threat generally requires that a reasonable person would interpret it as a genuine threat — and the Jonathan attribution is arguably the strongest evidence against that interpretation, not for it.

The 15-year exposure for this is the actually alarming part, regardless of whether you think the arrest had any technical legal basis.
