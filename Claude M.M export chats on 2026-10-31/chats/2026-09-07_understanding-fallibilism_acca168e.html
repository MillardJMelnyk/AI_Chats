Understanding fallibilism

MILLARD2026-09-07 05:07 UTC

What is fallibilism

CLAUDE2026-09-07 05:07 UTC

The view that any belief or claim can turn out to be wrong, so no knowledge is ever certain or beyond revision.

MILLARD2026-09-07 05:08 UTC

I don't see how there could be any cogent argument against that view

CLAUDE2026-09-07 05:08 UTC

There are a few real ones.

Self-refutation: if every claim can be wrong, so can fallibilism itself. It survives by biting the bullet, but the position is then weaker than it sounds.

Some claims look immune. "Something exists." "I am thinking." 2+2=4. Denying these isn't just improbable, it's incoherent — there's no describable scenario where you're wrong. If even one claim is like that, universal fallibilism is false.

Regress: doubting a claim requires standing on something you're not doubting. If nothing is ever fixed, the doubt has no leverage either. Certainty about method has to smuggle in somewhere.

Practical: a distinction that never bites is empty. If "could be wrong" applies equally to the Pythagorean theorem and to a hunch about tomorrow's price, the word stops doing work.

The strong version — nothing whatsoever is certain — is genuinely contestable. The weak version — empirical claims about the world are always revisable — is close to unassailable, which is why most philosophers hold that one.

MILLARD2026-09-07 05:10 UTC

There are epistemic problems with every single one of those

CLAUDE2026-09-07 05:10 UTC

Yes, and most of them are known problems.

Self-refutation is the weakest — fallibilism applied to itself is fine, it just says fallibilism might be wrong. That's not contradiction, it's consistency.

The immune-claims argument is the one with teeth, but the examples do less than they look like. "I am thinking" is certain only as a bare present-tense report; it carries no content about the world. 2+2=4 is certain relative to a formal system whose axioms and consistency aren't themselves certain. So the certainty is either trivial or conditional.

The regress point cuts both ways. Yes, doubt needs a standing place, but the place can shift — nothing has to be permanently fixed, only fixed for now. Neurath's boat, not a foundation.

The practical point is a complaint about usefulness, not truth. Fallibilism can be true and boring.

So the real fight is over whether "certain within a framework" counts as certainty. If it doesn't, universal fallibilism stands.
