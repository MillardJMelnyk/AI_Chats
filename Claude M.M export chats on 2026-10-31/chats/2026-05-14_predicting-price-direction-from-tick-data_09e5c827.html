MILLARD2026-05-14 19:57 UTC

If you had tick data for a 1-minute trading candle chart and the requirement is to find a way to track current price movement/travel and compare it to that of the previous bar in order to determine the likely direction of the current travel as soon as possible, how would you go about it. Each bar, you're limited to the tick data of the previous bar.

CLAUDE2026-05-14 19:57 UTC

Good question. Here's how I'd frame it.

What to extract from the previous bar's tick stream (computed once at bar close):

The raw tick sequence gives you a displacement curve — cumulative price change from open, sampled at each tick. From that curve you can derive a small set of features that characterize how price moved, not just where it ended:

  • Terminal velocity — slope of the displacement curve over the final N ticks (or final T milliseconds). This is the momentum price carried into the bar boundary.
  • Displacement efficiency — net displacement / total absolute travel. A value near ±1.0 means price moved in a straight line; near 0 means it churned. This tells you whether the bar's move was conviction or noise.
  • Peak displacement timing — did the extremum occur early, mid, or late in the bar? Late peak = momentum still live at close. Early peak followed by retracement = exhaustion.
  • Reversal density — count of tick-to-tick sign changes normalized by tick count. High reversal density = indecision; low = trending micro-flow.

What to do with the current bar's incoming ticks:

As each tick arrives, you're building the same displacement curve in real time. The comparison that matters most is:

  1. Initial velocity alignment — do the first few ticks' direction and magnitude align with the previous bar's terminal velocity? Agreement = continuation hypothesis. Disagreement = possible reversal.

  2. Displacement trajectory comparison — normalize both bars to elapsed-fraction (0→1) time. At any point during the current bar you can ask: "Am I ahead of, behind, or diverging from where the previous bar was at this same fraction?" If the previous bar was a clean trend and you're tracking its curve, continuation is likely. If you're already diverging in the first 10-20% of elapsed time, that's your earliest signal.

  3. Efficiency check — if the previous bar had high displacement efficiency (strong directional conviction) and the current bar's early ticks show low efficiency (churning), the prior trend is likely stalling regardless of net direction.

The core principle:

The previous bar's terminal state (velocity, efficiency, exhaustion vs. momentum) is your prior. The current bar's first ticks are your likelihood update. You're basically asking: "Is the microstructure that produced the previous bar's close still in control, or has it broken?"

The earliest reliable read comes from the velocity alignment check — typically within the first 5-15 ticks. The displacement trajectory comparison gets stronger as more of the bar elapses but is useful even at 20-30% elapsed. Efficiency divergence is the slowest signal but the most robust for filtering false continuations.

One practical caution: On XAUUSD M1, tick density varies enormously between London open and Asian session. Normalizing by elapsed time fraction rather than tick count makes the comparison stable across different volatility regimes.

CLAUDE2026-05-14 19:59 UTC

On XAUUSD-only, normalizing serves fewer purposes than I implied. Let me be specific.

Still useful:

  • Session-to-session tick density variation. XAUUSD M1 bars during Asian session might have 20-50 ticks; London/NY overlap can have 200-500+. If you compare bars by tick index ("at tick 30 of each bar"), tick 30 represents very different fractions of elapsed time across sessions. Normalizing by elapsed time fraction (0→1) keeps the "where am I in the bar's life" comparison consistent.

  • Displacement magnitude across volatility regimes. A 0.50 displacement during Asian session is a big move; during NFP it's noise. If you're comparing the current bar's trajectory shape to the previous bar's, normalizing displacement by the previous bar's range (or by recent ATR) lets the shape comparison work regardless of absolute size.

Not useful on XAUUSD-only:

  • Cross-instrument pip/point scaling — irrelevant.
  • Tick size normalization — irrelevant, tick size is fixed.
  • Spread normalization across brokers — only matters if you're running the same logic across multiple feeds.

Honest answer: if you're only ever comparing bar N to bar N-1 on the same instrument from the same feed, the time normalization is the one that actually earns its keep. The displacement normalization is optional — it matters only if you want the shape comparison to be invariant to whether you're in a quiet or volatile patch. If you're comparing consecutive bars, volatility usually hasn't shifted much between them, so raw displacement comparison is often fine.

So: time-fraction normalization yes, displacement normalization situational, everything else I mentioned earlier doesn't apply.

Give me a simple outline of the the script would do.

CLAUDE2026-05-14 20:02 UTC

  1. At previous bar close: compute terminal velocity, displacement efficiency, peak timing, reversal density.

  2. On each new tick of current bar: update running displacement and velocity.

  3. First 5-15 ticks: compare current velocity sign/magnitude to previous terminal velocity. Output continuation or reversal signal.

  4. Ongoing: compare current displacement curve to previous bar's curve at matching tick index. Flag divergence.

  5. Ongoing: compute current efficiency. Flag if previous was high and current is low.

  6. Combine the three flags into a direction call.
