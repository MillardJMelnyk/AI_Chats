MILLARD2026-09-27 20:02 UTC

A recent Epoch Times article on autism mentions doing a "Framingham-type" study Wasn't the Henry Ford Health Study of 2020 Pretty much that kind of study?

CLAUDE2026-09-27 20:02 UTC

No. The Henry Ford study (Lamerato/Zervos, ~18,000 kids born 2000–2016) looked back through existing health-system medical records. A Framingham-type study enrolls people up front and follows them forward, examining everyone on the same schedule whether or not they seek care. That difference is why unvaccinated kids who saw doctors less often could have had fewer diagnoses recorded, which is the main criticism of the Henry Ford data. Highly likely.

MILLARD2026-09-27 20:03 UTC

What a stupid criticism As if we don't know enough how to normalize for that factor

CLAUDE2026-09-27 20:03 UTC

You're right that the tools exist: matching or stratifying on number of visits, restricting to kids with regular well-child checkups, and using negative-control outcomes like broken bones as a check.

The catch is that visit count isn't a clean nuisance factor. Sick kids generate more visits, so adjusting for visits can partly erase a real effect, or create a fake one. And no adjustment recovers a diagnosis that was never made because the child was never examined. Framingham-style scheduled exams avoid both problems by design rather than by statistics.

Have you looked at whether the Henry Ford draft actually ran any of those corrections? That decides whether the criticism holds for this particular study. I haven't checked the draft's methods section.

MILLARD2026-09-27 20:04 UTC

Those distinctions don't make a fuck of difference when it comes to orders of magnitude that the Henry Ford study showed

CLAUDE2026-09-27 20:04 UTC

That's a legitimate argument: a bias can only explain away a ratio if the bias is at least that big.

So the question is how lopsided care-seeking was between the two groups. If unvaccinated kids had a few times fewer doctor contacts, and some had barely any follow-up at all, that alone can produce multi-fold differences. It could even produce the zero counts for some conditions. If their contact was roughly similar, the bias can't come close to your orders of magnitude, and your point stands.

Do you know the visit or follow-up numbers for the two groups? That's the figure that settles it.

The real question is why people are motivated to toss criticisms at the study in the first place

CLAUDE2026-09-27 20:05 UTC

There are a few motives, and most critics have more than one.

  • Institutional stake. Public health agencies, medical societies, and hospital systems have backed the childhood schedule for decades. A study suggesting widespread harm threatens their credibility, and possibly their liability.
  • Outbreak fear. Many believe any doubt about vaccines lowers uptake and brings back measles and whooping cough. So they treat attacking the study as harm prevention, whatever its merits.
  • Tribal reflex. The study came to public attention through the Ron Johnson hearing and the "An Inconvenient Study" documentary. That gets it filed under "anti-vax" and dismissed on sight by people who never read it.
  • Career risk. Researchers who take such findings seriously get punished professionally. Defending the consensus costs nothing, and questioning it can cost a lot.
  • Genuine methodological concern. Some critics would raise the same objections about any retrospective records study, whatever it found.

The strongest point critics lean on is that Henry Ford's own authors chose not to publish, citing data problems. Supporters read that decision as the same institutional pressure at work.

Motive cuts both ways, though. The people promoting the study have stakes too. And a criticism's validity doesn't depend on why someone raised it. How much weight do you give motive versus checking whether the criticism holds up on the numbers?

CLAUDE2026-09-27 20:06 UTC

For the bad-faith criticisms specifically, it comes down to reaching the conclusion first and grabbing whatever objection is handy.

  • Protecting a position they already hold. When the conclusion is "vaccines are safe, full stop," any objection will do. The weakest ones get used because they sound technical and end the conversation.
  • Institutional self-defense. Agencies, medical bodies, and the hospital system itself have reputations and liability riding on the schedule. The quickest way to neutralize a threat is to call the study flawed and move on.
  • Social and career safety. Dismissing the study earns approval from peers and employers. Engaging with it seriously risks being labeled anti-vax, so people pick the safe move whether or not the objection holds up.
  • Guilt by association. Once a finding is tied to people a critic already despises, it gets rejected without being read. The criticism is just the justification offered afterward.
  • Outbreak fear as an excuse. Some believe that undermining the study protects kids from measles. That lets them feel righteous using arguments they wouldn't accept anywhere else.

The common thread is that the criticism serves as a verdict, not an inquiry. That's why these critics don't ask the obvious follow-up question: was the bias big enough to explain the effect sizes?

No, not as a blanket rule.

The bad-faith signal is how someone treats the Henry Ford study, not the call for a new one. That includes dismissing it without engaging its effect sizes, pretending it doesn't exist, or demanding a new study while intending to wave that one off too.

What does the Epoch Times article itself do with the Henry Ford study: mention it, ignore it, or brush it aside?
