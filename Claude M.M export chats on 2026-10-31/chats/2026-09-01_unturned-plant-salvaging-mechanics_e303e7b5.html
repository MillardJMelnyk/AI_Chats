Sample lease, Querétaro, 12 months, residential. Not a lawyer — this is a plain reading, not legal advice.

Parties

Three signers: landlord, tenant, and an obligado solidario — a co-signer who is jointly liable for everything you owe. You must produce one. Each party declares nationality, CURP, RFC, INE number, phone, email. As a foreigner you have no INE; that gets substituted with passport/residency card, and you'll need a CURP.

The core deal

  • 12 months fixed, dates blank. Renewal is not automatic: you must give written notice 30 days before expiry, and the landlord can refuse. Renewal = brand new contract, rent bumped by INPC (Mexican CPI).
  • Residential use only.
  • Rent blank, paid monthly in advance, 1st–5th, by deposit or bank transfer only. Maintenance is included in the rent.
  • Deposit = one month.

Money clauses that bite

  • Late fees: 6th–8th = 2%; 9th–11th = 4%; after the 11th = 6%. Bounced check = 20%.
  • Day one of non-payment, the landlord notifies SBI Comercializadora to start executing the legal policy — i.e. the eviction machinery starts immediately, not after a grace period.
  • Deposit is not rent. You cannot skip the last month. Landlord holds it 30 days after you hand back the property, returns it with no interest, minus repairs for anything beyond normal wear and minus unpaid services.
  • Every month is paid in full even if you don't use the place and even if you haven't formally handed it back.
  • Utilities (water, gas, electricity) are yours. Rent is not considered paid if the utilities for that period are unpaid — so a missed CFE bill converts into rent arrears. Receipts stay in the owner's name. Any internet/cable/phone you contract, you must pay off and cancel on exit.

Property condition

  • You accept it as habitable and cannot withhold rent for improvements.
  • Photo inventory taken at signing.
  • No alterations without prior written consent. Any improvement you make either stays for free or you pay to undo it — landlord's choice.
  • Hidden defects: you must notify in writing within 48 hours of discovering them. Miss the window and it's arguably your problem. This is the tightest clause in the document.

Criminal / extinción de dominio block (clauses 11 and 12)

Unusually heavy. No weapons, drugs, explosives, flammables. You indemnify the landlord and owner completely; if a forfeiture proceeding starts, you pay the lawyers and all damages. And the mere fact that the property is investigated, secured, or "related" by any authority to a crime — committed inside or outside the property, by you, your guarantor, or anyone you let in — is automatic grounds for rescission. No conviction needed.

Note: it cites the Ley Federal de Extinción de Dominio of 2009 as amended 2014. That law was repealed and replaced by the Ley Nacional de Extinción de Dominio in 2019. Outdated citation, common in boilerplate.

Other restrictions

  • No subletting, no assignment of rights, in whole or part.
  • Neither party liable for earthquakes, floods, force majeure — unless a court finds it was your fault.
  • Póliza jurídica: you must buy the legal/eviction policy and pay all its fees, and renew it 30 days before the anniversary. Fail to renew and it's grounds for rescission — and the landlord can renew it himself and bill you without asking.

Getting out

  • Rescission grounds include: late rent, one month owed, breach of any clause, wrong use, subletting, unpaid utilities, noise or "inappropriate attitudes" with neighbors, breaking condo rules, and death of the tenant.
  • Early exit: 30 days written notice with signed acknowledgment, plus you must let him hang a "for rent" sign and show the interior.
  • Penalty for leaving early: two months' rent. Same two-month penalty applies if he rescinds because you breached. So a breach costs you the deposit plus two months.

Drafting errors

  • Clause 18 (early termination penalty) cross-references "Cláusula Décima Sexta" for the notice rule, but notice is in Décima Séptima. Sixteenth is the rescission list. Sloppy, and ambiguous enough to argue over.
  • Clause 12 §I is written from the landlord's side declaring he doesn't know of your criminal involvement — a good-faith shield for him, no benefit to you.

What's worth pushing on before signing

The 48-hour hidden-defect window, the two-month early-exit penalty, "death of the tenant" as a rescission cause, and who actually pays for the póliza jurídica. Everything else is standard Querétaro boilerplate.

I overstated it. Most of the structure is standard Querétaro boilerplate, but several clauses are landlord-favorable beyond the norm and a few are likely unenforceable. Not a lawyer.

Genuinely concerning:

The 48-hour hidden-defect window. Mexican civil law puts the duty of necessary repairs on the landlord and warrants against hidden defects. This clause tries to shift that onto you by making a 48-hour written notice a precondition. Harsh and probably not enforceable as written, but it will be used against you in an argument.

"Death of the tenant" as rescission grounds. Under the civil code a lease normally survives the tenant's death — heirs and cohabitants continue. This clause is atypical and cuts against default law.

"Rescission without any judicial intervention." Not how it works. Evicting you still requires a court. The clause is written to make you believe otherwise.

The extinción de dominio block. Rescission triggered by the property being merely investigated or related to a suspected crime — committed inside or outside the property — by you, your guarantor, or anyone you let in. No conviction required, no proof required. That is far broader than necessary to protect the owner. You also swear under oath you have never committed any crime, ever.

Double penalty on breach. Clause 18 applies the two-month penalty not just to early exit but to any rescission you cause. So a late-payment breach costs you the deposit, two months' rent, plus damages.

The póliza jurídica clause. You pay for the landlord's eviction insurance, and if you don't renew it he renews it himself and bills you without your authorization. Note the policy company, SBI, is named by name in the contract — and this sample contract is SBI's own document. The contract routes business to its author.

Utilities converted into rent arrears. An unpaid CFE bill means rent is deemed unpaid, which triggers the SBI machinery on day one. A billing dispute becomes an eviction risk.

Asymmetry. There is no clause anywhere giving you a remedy if the landlord breaches. No repair obligation with a deadline, no rent abatement, no termination right. Every exit path is priced against you.

Internal inconsistency. Clause 1(c) says the arrendador is the sole legitimate owner, but clauses 2 and 12 repeatedly distinguish "owner" from "arrendador." Find out which you're dealing with, and if it's not the owner, demand to see his authority to lease.

Guarantor exposure. Your obligado solidario's liability is unlimited in amount and has no end date. Whoever signs that should read clauses 12 and 18 specifically.

Worth paying a Querétaro attorney a few hundred pesos to review before you sign. The blanks are all still empty, which means you have leverage to negotiate now that you won't have later.

Correcting one thing I said earlier: the deposit is not forfeited. Clause 4 says the opposite — the landlord may not apply the deposit to unpaid rent or any other unmet obligation. It comes back 30 days after handover, minus physical damage beyond normal wear and unpaid utilities. So on a payment breach the deposit is still yours; it just doesn't offset anything.

Here's the actual mechanics.

What clause 18 says. First paragraph: if you end the lease early, you pay two months' rent as a penalty, vacate, and pay within 30 days of giving notice. Second paragraph, one sentence: the same applies if you "give cause" for any rescission ground in clause 16.

So the two-month figure was written as an early-exit fee and then bolted onto every breach in the list by reference.

What clause 16 lists. Nine grounds: not paying punctually; owing a month or more; breaching any clause at all; wrong use; subletting; late utility payments; noise or bad behavior with neighbors; violating condo rules; death of the tenant. Item (c) — breach of any clause — swallows the whole contract. Every obligation in the document becomes a potential two-month trigger.

The stack on a payment breach. Late fee under clause 6 (2/4/6%). Plus the unpaid rent itself. Plus two months under 18. Plus damages and losses under 16, which says the breaching party pays them. Plus the póliza jurídica costs under 15, which are yours. Plus your guarantor is on the hook for all of it.

The two textual problems.

First, "diera motivo a alguna causal" — gives cause for a rescission ground. It doesn't say the landlord must actually rescind. Read literally, the penalty attaches to the breach itself, whether or not he ends the lease. That's the aggressive reading and it's the one a collections lawyer would take. The sane reading is that the penalty only makes sense alongside actual termination, since the first paragraph is entirely about vacating. Ambiguous drafting, and ambiguity in a contract of adhesion is normally construed against whoever drafted it.

Second, clause 18 cross-references clause 16 as the source of the notice requirement. Notice is in clause 17. Clause 16 is the rescission list. The paragraph doesn't cleanly cite its own trigger.

Where it gets absurd. Death of the tenant is a rescission ground in 16(i). Under the second paragraph, the estate arguably owes two months' rent for the tenant having died. That's a decent illustration of the clause being drafted by reference without anyone reading the result.

Practical exposure. A transfer that lands on the 7th realistically costs you 2%. Nobody terminates over that. The risk is that a small, arguable breach gives him a lever worth two months' rent, and clause 6 says the SBI machinery starts the first day of non-payment, so you're negotiating against a legal-policy company rather than a person.

What to change before signing. Ask to delete the second paragraph of clause 18 entirely — the two-month fee should apply to voluntary early exit only. If he won't, narrow it to grounds 16(b) through (f) and strike (a), (c), (g), (h), (i). Also add a cure period to 16(a): rescission only if payment is more than 15 days late after written notice.

The finding that matters most

Clause 3 does not tell you about Article 2378 of the Querétaro Civil Code.

Article 2378 provides that when a lease expires, the tenant — provided they are current on rent payments — has the right to have the contract extended for one year. The landlord may increase the rent by the amount of the annual legal interest. For a fixed-term contract, the tenant asserts the extension by counterclaim when answering an eviction suit. Source shows the text as current with modifications, last updated 25 August 2026.

Clause 3 of your contract says the landlord "reserves the right to accept said extension" and that renewal is by new contract with an INPC increase. That framing understates your position. If you're current on rent, the statute gives you a one-year extension as a right — and caps the increase at legal interest, not INPC. Which figure is higher depends on the year, but the mechanism is a statutory floor the contract doesn't mention.

Two caveats I can't resolve from here: whether Article 2378 is validly waivable by contract, and whether signing this document constitutes such a waiver. Article 6 of the same code says private rights may only be waived where the waiver doesn't affect public interest, and Article 7 requires any waiver be made in clear and precise terms leaving no doubt about the right being renounced. This contract never mentions Article 2378, so it arguably fails that clarity test. Ask a Querétaro attorney about this specifically. It's the single highest-value question in the document.

Clause 10 — Hidden defects

Written notice to landlord required; landlord repairs or authorizes repairs once notified; notice within a maximum of 48 hours from when the tenant became aware.

This is the harshest clause in the document. Under the civil code framework the landlord is obliged, even without express agreement, to deliver the property fit for use and in conditions of hygiene and safety, to maintain it during the lease making all necessary repairs, and to answer for damages from hidden defects existing before the lease. The tenant's corresponding duty is to notify of needed repairs "a la brevedad posible" — as soon as possible — and the penalty for failing to is paying damages caused by the omission, not forfeiting the claim. And if the landlord fails to repair, the tenant may rescind or go to court. (Verified in the Código Civil Federal, arts. 2412, 2415, 2416; the Querétaro code mirrors this structure but I did not verify its article numbers.)

So the contract converts "as soon as possible, penalty is damages" into "48 hours, or arguably lose the right." That's a compression of a statutory standard into a trap. Whether it's enforceable as written is a real question. Whether it will be waved at you in a dispute is not.

Negotiate this to a minimum of 5 business days, or to "a la brevedad posible" tracking the statute. Given the flooding and leak complaints on record for this development, this clause and rainy season are on a collision course.

What the contract contains nowhere: any landlord repair deadline. Any rent abatement if the unit is uninhabitable. Any self-help remedy if he doesn't respond. Any termination right for you if he breaches. Any cap on clause 15 charges. Any cure period. Any penalty on him for anything at all.

That asymmetry is common in Mexican residential leases. Name it anyway: your real protections are in the civil code — the landlord's non-waivable-by-silence duties to deliver a habitable property, maintain it, make all necessary repairs, guarantee peaceful enjoyment, and answer for pre-existing hidden defects; your right to rescind or go to a judge if he doesn't; and Article 2378's extension right. The contract is drafted to make you forget those exist. They do.
