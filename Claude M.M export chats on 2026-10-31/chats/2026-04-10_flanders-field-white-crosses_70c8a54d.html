CLAUDE2026-04-10 04:22 UTC

Close, but a couple of corrections:

In Flanders Fields is a famous WWI poem by Canadian Lt. Col. John McCrae, written in 1915. It references the battlefield region of Flanders (Belgium/France), where intense fighting occurred and poppies grew over the graves — which is why the poppy became the symbol of remembrance for WWI dead.

The iconic image you're thinking of — rows and rows of white crosses — describes military cemeteries generally, with the most famous examples being:

  • Normandy American Cemetery (Colleville-sur-Mer, France) — WWII, overlooks Omaha Beach, over 9,000 white marble crosses and Stars of David
  • Flanders Fields American Cemetery (Waregem, Belgium) — WWI, actually does exist and does have white crosses, though it's small (~368 graves)
  • Arlington National Cemetery — U.S., white headstones in rows

The Flanders region itself has many Commonwealth War Graves Commission cemeteries (like Tyne Cot, the largest Commonwealth WWI cemetery), but those use Portland stone headstones, not crosses per se.

So the mental image isn't wrong — it just blends the famous name from the poem with the visual of military cemetery rows.
