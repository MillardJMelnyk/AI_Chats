This is ACCR's standard privacy notice — the same firm that drafted the lease you're negotiating. It's a template, and it's sloppier than the law requires. What matters:

What you're actually authorizing (clause 3)

The purposes are listed as "en forma enunciativa, más no limitativa" — illustrative, not limiting. That's an open-ended blank check: anything they list is an example, not a boundary. Inside that list they claim the right to "investigarte... verificar tus antecedentes jurídicos, financieros, civiles, patrimoniales, solicitar información a entidades gubernamentales y privadas" — investigate you, and check your legal, financial, civil and property background with both government and private entities. That's a full credit and asset trawl, which is normal for a póliza jurídica, but note the scope is stated as unlimited.

The transfer clause is the real problem

"nos facultas expresamente a transferirlos a autoridades de cualquier nivel (Federales, Estatales, Municipales), organismos públicos y privados" — you expressly authorize transfer to authorities at any level, and to public and private bodies. Unnamed private third parties, no purpose stated, no limit. Mexican law (LFPDPPP art. 36) requires transfers to identify who receives the data and why, and requires a clause letting you refuse the transfer. There is no such box here. This is the single clause I'd push back on.

Indefinite retention (clause 4)

"La temporalidad del manejo de tus Datos Personales será indefinida" — they keep your data forever. The law requires deletion once the purpose is exhausted. Forever means your financial background sits in their database long after you move out.

You can't actually exercise your rights

Clause 6 tells you that you have access, rectification, cancellation and opposition rights, then gives you no address, no email, no data-protection contact, no procedure, no response deadline. The law requires the responsible party's domicile and a designated contact. Without them the clause is decorative.

Other defects

  • Never states what data they collect — required by law.
  • Clause 7 puts the burden on you to check their website for changes, rather than notifying you.
  • Clause 9 says paying them presumes total acceptance. For financial and property data, Mexican law requires express written consent, not presumed. Your signature on the line at the bottom is what actually supplies it.
  • Opening line calls the law a presidential decree. It isn't — it's an act of Congress. Clause 1 says the notice protects "personal directivo, personal administrativo" — directors and administrative staff. That's employee boilerplate; the template wasn't written for tenants.

Practical read

Nothing here is unusual for a Mexican rental-guarantee firm; every one of them runs a template like this. Refusing to sign it outright most likely kills the rental. The two things worth raising alongside your lease amendments, since you're already negotiating:

  1. Ask them to limit the transfer clause to what the guarantee actually requires, or write next to your signature: "No consiento la transferencia de mis datos personales a terceros privados." You have a statutory right to refuse that transfer.
  2. Ask for the full privacy notice with their domicile and the contact for ARCO requests. If they can't produce one, that tells you something about how they handle your file.

I'm not a lawyer, and none of the above is a legal opinion on your position.

Clause by clause, with the Spanish that matters translated.

Header

"En términos de lo previsto en la Ley Federal de Protección de Datos Personales en Posesión de los Particulares... EL DESPACHO JURÍDICO ACCR ARRENDAMIENTO CONFIABLE, S.C. (EN LO SUCESIVO EL PRESTADOR), por decreto Presidencial publicado en el D.O.F, establece el presente Aviso de Privacidad."

"Under the Federal Law on the Protection of Personal Data Held by Private Parties, the law firm ACCR Arrendamiento Confiable, S.C. (hereinafter THE PROVIDER), by Presidential decree published in the Official Gazette, issues this Privacy Notice."

Two things. First, EL PRESTADOR — "the provider" — is how they refer to themselves for the rest of the document. You are the titular, the data subject, addressed throughout as "tú." Second, and more useful to you: this notice is written against a law that no longer exists. The 2010 law was replaced on 20 March 2025 by a new LFPDPPP, in force the following day, and INAI, the regulator, was dissolved; data-protection supervision now sits with the Secretaría Anticorrupción y Buen Gobierno. Every article number cited in this document (16, 22) is from the repealed text. That doesn't void the notice, but it tells you nobody has touched this template in over a year, and it means the compliance points below are measured against a framework the firm isn't tracking.

Clause 1 — Purpose

"tiene por objeto la protección de los datos personales de toda persona que solicite nuestros servicios profesionales (personal directivo, personal administrativo)"

"The purpose is the protection of the personal data of any person who requests our professional services (directive personnel, administrative personnel)."

The parenthetical is nonsense in your context. Personal directivo and personal administrativo mean company managers and admin staff — this is language from an employee-facing template. You are a prospective tenant, not their staff. The notice's own stated scope doesn't describe you. Not fatal, but it's the first evidence this document was pasted together rather than drafted.

The rest — "tratamiento legítimo, controlado e informado... autodeterminación informativa" (legitimate, controlled and informed processing... informational self-determination) — is lifted verbatim from the statute's purpose clause. Boilerplate, no obligation added.

Clause 2 — Definition

"Dato Personal es cualquier información concerniente a una persona física identificada o identificable"

"Personal Data is any information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person."

Straight statutory definition, copied correctly. Nothing to object to. Worth understanding that it is deliberately broad: your name, RFC, CURP, address, phone, income, employer, bank details, and anything that can be linked back to you all fall inside it.

Clause 3 — The operative clause

This is the whole document. Break it into four parts.

(a) How you give consent

"Al proporcionar tus Datos Personales por escrito, a través de una solicitud, formato en papel, formato digital, correo electrónico, Whats App o cualquier otro documento, aceptas y autorizas al PRESTADOR..."

"By providing your Personal Data in writing, through an application, paper form, digital format, email, WhatsApp or any other document, you accept and authorize THE PROVIDER..."

Consent is triggered by the act of handing over information, through any channel. If you send them a photo of your passport or your bank statements over WhatsApp, that is the consent event. There is no separate moment where you agree — supplying the data is agreeing.

(b) What they do with it

"utilizar y tratar de forma automatizada tus datos personales e información suministrados, los cuales formarán parte de nuestra base de datos"

"To use and process your personal data and supplied information in automated form, which will become part of our database."

Automated processing, permanent database entry. Combined with Clause 4, your file becomes a standing record in their system.

(c) The phrase that removes the limits

"con la finalidad de usarlos, en forma enunciativa, más no limitativa, para:"

"for the purpose of using them, by way of illustration but not limitation, for:"

Enunciativa, más no limitativa is a term of art in Mexican drafting. It means the list that follows is a set of examples, not a boundary. Legally, the purposes stated do not constrain them — anything else they later decide to do with your data is arguably covered because the list was never exhaustive. This single phrase is what turns a purpose list into an open authorization. It also runs directly against the statutory finalidad (purpose limitation) principle that Clause 5 claims they honor.

(d) The list itself

"identificarte, ubicarte, comunicarte, contactarte, investigarte, revisar la originalidad de tus datos, verificar tus antecedentes jurídicos, financieros, civiles, patrimoniales, solicitar información a entidades gubernamentales y privadas, elaborar contratos, enviarte información, por cualquier medio que permita la ley para cumplir con nuestros fines sociales."

Item by item:

  • identificarte — identify you.
  • ubicarte — locate you. Physical location, address tracing. This is the one people skim past. It authorizes them to find you, which is exactly what a rental-guarantee firm needs if you stop paying and move.
  • comunicarte / contactarte — communicate with and contact you.
  • investigarte — investigate you. Unqualified.
  • revisar la originalidad de tus datos — verify your documents are genuine, i.e. check your ID and payslips aren't forged.
  • verificar tus antecedentes jurídicos — check your legal record: court cases, judgments, any litigation history.
  • financieros — credit history. In practice, Buró de Crédito.
  • civiles — civil status and civil proceedings: marriage, divorce, family court.
  • patrimoniales — your assets. Property registry searches, what you own.
  • solicitar información a entidades gubernamentales y privadas — request information about you from government bodies and private companies. This is a fishing licence with no named recipients.
  • elaborar contratos — draft contracts. The legitimate one.
  • enviarte información — send you information. Marketing, with no opt-out offered.
  • por cualquier medio que permita la ley para cumplir con nuestros fines sociales — by any means the law permits, to fulfil our corporate purposes. Fines sociales means the company's corporate object, not "social ends."

Separate point on the financial check: pulling a Mexican credit report requires its own specific, express written authorization under the credit-bureau statute. A general line buried in a privacy notice does not satisfy that. If they intend to query Buró de Crédito, they should be putting a separate authorization form in front of you. If they don't, and they pull it anyway, that is a distinct violation from anything in this document.

(e) The transfer paragraph

"nos facultas expresamente a transferirlos a autoridades de cualquier nivel (Federales, Estatales, Municipales), organismos públicos y privados."

"You expressly empower us to transfer them to authorities at any level (Federal, State, Municipal), and to public and private bodies."

The transfer to authorities is defensible — they may have to respond to a court or a tax authority. Private bodies is the problem. No recipient is named, no purpose is stated, no category is defined. On its face this authorizes handing your complete file — income, assets, credit, identity documents — to any private company they choose, for any reason, permanently, with no notification to you. This is the clause with real downside for you, and it is the one I'd fight.

Clause 4 — Retention

"La temporalidad del manejo de tus Datos Personales será indefinida a partir de la fecha en que nos los proporciones, con las limitaciones de Ley"

"The retention period for handling your Personal Data will be indefinite from the date you provide them, with the limitations of the Law."

Indefinite. Not "for the duration of the lease," not "plus five years for tax purposes." Forever. Your financial and asset profile stays in their database after the tenancy ends, after you leave Mexico, after the firm changes ownership.

Con las limitaciones de Ley is the only brake, and it defines nothing — it points at a statute they've already cited wrong. Retention limited only by an unspecified external reference is not a retention policy.

Clause 5 — Their obligations

"El PRESTADOR es responsable del tratamiento... obligada a cumplir con los principios de licitud, consentimiento, información, calidad, finalidad, lealtad, proporcionalidad y responsabilidad... se compromete a guardar estricta confidencialidad... mantener las medidas de seguridad administrativas, técnicas y físicas."

"THE PROVIDER is the data controller... obliged to comply with the principles of lawfulness, consent, information, quality, purpose, loyalty, proportionality and responsibility... undertakes to maintain strict confidentiality... and to maintain administrative, technical and physical security measures."

Responsable is the legal term for data controller — it establishes who is accountable, and it's correctly assigned to them. The eight principles are recited straight from the statute.

What this clause gives you: nothing you didn't already have. Every duty listed is imposed by law whether or not they write it down. What's missing is anything specific — no named security standard, no breach-notification commitment, no liability, no time limit on responding to an incident. And note the internal contradiction: they promise to honor the finalidad and proporcionalidad principles two paragraphs after writing a purpose clause that is explicitly non-limiting and a retention period that is explicitly indefinite. They cannot do both.

Clause 6 — Your rights

"tienes derecho en cualquier momento a ejercer tus derechos de acceso, rectificación, cancelación y oposición al tratamiento de tus datos personales."

"You have the right at any time to exercise your rights of access, rectification, cancellation and opposition."

These are the ARCO rights. In practice:

  • Acceso — demand to see everything they hold on you, and where they got it.
  • Rectificación — force correction of anything wrong.
  • Cancelación — force deletion.
  • Oposición — object to a specific use even while they keep the data. This is the one that lets you stop the marketing or stop a particular transfer without killing the whole file.

The defect: the clause tells you the rights exist and gives you no way to use them. No address, no email, no phone, no named data-protection contact, no form, no procedure, no deadline for their reply. The law requires the controller to designate a personal-data contact and to publish the means of exercising ARCO in the notice itself. As written, you would have to guess where to send a request. That is the clearest single failure in the document, because it's the clause that converts your rights from theory into something you can actually enforce, and it's empty.

Clause 7 — Unilateral amendment

"El PRESTADOR Ocasionalmente modificará y corregirá este Aviso de Privacidad, por lo tanto, te pedimos que revises este aviso regularmente en la página de internet en https://arrendamientoconfiable.com/"

"THE PROVIDER will occasionally modify and correct this Privacy Notice; therefore we ask you to review this notice regularly on the website."

They can change the terms whenever they want, and the burden of finding out is yours. The law requires the controller to state the procedure by which it will communicate changes to you. "Go check our website" shifts a duty they owe you onto you.

The practical effect compounds with Clauses 3 and 4: purposes are already non-limiting, retention is already indefinite, and now the terms can be broadened later without telling you, over a dataset they hold forever. Note also the link is just the homepage — not a page where the notice actually lives.

Clause 8 — Protection reference

"El PRESTADOR protegerá tus Datos Personales en los términos de las fracciones del artículo 16 de la LEY FEDERAL..."

"THE PROVIDER will protect your Personal Data under the terms of the fractions of article 16 of the Federal Law."

They cited the wrong article. In the 2010 law, article 16 is not about protecting data — it lists what a privacy notice must contain: the controller's identity and domicile; the purposes; the options and means offered to limit use or disclosure; the means for exercising ARCO rights; transfers where applicable; and the procedure for communicating changes.

So they've pointed at a compliance checklist and called it a protection promise. And measured against that checklist, this notice fails most of it: no domicile, no options to limit use, no ARCO channel, and a change procedure that consists of telling you to check a website. Under the 2025 law it's worse in a different direction — the new integral notice must spell out which personal data will be processed, flag any sensitive data, and separate the purposes that require your consent from those that don't. This notice never says what data it collects at all.

Clause 9 — Consent by conduct

"Aportar tus datos personales, cubrir el pago parcial o total de nuestros servicios, es un hecho que presume tu total aceptación al contenido del presente Aviso de Privacidad."

"Providing your personal data and paying part or all of our fees is a fact that presumes your total acceptance of this Privacy Notice."

They're constructing consent from behavior: hand over documents or pay the póliza fee, and you're deemed to have accepted everything above.

Tacit consent works for ordinary data. It does not work for what this notice is actually after. Financial and patrimonial data require express consent under Mexican law — and Clause 3 is explicitly built around antecedentes financieros and patrimoniales. A presumption drawn from paying a fee cannot authorize an asset and credit investigation. Which is precisely why there's a signature line.

Signature block

"Acepto de conformidad el contenido del Aviso de Privacidad que me da ACCR ARRENDAMIENTO CONFIABLE S.C." — NOMBRE Y FIRMA

"I accept in agreement the content of the Privacy Notice given to me by ACCR Arrendamiento Confiable S.C."

This line is the load-bearing part of the entire document. Everything above it — the non-limiting purposes, the financial and asset investigation, the indefinite retention, the transfer to unnamed private parties — is weakly supported until you sign. Your signature converts all of it into express written consent. That is what they need and what the rest of the page is designed to obtain.

There is also no date field. Fill one in yourself and keep a photographed copy of the exact page you signed, including anything you write on it.

What to do with it

Anything you want limited has to be written on the page before your signature, or attached and referenced. Once signed clean, you have consented to the broadest reading of every clause above.

Two additions worth making, both minimal and both defensible to a landlord's agent:

  1. Above your signature: "No consiento la transferencia de mis datos personales a terceros privados. Consiento únicamente las transferencias requeridas por autoridad competente o legalmente exigibles." — I do not consent to the transfer of my personal data to private third parties; I consent only to transfers required by competent authority or legally mandated. Opposition to a transfer is a right you hold; you are not asking a favour.
  2. Ask them in writing for the domicile and the contact for ARCO requests, since the notice omits both. Their answer, or lack of one, is information.

If they refuse both, that is worth knowing before you sign the lease itself rather than after.

Standard caveat, said once: I'm not a lawyer and this isn't a legal opinion on your position. If you want the two annotations to hold up, a Querétaro abogado will charge you very little to look at one page.

What it is: the tenant application form for the póliza jurídica — the questionnaire that feeds the investigation you read about yesterday in Clause 3 of the privacy notice. (That's why the third section is headed DEL INMUEBLE ARRENDADO — "the leased property.") This is the collection instrument; the privacy notice is the permission slip.

Should you fill it out: yes, if you're going ahead with that house. There is no póliza without it, and no lease without the póliza. But four things on it need a decision from you before you write anything:

1. Employment block. Empresa donde labora, domicilio de su trabajo, teléfono de su trabajo (el número que proporcione debe ser confirmable), nombre de su jefe inmediato. They will call that number and ask about you. Online English teaching for a small remote company is the hardest kind of employment for a Mexican verification desk to confirm — no local office address, possibly no switchboard, possibly a foreign number. Sort out in advance who picks up and what they'll say. This is the single most likely reason for a rejection or a demand for a stronger obligado solidario.

2. RFC and INGRESO MENSUAL. RFC is your Mexican tax ID. If you don't have one, that's a gap you need to resolve before submitting, not after. Monthly income will be checked against the rent — Mexican pólizas typically want provable income around three times rent, and "provable" means bank statements or payslips, not a number on a form.

3. Four references with phone numbers — two family, two personal or work — plus everyone who will live in the property. You are handing over other people's names and phone numbers, and those people will be called. Tell them first. Their data goes into the same database with the same indefinite retention.

4. Requiere factura del arrendamiento: SÍ / NO. Only tick SÍ if you actually need a tax invoice for the rent. You don't, for personal residential use, and asking for one sometimes prompts the landlord to push the tax cost onto the rent.

Then the declaration above the signature line:

"Declaro bajo protesta de decir verdad... autorizo su investigacion a fin de constatar su originalidad y validez. Reconozco que es un servicio juridico que ofrezco en garantia a el arrendador..."

"I declare under penalty of perjury... I authorize its investigation in order to verify its originality and validity. I acknowledge that this is a legal service that I offer as a guarantee to the landlord."

Two live points. Bajo protesta de decir verdad means a false or padded answer isn't just a rejected application — it's grounds to void the lease later. And the last sentence fixes you as the party providing the póliza, which is the standard basis for the tenant paying its fee. If you and the landlord agreed anything different about who pays, this form contradicts it.

CLAUDE2026-09-07 20:41 UTC

Yes. Six things.

1. Don't sign or pay until the lease amendments are agreed. This is the one that costs you real money. Once you've signed the application and paid the póliza fee, you're financially committed to that house and your leverage on the lease clauses evaporates. Settle the contract first, then submit the form. Before you pay, get in writing: the fee amount, who pays it, and whether it's refunded if the investigation declines you or the landlord backs out. Many firms don't refund.

2. ACCR is the landlord's lawyer, not a neutral service. The form says so outright — the póliza is a guarantee you provide to the landlord. So you pay a law firm to hold your complete financial profile, employer, boss's name, references, and asset picture, and that firm's client is the other side. If you ever miss rent, the entity with the file on you is the entity that comes after you. Combine that with ubicarte from the privacy notice and the picture is clear: they've been authorized to find you.

3. The authorization has no expiry. The form authorizes verification, and the privacy notice makes the processing indefinite. Nothing stops them re-running checks on you during the tenancy or after it ends. You're not consenting to one snapshot.

4. The declaration is sworn, and it stays live. Bajo protesta de decir verdad means an inaccuracy isn't just a rejected application — it's a defect they can raise later, including as grounds against you in an eviction. The field that trips people is personas que habitarán el inmueble. List everyone. If that changes later, notify them in writing rather than letting the form go stale.

5. Your obligado solidario is more exposed than you are. They get investigated on the same terms and their data goes in the same database. A solidary obligor in Mexico can be pursued for the full debt directly, without the landlord having to exhaust remedies against you first. Make sure whoever signs understands that, not just that they're "vouching."

6. Everything you hand over is held forever with no breach commitment. Passport, RFC, bank statements, income — indefinite retention, no notification duty if they're hacked, no ARCO contact given. Send documents through one channel you can log, keep copies of exactly what you sent and when, and don't send more than they specifically ask for.

None of these are reasons not to proceed. Point 1 is the one with a deadline.

A sworn statement of income — the self-employed alternative to payslips and an employer verification call. Instead of ACCR phoning your boss, you declare your own earnings under penalty of perjury.

This is the piece that solves the problem I flagged on the application form. If your income is from online teaching and trading rather than a Mexican employer with a switchboard, this letter is how you prove solvency.

What each blank wants:

  • de manera independiente desde hace ___ años — how many years you've done it independently

The date says 20 July 2022. Template leftover. Change it to the day you sign.

"Procedencia de los recursos... cien por ciento lícitas" — you're declaring your money comes from entirely lawful sources. That's an anti-money-laundering line, standard, but it's part of what you're swearing to.
