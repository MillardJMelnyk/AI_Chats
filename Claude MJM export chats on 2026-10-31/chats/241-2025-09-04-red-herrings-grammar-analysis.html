And the red herrings keep a-coming. is that correct as written.
Claude2025-09-04 15:31 UTC

The sentence "And the red herrings keep a-coming" is grammatically acceptable as written, though it's quite informal and stylized.

The phrase "keep a-coming" uses an archaic or dialectal construction where "a-" is prefixed to the present participle. This form was more common in older English and is still found in some regional dialects, folk songs, and informal speech. It's equivalent to saying "keep coming."

In standard modern English, you'd typically write:

  • "And the red herrings keep coming"

The "a-coming" form gives the sentence a folksy, colloquial, or deliberately old-fashioned tone. Whether it's "correct" depends on your intended style and audience - it's perfectly fine for informal writing, creative writing, or when you want that particular flavor of speech.

are there better idioms than "red herring" that mean the same?
Claude2025-09-04 15:31 UTC

Here are some alternatives to "red herring" that convey similar meanings:

For deliberate misdirection:

  • "Smoke and mirrors" - suggests intentional deception or distraction
  • "Wild goose chase" - pursuing something futile or misleading
  • "Throwing someone off the scent" - deliberately misdirecting
  • "Sleight of hand" - diverting attention from what's really happening

For distractions or irrelevant information:

  • "Rabbit hole" - getting sidetracked into irrelevant details
  • "Straw man" - misrepresenting an argument to deflect from the real issue
  • "Chasing shadows" - pursuing something insubstantial
  • "Barking up the wrong tree" - focusing on the wrong thing entirely

For false leads:

  • "Dead end" - a path that leads nowhere
  • "False start" - beginning with the wrong approach
  • "Blind alley" - a direction that won't lead to solutions

The best choice depends on your context. "Smoke and mirrors" works well for intentional deception, "rabbit hole" is great for getting lost in irrelevant details, and "wild goose chase" emphasizes the futility of the pursuit. "Red herring" remains popular because it's concise and widely understood, but these alternatives can add variety and sometimes more precise meaning to your writing.

of all those only straw man is comparable. "Red herring" denotes the object doing the distracting, not the distraction itself.
Claude2025-09-04 15:33 UTC

You're absolutely right - I mixed up the perspective. A "red herring" is the misleading thing itself, not the act of being misled.

Looking at my list again with that correction:

  • "Straw man" - yes, this is the misleading argument itself
  • "Smoke and mirrors" - this refers to the deceptive techniques, not the specific misleading element
  • "Wild goose chase" - this is the futile pursuit, not the thing being pursued
  • "Rabbit hole" - this is the distracting path, not what leads you into it

Other objects that serve as distractions:

  • "Decoy" - something specifically designed to mislead
  • "False flag" - an operation designed to appear as if carried out by another party
  • "Trojan horse" - something that appears benign but serves a hidden purpose
  • "Smokescreen" - something used to conceal true intentions

But honestly, "red herring" is quite unique in how specifically it captures that concept of a misleading clue or piece of information that diverts attention from the real issue. Most other idioms describe either the process of being misled or the broader deceptive strategy, rather than the specific misleading element itself.

Millard2025-09-04 15:33 UTC
so red herring must still be in current use, because I don't see anything as good from your lists.
Claude2025-09-04 15:33 UTC

Exactly. "Red herring" has persisted in current use precisely because it fills a specific linguistic niche that other idioms don't quite match. It's particularly valued in:

  • Mystery/detective fiction - where misleading clues are a key plot device
  • Logic and argumentation - as a specific type of fallacy
  • Legal contexts - for evidence or arguments that distract from the main issue
  • Academic and analytical writing - when discussing misleading information or diversionary tactics

The phrase remains current because it's both precise and concise. While "decoy" comes close, it often implies something more deliberately planted, whereas a red herring can be accidentally misleading. "Smokescreen" suggests broader obfuscation rather than a specific misleading element.

The fishing origin (dragging a strong-smelling fish across a trail to throw hunting dogs off the scent) gave us this perfectly specific metaphor for "a piece of information that misleads or distracts from the relevant issue."

Sometimes the best idiom is the one that's stuck around - and "red herring" has earned its place by being irreplaceable for what it describes.
