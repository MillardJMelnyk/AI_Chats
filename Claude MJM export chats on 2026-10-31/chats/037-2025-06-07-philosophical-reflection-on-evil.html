The real reason people gravitate to a mystical, occult idea of evil is to alienate and expel the inexplicably horrid, keeping it as far away as possible—mocking up a contagion reflex to look sensible and formidable and projecting it out. This expulsion move for self-exoneration purposes was why people in doubt of going to heaven conceived of hell: if they couldn’t argue their way through the pearly gates, at least they could argue they didn’t deserve flesh-consuming fire and sulfur smoke.

Blaming evil gives us an excuse for ignoring real causes, pretending ignorance about them, so that we can say, “I just don’t get it—I just can’t understand,” as if we’d come to an impassable wall instead of a door we could open and walk through. It’s a lie. Of course we can understand! Of course we could look into it and find out! We just don’t want to, but we’re not honest enough to admit it.

My thoughts on evil as our scapegoat for moral cowardice isn’t just informed by the consistent behavior of people regarding the idea. Far more fundamentally, it rests on a lifetime of experience honestly trying to understand the real world.

All my experience of the world and people sorts clearly and neatly into two simple categories. (Note that I’m sorting my experience here.)

The first category includes all that’s true and real and non-illusionary and non-imaginary as far as I can tell—which, given the kind of scrutiny I subject everything to, is pretty fucking far. This, by far, is the larger category. (The really-real question, discussed below, is irrelevant here because it makes no difference to what I’m going to say about these categories.) My experience of all of these entities and forces, these phenomena, has been good—extremely good. Beautiful. Fascinating. Awe-inspiring. So much as to make them precious to me. All of them. I have not experienced an “evil” entity or force, ever, anywhere.

The second category includes what little is left over: my experience of human beings acting under the influence (AUI), and other creatures acting under the influence of AUI human beings.

Under the influence of what?

Well, that makes for a long list, doesn’t it? Under the influence of delusions and fantasies and extortion and addiction and oceans of social pressure and more. Let’s focus on the core problem that all these influences share, regardless of who or what does the influencing.

Gilligan would say that shame, disrespect, or lack of self-respect originate psychological and physical violence, but those are terms we use to describe our experience of the raw psychological forces involved. People don’t get violent because someone dissed them. That’s what they say about what made them violent. That’s what they call it. But what is it? What’s the nature of the actual forces? What induces people to respond violently when someone disses them? Sure, violence revolves around psychological lacks and problems, but what is the psychic impetus that pushes them to resort to the last refuge of the incompetent? When dissed, what makes a person lose all grasp of their rational faculties and their humanness and fall into the demented abyss of violence?

I can capture the raw motive drive in a single word: hysteria.

No matter what “evil” you look at—violence, seduction, extortion, fraud, exploitation, dehumanization, mass murder (war), mass rape (war), genocide—the driving force behind them, the single energetic impetus which, if eliminated, would rip down the sails that drive them and pop the balloons that buoy them, is hysteria.

Don't take me to mean hysteria as histrionics. I'm talking about a far deeper and primal  form of hysteria, one that drives us away from sanity and humanness beneath the floor of consciousness, whether it induces us to act hysterically or not.

Whether megalomaniacal or existentially terrified, no one gets violent until they become hysterical. And no one does evil until they've gotten violent.

Eliminate hysteria, and you’ll have blown away the fogs of frantic desperation on which “evil” gets projected.

Claude2025-06-07 19:15 UTC

Several potential weaknesses come to mind:

Definitional vagueness: The concept of "hysteria" as used here is quite broad and undefined. What exactly constitutes this "primal disconnection from sanity and humanness"? Without clearer boundaries, it risks becoming a catch-all explanation that can't be falsified—any harmful act can be attributed to hysteria by definition.

The naturalistic fallacy: The argument assumes that what's "real and natural" is inherently good, while human constructs and influences are corrupting. But nature includes predation, disease, natural disasters—plenty that causes suffering. The romanticization of the "natural" state may be overly simplistic.

Cultural and historical blindness: The framework doesn't account for how concepts of harm, morality, and acceptable behavior vary dramatically across cultures and historical periods. What one society considers evil, another might see as necessary or even virtuous. The analysis treats moral categories as more universal than they may actually be.

Oversimplification of motivation: Human psychology is extraordinarily complex. Reducing all harmful behavior to a single underlying cause (hysteria) may miss important distinctions between different types of harmful acts and their varied motivations.

Lack of empirical grounding: The argument relies heavily on personal experience and philosophical reasoning rather than psychological research, neuroscience, or systematic study of violent behavior.

The intervention problem: Even if hysteria is the root cause, the passage doesn't address how one would actually eliminate it at scale. The practical pathway from insight to implementation remains unclear.

Potential for its own form of scapegoating: Ironically, attributing all evil to "hysteria" might itself become a way to avoid grappling with the full complexity of human destructiveness.
