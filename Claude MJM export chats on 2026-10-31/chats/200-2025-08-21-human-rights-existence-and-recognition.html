I can see the logic in your argument, but I think there are several ways to challenge this position:

The Discovery vs. Creation Problem

Your argument assumes rights are purely conventional - that they exist only when stipulated and recognized. But consider this analogy: before humans discovered gravity, did gravitational force exist? Most would say yes. Similarly, one could argue that certain moral facts about human dignity and treatment exist independently of our recognition of them. The rights we "stipulate" might be discoveries rather than inventions.

Historical Precedent Against Pure Conventionalism

History provides compelling counterexamples. When abolitionists argued against slavery before it was legally prohibited, were they creating new rights or recognizing existing ones? When they said "slavery violates human dignity," they weren't just stipulating a new convention - they were claiming to recognize something that was already morally true. If rights required prior stipulation and recognition, moral reformers could never legitimately challenge existing systems.

The Bootstrapping Problem

Your position creates a logical difficulty: how do the first rights ever get stipulated? If rights can only exist through recognition, what gives anyone the authority to make that first stipulation? There seems to be a missing foundation - either some rights exist pre-conventionally to ground the authority to stipulate others, or we're in an infinite regress.

The Universality Intuition

While your English word analogy is clever, rights claims seem different from linguistic conventions. When people across cultures independently develop similar moral intuitions about harm, dignity, and fairness, this suggests something beyond mere stipulation. The convergence of moral systems on certain principles - despite lacking common stipulation - points toward something more fundamental.

What's your response to these challenges?
