Claude2025-07-17 16:56 UTC

This H.G. Wells quote touches on a fascinating tension in social and political transformation. Wells seems to be acknowledging that even positive systemic change - toward what he calls "world social democracy" - will inevitably face resistance and create casualties among those who can't or won't adapt.

There's something both prescient and troubling about his observation. The prescient part is his recognition that major transitions, even beneficial ones, are messy and painful. History bears this out - think of how industrialization improved living standards overall but devastated traditional communities, or how democratic movements often face violent backlash.

The more unsettling aspect is the almost clinical way he discusses people "dying protesting against" change, as if this is an acceptable cost of progress. It reflects a kind of utilitarian thinking common in his era - that individual suffering can be justified by greater collective benefit.

What strikes me most is his phrase about "malcontents, many of them quite gallant and graceful-looking people." There's an acknowledgment that opposition to change doesn't necessarily come from bad people, but from those who may have legitimate grievances or who simply can't adapt to new realities.

This feels relevant to many contemporary debates about technological disruption, globalization, and social change. The challenge is how to pursue beneficial transformation while minimizing harm to those displaced by it, rather than simply accepting their suffering as inevitable.

What's your sense of this quote? Does it resonate with current changes you're observing?

What did he think this "new world order" was?
Claude2025-07-17 16:57 UTC

Wells wrote extensively about his vision of a "New World Order" in his 1940 book of the same name. His concept was essentially a "socialistic and cosmopolitan world government" that would transcend national boundaries and aim for "peace, order, and shared human progress."

His specific vision included several key elements:

A unified world government responsible for maintaining peace and promoting the common good, along with a global educational system that would promote scientific and technological progress and foster a sense of shared humanity.

Wells believed this was necessary because he saw nationalism and competing nation-states as the root cause of war and conflict. Writing during World War II, he argued that only a centralized world authority could prevent future global catastrophes.

What's particularly interesting is that Wells wasn't advocating for some shadowy conspiracy - he was openly proposing what he saw as a rational, scientific solution to humanity's problems. He genuinely believed that technological progress was making the world smaller and more interconnected, requiring new forms of governance to match.

His "New World Order" was meant to be:

  • Democratic and socialist in nature
  • Focused on scientific advancement and education
  • Committed to eliminating war through unified global governance
  • Dedicated to human progress and shared prosperity

The quote you shared reflects his recognition that even this benevolent vision would face resistance from people attached to older systems - nationalism, traditional hierarchies, local autonomy - even if those systems were demonstrably harmful.

It's worth noting that Wells' ideas were influential but controversial even in his time, and his use of the term "New World Order" later became associated with various conspiracy theories, though that wasn't his intention.

Claude2025-07-17 17:02 UTC

That's a sharp observation. You're pointing to a fundamental contradiction in Wells' thinking - he's essentially arguing that his vision of progress is so obviously correct that resistance to it can be dismissed as backward thinking from people who will inevitably be swept aside by history.

There's definitely an element of intellectual supremacism there. Wells presents his "world social democracy" as the rational, scientific solution, implying that those who oppose it are either ignorant or clinging to outdated systems. The phrase about "malcontents" - even "gallant and graceful-looking" ones - suggests he sees opposition as ultimately illegitimate, regardless of the character or reasoning of those who resist.

This reflects a broader pattern in utopian thinking where the presumed righteousness of the end goal is used to justify dismissing or overriding dissent. The logic becomes: "This system will be better for everyone, so anyone who opposes it must be wrong, and their resistance is just an unfortunate obstacle to overcome."

It's particularly telling that he frames this as inevitable - that people "will hate" and "will die protesting" - rather than considering whether their objections might have merit or whether the transition could be designed to address their concerns.

This kind of thinking has appeared across the political spectrum in various forms - the assumption that one's preferred system is so obviously superior that opposition must stem from ignorance, selfishness, or attachment to harmful traditions rather than legitimate disagreement about values or methods.

You're right that it's a form of supremacism - the supremacy of his particular vision of rationality and progress over other ways of understanding human flourishing and social organization.
