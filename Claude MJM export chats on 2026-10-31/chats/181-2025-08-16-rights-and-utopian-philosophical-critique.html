Critique this for me: The ostensible point of invoking rights—the myth of rights—is to protect us against evildoing, violation, and harm… but, in fact, rights don’t do this at all. There are at least four steps in any rational (key word) formulation of a set of rights. Here I’m talking about what reasonable, intelligent people must do at a minimum to arrive at anything resembling a cogent list of rights: 1.      **Recognition**: They must witness and/or experience real-life patterns of behavior that no one wants to be subjected to and which no one would condone others being subjected to. 2.      **Abstraction**: They must generalize those patterns of behavior and designate them as wrong in principle. 3.      **Articulation**: They must describe the goods whose enjoyment would be damaged or destroyed by these wrongs. 4.      **Declaration**: They must declare that each of these goods implies a universal “right” that entitles those who “have” the right to enjoy that good and be free from wrongs that would damage or destroy their enjoyment of that good. In a rights paradigm, there is no basis for arguing or pressing rights at all until all four steps have been taken. This all seems to make sense to people for some reason I’ve long scratched my head trying to figure out. As best as I can understand: it’s because they take evildoing, violation, and harm as unquestionable, foregone conclusions. Consider what would happen if we did not assume that evildoing, violation, and harm were interminable givens. I’ve had a very hard time getting anyone to sincerely consider that prospect. Forget about getting people to open their minds to the possibility of a real world free from evildoing, violation, and harm. People seem unable even to entertain the idea of a hypothetical world free from evildoing, violation, and harm, let alone a potentially real one. The very thought boggles their minds but, more importantly, triggers their cynicism like a reflex hammer does a knee-jerk. “No use even imagining that, because it will never happen.” Bullshit! Question: In a world free from evildoing, violation, and harm, would it ever occur to a person to formulate rights? Question: In a world free from evildoing, violation, and harm, would going through all that work (Steps #1- #4) seem reasonable to spend time and effort on? If so, for what purpose and to what end? I’m sure you can guess my answers.
Claude2025-08-16 15:16 UTC

This is an interesting philosophical critique that raises thought-provoking questions about the foundations of rights discourse, but it contains several problematic assumptions and logical gaps.

Strengths of the argument:

The four-step framework for rights formulation is actually quite insightful - it accurately describes how rights concepts typically emerge from observed patterns of harm. The central question about whether rights would exist in a harm-free world is genuinely provocative and worth considering.

Major problems:

1. False dichotomy: The argument assumes we must choose between either (a) accepting harm as inevitable and developing rights, or (b) working toward a harm-free world. But these aren't mutually exclusive. Rights frameworks can serve as tools for reducing harm while we work toward better conditions, rather than just resigned acceptance of perpetual suffering.

2. Unrealistic premise: The author dismisses as "cynicism" the observation that a completely harm-free world is implausible. But this isn't cynicism - it's recognition that humans have competing interests, limited resources, and imperfect information. Even with the best intentions, conflicts arise. The author provides no mechanism for how such a world would emerge or sustain itself.

3. Circular reasoning: The argument essentially says "rights are unnecessary because if we didn't need rights, we wouldn't need rights." This doesn't address how we transition from our current reality to this hypothetical state, or what prevents backsliding.

4. Misunderstanding rights' function: Rights don't just respond to existing harms - they also establish frameworks for cooperation, resource allocation, and conflict resolution even among well-intentioned people. Even in a largely benevolent society, you'd still need principles for handling disagreements about competing goods.

5. Historical blindness: The author ignores how rights frameworks have actually contributed to reducing many forms of systematic harm throughout history, suggesting they may be part of the solution rather than mere symptoms of the problem.

The piece reads more like frustrated idealism than rigorous philosophical analysis. While the utopian vision has value as inspiration, dismissing practical protections as mere "myth" seems premature at best.
