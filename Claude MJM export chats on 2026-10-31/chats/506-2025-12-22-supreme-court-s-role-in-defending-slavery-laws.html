Supreme Court's role in defending slavery laws

Millard2025-12-22 14:54 UTC
did the US Supreme Court uphold laws that furthered and defended slavery?
Claude2025-12-22 14:55 UTC

Yes.

Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857): Ruled that Black people, whether enslaved or free, could not be US citizens and had no standing to sue in federal court. Declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional, holding that Congress lacked power to prohibit slavery in federal territories. Affirmed that enslaved people were property under the Fifth Amendment.

Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842): Struck down state personal liberty laws, upheld federal Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, ruled that slaveholders had constitutional right to reclaim escaped slaves even in free states.

Strader v. Graham (1851): Held that status of enslaved people taken temporarily to free states reverted to slave status upon return to slave states, denying any freedom claim.

These decisions actively protected and expanded slavery's legal framework until the 13th Amendment (1865) abolished the institution.

Millard2025-12-22 14:56 UTC
What about endorsing the practice of slavery itself?
Claude2025-12-22 14:56 UTC

Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857) went beyond upholding laws to explicitly endorse slavery as an institution:

Chief Justice Taney's majority opinion declared that Black people "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect" and were "so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect." The decision characterized enslaved people as property with constitutional protection, stating slaveholders had the right to take their "property" into any territory.

The opinion treated slavery not as a necessary evil to be tolerated but as a constitutionally protected right that could not be interfered with by Congress or territorial governments.

Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842) similarly endorsed the practice by characterizing the return of escaped slaves as a "positive, unqualified right" of slaveholders requiring active federal enforcement.

These rulings provided constitutional legitimacy to slavery itself, not merely passive recognition of existing state laws. The Court constructed affirmative constitutional protections for the practice until military defeat and constitutional amendment ended the institution.
