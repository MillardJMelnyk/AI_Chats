Summary of findings:

Textual evidence outside the Bible:

  • The MUL.APIN compendium (cuneiform tablets copied in the 8th century BCE) explicitly associates Kaiwan with Saturn, documenting its position and divine attributes.
  • Kajamānu or Kayyamanu (Akkadian for "the constant") is the ancient Mesopotamian name for the planet Saturn. In ancient Mesopotamia, he was regarded as the "star of Ninurta."
  • The Damascus Document in the Dead Sea Scrolls references Amos 5:26 but interprets it symbolically.

Archaeological evidence:

  • One website claims artifacts exist but provides no museum identification, excavation records, or academic citations. This claim cannot be verified.
  • Michael Heiser, a biblical scholar, notes there's "no evidence in artifacts or texts from ancient Israel" that the six-pointed star was a national symbol then.
  • Gershom Scholem, a leading expert on Jewish mysticism, found no evidence linking the hexagram to ancient pagan stars.
  • The August 1862 edition of The Quiver noted, "The fact is, we know but little respecting the false gods worshipped in Syria and Palestine at different times, although the names of many of them have come down to us."

Critical gaps:

  • The Bible doesn't say what the Star of Remphan looked like. Claims it's a hexagram are pure speculation.
  • Both "Kiyyun" and "Remphan" are hapax legomena (words appearing only once), and there is debate whether they are meant as common or proper nouns.

Answer: We have Mesopotamian cuneiform texts documenting Saturn worship under the name Kajamanu/Kaiwan — this is the underlying referent. However, no authenticated archaeological artifact or ancient text depicts or describes the specific visual form of "the star." The physical appearance of this idol remains unknown.
