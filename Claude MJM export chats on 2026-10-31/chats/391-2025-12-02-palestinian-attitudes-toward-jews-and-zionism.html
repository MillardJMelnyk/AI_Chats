Short answer: Attitudes vary, but polling shows high levels of antisemitism alongside a rhetorical distinction many Palestinians maintain between "Jews" and "Zionists."

Key points:

  • ADL and Pew surveys consistently show the MENA region, including Palestinian territories, with among the highest rates of antisemitic attitudes globally (70-90% range on various measures).

  • Many Palestinian political figures, intellectuals, and ordinary people explicitly distinguish Zionism from Judaism. Hamas's 2017 charter formally adopted this framing (the 1988 version was overtly antisemitic).

  • In practice, the distinction often blurs—particularly in conflict periods. Anti-Jewish rhetoric, imagery, and tropes appear in media, education materials, and political speech, often not limited to Israelis.

  • Diaspora Jews who explicitly oppose Zionism are sometimes embraced as allies; this suggests the distinction is operationally real for some.

Bottom line: The rhetorical distinction exists and is sincerely held by some. Empirical data on attitudes suggests widespread hostility that frequently extends beyond Israeli Jews to Jews generally.

Claude2025-12-02 13:56 UTC

Pre-Zionist period (pre-1880s):

Jews lived in Ottoman Palestine for centuries (Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed, Tiberias) under dhimmi status—subordinate but protected. Relations were generally stable. Sporadic violence occurred (1834 Hebron looting, 1838 Safed attack) but was rare, localized, and typically tied to general instability rather than anti-Jewish campaigns specifically.

Escalation timeline:

  • 1880s-1917: Tensions rose with Zionist immigration, land purchases, and explicit settler-colonial aims
  • 1920s: First major organized violence (Nebi Musa 1920, Jaffa 1921, Hebron 1929)
  • 1936-39: Arab Revolt—large-scale armed resistance
  • 1948+: Nakba and subsequent conflicts produced mass displacement and generational trauma

Comparison:

The historical record shows a clear causal arc. Pre-Zionist Palestinian attitudes toward Jews were typical of dhimmi-majority relations in the Ottoman world—unequal, occasionally tense, but not characterized by systematic hatred or organized violence. The intensity of hostility tracks directly with Zionist colonization, dispossession, and the establishment of Israel.

Bottom line: Contemporary Palestinian hatred of Jews is overwhelmingly a product of the conflict itself, not a pre-existing condition that preceded it.
