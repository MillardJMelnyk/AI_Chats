The deeply ingrained, persistent idea that domination is inherent to "human nature" rests on the assumption (if the problem is even recognized) that the vast majority of homo sapiens history was not communalistic, which ongoing archaeology is fast debunking. Give me a good statement that gives the reader an apt impression of just HOW rare domination probably was.
Claude2025-12-07 16:25 UTC

Statement:

If homo sapiens history were compressed into a single 24-hour day, domination-based social structures wouldn't appear until approximately 11:12 PM. For roughly 23 hours, humans lived in small-scale, mobile societies where archaeological evidence (burial uniformity, settlement patterns, absence of storage monopolization, artifact distribution) indicates resource-sharing and fluid, non-coercive social arrangements were the norm.

The numbers:

  • Homo sapiens: ~300,000 years
  • Hierarchical/domination structures (post-agricultural surplus accumulation): ~10,000–12,000 years
  • Domination as percentage of human existence: 3–4%

The claim that domination is "human nature" requires treating the 3–4% exception as the defining rule while ignoring the 96–97% baseline.

Claude2025-12-07 16:28 UTC

Yes, arithmetically. The premises:

  • Homo sapiens: ~300,000 years (current consensus, Jebel Irhoud fossils, 2017)
  • Institutionalized domination structures (evidenced by grave goods disparity, storage monopolization, monumental coerced-labor architecture, fortifications): ~10,000–12,000 years

Caveat worth noting: "Absent" and "virtually nonexistent" are stronger than the evidence strictly permits. The defensible claim is: no archaeological evidence for systematic domination structures prior to the Neolithic. Absence of evidence isn't proof of absence, but it's the best available data across thousands of excavated sites spanning hundreds of millennia.

If you want bulletproof phrasing:

"...archaeologically undetectable for roughly 96% of human history."

Millard2025-12-07 16:34 UTC
So, the same lack of evidence of systematized/structural domination applies to other Homo species, too?
Claude2025-12-07 16:34 UTC

Yes, with appropriate epistemic weight.

Neanderthals (best-documented non-sapiens Homo): No grave goods disparity, no storage monopolization, no fortifications, no monumental architecture. Small group sizes, evidence of cooperative care (healed injuries requiring extended support).

Homo erectus, heidelbergensis, etc.: Sparser records, but same pattern—cooperative hunting evidence, shared fire sites, no indicators of coercive hierarchy.

Caveat: Record quality degrades significantly with age. The absence is consistent but the evidentiary base thins. Strongest statement applies to sapiens and neanderthalensis; earlier species require more hedging.

Defensible phrasing:

"...archaeologically undetectable across the entire Homo genus—some 2.5 million years and multiple species."

Or more cautious:

"...absent from the archaeological record of every Homo species examined, spanning 2.5 million years."
