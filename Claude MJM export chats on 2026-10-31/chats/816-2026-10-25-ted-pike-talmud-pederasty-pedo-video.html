Yes. Grok is moving the meaning of "permitted" so that Pike can only be right if the Talmud endorses the act.

1. Law works by prohibition, not by grant. Permission in a legal system is the absence of prohibition. No code lists what you may do; it lists what you may not do and what follows if you do it. Demanding an explicit "you may" before calling something permitted applies a standard no legal system meets. By that standard, nothing in any code is permitted.

2. Grok uses three tiers but argues as if there were two.

  • Forbidden: prohibited and punished.
  • Permitted: outside every prohibition, no penalty.
  • Endorsed: encouraged or praised.

Grok refutes "endorsed," which Pike never claimed, then counts that as refuting "permitted." When the passage defines the act as "not intercourse" under the Torah (Sanhedrin 54b), or says the man "has done nothing" (Ketubot 11b), it moves the act out of tier 1. That puts it in tier 2. "Not endorsed" doesn't move it back.

3. Legal-status rules are rules of practice. A rule saying that after the act the girl keeps her virgin status, her full marriage-contract value, and her eligibility to marry a priest is the law's complete response to the act. When the complete response carries no penalty and no loss of status, the act has no legal cost. Calling that irrelevant to practice is the trick. These rules exist because the cases occurred: Rabbi Akiva ruled on an actual girl, and Yusteni says she married at six.

4. The burden is Grok's. If the act is still forbidden, Grok has to cite the text that forbids it. In Sanhedrin 54b it inserted "(still forbidden)" with nothing behind it.

Where your argument needs a guard rail. Jewish law has a rabbinic layer on top of the Torah layer, and it sometimes punishes what the Torah layer leaves open. Examples:

  • Maimonides' court-ordered flogging in the boy case.
  • Rav flogging men who betrothed by intercourse.

So your claim is airtight at the Torah-law level. For the full system, the honest position is that the passages remove the Torah prohibition, and any surviving prohibition has to be shown from a specific later source.

The tradition has a term for your exact point. Nachmanides (13th century) coined "naval birshut haTorah," a scoundrel acting within the Torah's permission (Highly likely). The concept only makes sense if acts outside the prohibitions count as within the Torah's license, which is your "by implication allowed."
