The Hasbará: or why Zionism avoids debate and nests in victimhood and systematic lies Summary Hasbará operates as a discourse management device whose main objective is not to demonstrate truth, but to redirect the argumentative focus, reformulate the premises of the debate, and reinforce interpretive frameworks favorable to the Israeli narrative (The Jewish Agency for Israel, 2002). This article examines Hasbará from its contemporary development, analyzes its discursive mechanisms based on two basic works, and exposes its historical application in different key moments of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, incorporating evidence from international reports and academic studies that highlight its role in the systematic evasion of responsibilities (Amnesty International, 2024; B'Tselem, 2025; Finkelstein, 2000; Frank, 2009; Pappe, 2006). 1. Introduction Discussing Israel, especially regarding the conflict with Palestine, often results in thematic displacements, argumentative diversions, and emotional appeals that hinder direct discussion about responsibilities, historical facts, or violations of international law. This phenomenon is documented in political communication manuals prepared for diplomats, activists, and pro-Israel spokespersons, who have systematized persuasive rhetoric techniques to intervene in public debates, especially in academic, journalistic, and digital environments (The Jewish Agency for Israel, 2002; Frank, 2009). Far from being a spontaneous phenomenon, Hasbará constitutes an institutionalized communication strategy, linked to geopolitical, cultural, and symbolic objectives, where perpetual victimization serves to delegitimize legitimate criticism and perpetuate narratives of moral superiority (Mearsheimer & Walt, 2007; Shlaim, 2000). 2. First book: The Hasbara Handbook (The Jewish Agency for Israel, 2002) The Hasbara Handbook: Promoting Israel on Campus was developed as a manual for students and organizations seeking to promote the image of Israel in educational spaces. The text does not focus on historical facts, but on persuasion techniques, explicitly explaining how to shift the focus of the argument when a discussion becomes unfavorable. The manual introduces two main strategies: the “positive framing” model and the “reactive framing” model (The Jewish Agency for Israel, 2002; Nelson, 2012). 2.1 The “positive framing” consists of emphasizing values associated with Israel such as “democracy,” “scientific innovation,” “religious pluralism,” or “ancestral land,” even if these aspects are irrelevant to the subject under discussion. The objective is to replace the uncomfortable question with a favorable emotional image, a tactic that ignores evidence of structural discrimination and aligns with broader propaganda efforts (Finkelstein, 2000). 2.2 The “reactive framing,” in turn, instructs responding to criticism by directing the focus toward opposing actors, for example: when bombings are discussed, “terrorism” is mentioned; when occupation is mentioned, “self-defense” is mentioned; when apartheid is mentioned, “antisemitism” is mentioned. The response does not seek to resolve what was said, but to change the axis of meaning of the debate, a practice that has been criticized for its similarity to state disinformation techniques (Frank, 2009; Oren, 2007). The manual does not attempt to conceal this operation; on the contrary, it presents it as a legitimate tool in the “battle for public opinion.” This confirms that the systematic shifting of focus when discussing with defenders of Israel is not random, but methodological, and extends to modern digital campaigns that amplify selective lies (Nelson, 2012). 3. Second book: The Israel Project’s Global Language Dictionary (Frank, 2009) The Global Language Dictionary, prepared by The Israel Project under the direction of Frank Luntz, represents a sophisticated evolution of Hasbará, aimed at political leaders, journalists, and international communicators. This manual of more than 100 pages details a dictionary of phrases tested in focus groups to maximize empathy and minimize criticism, prioritizing emotional language over verifiable facts (Frank, 2009). It explicitly instructs avoiding terms such as “occupation” or “illegal settlements,” substituting them with euphemisms such as “disputed territories” or “communities,” while repeatedly emphasizing “peace,” “security,” and “the right to defend itself,” even in contexts of disproportionate aggression. Among its central tactics, the dictionary recommends starting every response with empathy toward Israeli victims (“Israelis just want to live in peace”), and then pivoting toward accusations against “terrorists” or “extremists,” regardless of the original question. It warns against debating historical or legal data, recommending instead appealing to emotional analogies (e.g., comparing Hamas to Al Qaeda) and accusing critics of “hating Israel” or being “antisemitic” (Frank, 2009; Massad, 2006). This operation is not defensive, but proactive: the objective is to “win the message” before the interlocutor formulates their argument, a form of narrative control that reveals systematic contempt for substantive debate (Chomsky, 1983). The document, leaked in 2009, confirms the institutionalization of strategic lying as communication policy, extending Hasbará from university campuses to global press rooms and social networks (Nelson, 2012; Pappe, 2006). 4. Historical application Since the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, Hasbará has operated to frame the international narrative of the conflict, concealing mass expulsions and presenting the Nakba as a “voluntary exodus” (Morris, 1987; Pappe, 2006). During the 1967 War, territorial expansion was presented as an act of “preventive defense,” despite evidence of aggressive planning (Oren, 2007). In the 1982 invasion of Lebanon, the bombings were justified as actions against “terrorist bases,” disregarding the civilian dimension of the damage and massacres such as Sabra and Shatila (Fisk, 2001). During the First and Second Intifada, the narrative centered on Palestinian violence, omitting the structural relationship of military occupation and the disproportionate use of force (Reinhart, 2002). In the operations in Gaza (2008–2009, 2014, 2021, 2023–2025), the framework of “self-defense” has been insisted upon despite the extreme imbalance of forces and international documentation of war crimes by Israel, including indiscriminate attacks, blockades that constitute collective punishment, and systematic genocide, with more than 70,000 Palestinians killed and 170,000 injured up to October 2025, according to the Gaza Ministry of Health reported by the UN (UN, 2025; HRW, 2024; Amnesty International, 2024; B'Tselem, 2025). These figures and patterns have been explicitly classified as genocide by United Nations bodies in 2025. The Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory concluded that Israel has committed and continues to commit genocide in Gaza, finding evidence of four of the five prohibited acts under the 1948 Genocide Convention — killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction, and imposing measures intended to prevent births — accompanied by direct evidence of genocidal intent in statements by senior Israeli officials and systematic military conduct (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2025a). Similarly, the UN Special Committee to Investigate Israeli Practices described the situation as “the ongoing genocide against the Palestinians” and warned of a deliberate policy aimed at the destruction of Palestinian society (United Nations General Assembly, 2025). These narratives follow to the letter the recommendations of the Global Language Dictionary: emphasizing “rockets” and “terror tunnels” while omitting the context of siege and apartheid (Frank, 2009; Mearsheimer & Walt, 2007). In all these stages, the shifting of focus has been constant: from occupation to “security,” from civilian devastation to “the fight against terrorism,” from political criticism to “antisemitism,” a pattern that reveals a chronic evasion strategy (Finkelstein, 2000; Massad, 2006). Conclusion Hasbará is not only a practice of discursive defense; it is a system of legitimacy fabrication that prioritizes manufactured victimization over verifiable facts. Its main objective is not to explain, but to configure the field of what can be said, restricting criticism and reorienting the debate toward frameworks that favor the Israeli state narrative, even at the cost of documented lies proven in opinion laboratories (Frank, 2009; Nelson, 2012). In 2025, two separate United Nations investigative bodies – the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory and the Special Committee to Investigate Israeli Practices – have formally concluded that Israel is committing genocide against the Palestinian people in Gaza, finding evidence of multiple prohibited acts under the 1948 Genocide Convention accompanied by clear genocidal intent (United Nations Human Rights Council, 2025a; United Nations General Assembly, 2025). Far from protecting Israel from unjust accusations, Hasbará has become the communicative shield that enables the continuation of these internationally recognized crimes with impunity. Understanding it does not imply adopting a specific stance regarding the conflict, but recognizing the political dimension of communication in the construction of meaning. Without this recognition, any debate becomes hostage to strategies whose purpose is not truth, but rhetorical victory, perpetuating cycles of impunity (Pappe, 2006; Shlaim, 2000).
