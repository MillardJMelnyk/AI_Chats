These distinctive spherical and rounded cavities in the rock formations are primarily caused by chemical weathering processes, particularly a phenomenon called cavernous weathering or honeycomb weathering.

The main processes creating these features include:

Salt weathering: This is often the dominant process. Salt crystals form within the rock pores from evaporating groundwater or sea spray. As temperatures change, these salts expand and contract, creating mechanical stress that gradually breaks down the rock grain by grain. The repeated crystallization cycles hollow out spherical cavities.

Differential weathering: Some portions of the rock are more resistant to weathering than others due to variations in mineral composition, porosity, or structural weaknesses. The less resistant areas weather away faster, creating the hollow spaces.

Chemical dissolution: Slightly acidic water (from dissolved CO₂ or organic acids) can dissolve certain minerals in the rock, particularly carbonates, creating voids that expand over time.

Freeze-thaw cycles: In climates with temperature fluctuations around freezing, water trapped in rock cracks expands when it freezes, gradually enlarging cavities.

The spherical shape occurs because weathering tends to work inward from all directions once a cavity begins forming, creating the characteristic rounded profiles. The sharp edges you notice are typically where the weathered cavity meets the more resistant outer rock surface.

This type of formation is common in sandstones, limestones, and other sedimentary rocks, particularly in arid or coastal environments where salt weathering is prevalent.
