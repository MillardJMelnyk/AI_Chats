Premise correction: The ~90% depopulation estimate is attributed overwhelmingly to disease (smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus), not direct killing. If you mean population decline broadly, use that; if you mean killing specifically (violence/war/enslavement), the 90% figure does not apply — direct-violence mortality estimates are far lower and regionally variable, with no single aggregate figure agreed upon.

Timeline for the ~90% population collapse (disease-driven):

  • First contact: 1492 (Caribbean).
  • Estimated pre-Columbian population of the Americas: ~40–100 million (wide range, contested; Dobyns' 1966 estimate ~90–112M is high-end, Denevan's 1992 consensus estimate ~54M is more commonly cited).
  • ~90% decline is generally dated as achieved by ~1600–1650, i.e., roughly 110–160 years after 1492.
  • Mechanism: epidemics often outran physical European contact — pathogens spread through Indigenous trade/social networks ahead of colonizers, so many populations collapsed before ever meeting a European in person.

Sourcing caveat: These are historical demographic reconstructions, not census data — pre-contact population baselines are estimated via archaeology, carrying capacity models, and colonial-era records, and the actual figure is disputed by ±50% or more depending on methodology (Denevan 1992, The Native Population of the Americas in 1492; Dobyns 1966, Current Anthropology).

Violence-attributable mortality (war, massacre, forced labor, enslavement, starvation from displacement) — separate from epidemic disease:

  • Absolute numbers for violence-caused deaths in North America (north of Mexico) across the full colonial period (1492–~1900): estimates range 1–5 million, depending on which causes are bundled into "violence" (Thornton puts pre-contact N.   • Stannard (1992, American Holocaust) argues for a higher violence-share and frames the whole 400-year process as genocide, but his numbers are contested by demographers as not methodologically separable from disease deaths — flagging this because it's the outlier high-end claim, not consensus.

Timeframe if you want "when did violence-driven decline peak": concentrated in three windows — Caribbean 1492–1550, Northeast/Southeast US 1600s–1830s (King Philip's War, colonial wars, removals), and Western US 1850–1890 (California genocide, Plains wars). No single start-to-90% timeline exists because violence-driven decline wasn't continuous or aggregate — it was regional and episodic across ~400 years, unlike the disease collapse which was fast and near-universal by ~1650.
