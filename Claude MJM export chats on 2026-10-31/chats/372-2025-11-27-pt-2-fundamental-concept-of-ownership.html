What is the most fundamental building block unique to “civilized” societies? 

Not language — that predates civilization by hundreds of thousands of years. 

Not hierarchy, which developed as a superstructure. 

Not the family unit, which predates societies themselves.

Ownership.

For 10,000 - 15,000 years, every society we’ve called “civilized” has been built on it—but not only as its foundation. The canonical design of the structure and dynamics of “civilized” societies revolves around ownership, is configured to enable and sustain ownership, and is oriented to address the issues arising from ownership. We could well say that society is the collective establishment, perpetuation, and defense of ownership. Every other social structure and activity would fall apart without ownership first being firmly in place. In fact, everything else simply stops the instant ownership is seriously threatened—and nothing resumes until the threat has been eliminated. 

No other single factor exerts, societally, the kind of impact or draws investment with the gravity of ownership.

So, what exactly, in simple terms, is ownership?

Ownership is a particular relationship between a party and a good. 

Sadly, the near-universal consensus today is that ownership is the only kind of relationship between a party and a good. Strip away the legal overlays, the cultural variations, the philosophical justifications, and—at root—ownership implements a single principle:

First, deprive all—then share only if you want.

If a party owns a good, their relationship to it is absolutely exclusive. No other party can hold the rights to the good held by an owner unless ownership is transferred first, whether by legal means or illegal. Otherwise, every single party on Earth is excluded from access to, control of, or enjoyment of the good unless the owner gives it to them. 

Most critiques of ownership do not challenge this structure. They merely redistribute it. Collective ownership, commons, public property — these relocate the excluding party from individual to group, from private to state, or vice versa. The underlying principle of universal deprivation remains intact: some party controls, depriving all others except at the controller’s discretion.

The “sharing economy” doesn’t escape the paradigm of universal deprivation, either. You can’t share what you don’t have and don’t exclusively control. Joint ownership is a flavor, not an alternative. “Gift” economies fare no better. Gifting doesn’t escape ownership—it presupposes it.

There is one structural alternative. Only one.

First, share with all—then deprive only if absolutely necessary.

This isn’t a redistribution of ownership. It’s the eradication of ownership. It’s the displacement of a convention and a practice which is rooted—both psychologically and historically—in coercion, violence, and supremacism. Rooted in inhumanness. 

The immediate (knee-jerk) reaction I usually get to this prospect is opining in protest-cum-lament, like nested fledglings with beaks open wide and quivering: “But then what will we do? What will we do?” Such was I for most my life, too.

Before we answer that question, we’d do well to consider the nature of the question, and whether it’s a coherent one. In two decades of work on the subject, I have found nothing coherent in it.

But rather than frame this situation in terms of those who have no clue what to do unless we universally deprive others, let’s ask how we already do the alternative—because there is plenty of sharing in the world. In normally friendly situations, inclusion is the default. Exclusion requires justification—and the justification cannot be arbitrary but must meet whichever standard of necessity we agree on.

We already do this.

So, from whence the feigned ignorance, as well as the feigned ignorance that our ignorance is motivated?

Note what the universal share-first principle does not require:

It does not require technological abundance or post-scarcity conditions.

It does not require a mechanism for determining “necessary” to be specified in advance.

It does not require anyone’s agreement or buy-in to be coherent.

It’s a principle before it becomes a program. “How could that work?” and, “How could that be viable?” and other implementation questions are both premature and irrelevant until we have decided  whether the principle itself is sound, coherent, and preferable to the alternative. It’s as if we’ve forgotten that what we want comes first. What we can do about it is a downstream concern.

To my knowledge, no one is working on this. 

No one is fleshing out the concept. No one is developing its implications. No one is designing structures or systems that take universal sharing as foundational.

What’s worse, no one admits that this is something we must consider, discuss, flesh out, develop its implications, and design structures and systems for evaluation and experimentation. No one takes it seriously. No one has a hope in hell of any such endeavors bearing fruit.

Instead, for thousands of years, we’ve taken the epistemic stance of the cultist who does not need to do any of that work to know the “feasibility” and “value” of the idea, because the mere fact that it diverges from “the Truth” alone proves the idea is false.

We have been staring at deprive-first for ten to fifteen thousand years, certain that if we look in another direction, something terrible will happen. The vigil continues. The space remains vacant, completely unexplored.

Part 2: Objections

Every objection I’ve encountered falls into one of three categories: circular, misdirected, or premature.

“Human nature is selfish.”

This assumes what it needs to prove. Humans raised under deprive-first conditions behave accordingly. That tells us nothing about what humans would do under share-first conditions. The argument smuggles the paradigm into the premise, then claims the paradigm is validated by the conclusion.

“Tragedy of the commons.”

The “commons” in Hardin’s formulation is a collectively-owned resource — a different configuration of deprivation, not the absence of it. The “tragedy” arises from competing private interests exploiting a shared pool. Under share-first, there are no private interests extracting from a pool. The scenario doesn’t apply.

“No one would produce anything.”

This assumes production is motivated solely by the prospect of exclusive possession. But people produce for use, for others, for meaning, for participation, for recognition. Deprive-first suppresses most of these motivations by making production contingent on ownership. The objection mistakes a symptom of the current paradigm for a fixed feature of human behavior.

“It’s just communism.”

Communism relocates ownership from individuals to the collective or the state. The excluding party changes; the structure doesn’t. Communism is a reconfiguration of deprive-first, not a departure from it.

“How would it actually work?”

This demands implementation before the principle is even acknowledged. It’s a deflection. If the principle is incoherent, say so and show why. If it’s coherent but you doubt it can be implemented, that’s a different conversation — and one that can’t happen until the principle is engaged on its own terms.

“Someone has to decide who gets what.”

Under scarcity, yes — allocation decisions must be made. But the question is: what is the default prior to the decision? Deprive-first says: no one has access until someone grants it. Share-first says: everyone has access until necessity requires restriction. The allocation problem exists under both paradigms. The question is which default it operates from.

“People will take advantage.”

Some will. Under any system. The question is whether the structure itself generates and rewards exploitation, or whether it minimizes and isolates it. Deprive-first systematizes advantage-taking — it makes exclusive accumulation the path to security and power. Share-first removes that structural incentive.

Part 3: Vision and Invitation

What would we gain?

Not utopia. Not the elimination of conflict, scarcity, or difficult decisions. But a structural shift with consequences:

Security would not require accumulation. Access to what you need would not depend on your ability to exclude others from it.

Cooperation would not require enforcement. The default relationship between people would be mutual access, not mutual threat.

Power would lose its primary lever. Control over others depends on controlling what they need. Remove deprivation as the default, and domination loses its structural foundation.

The question “who owns this?” would disappear. In its place: “Is there a necessary reason to restrict access to this?” A harder question, but the right one.

This is not a blueprint. It is a direction.

The vigil has lasted long enough. I’m looking for people willing to turn their heads — not to look away from the problem, but to look at it, perhaps for the first time.

If you can see the inversion, and you’re interested in honest, open, intelligent conversation about it — I want to hear from you.

Here are the revised bullet points:

I've read the full article. Here's my critique across several dimensions:

Structural Problem

Page 7 appears to have duplicate bullet points—four shorter versions followed by the longer revised versions starting with "Survival and security would not require accumulation." This looks like an editing artifact where both sets were left in. You need to delete one set.

Also: the subtitle placeholder ("Add a subtitle...") still needs content.

Argument Strengths

The definition of ownership as "deprive-first, share only if you want" is sharp and does the work you need it to do. It cuts through the legal and philosophical overlays to expose the structural logic.

The dismissal of alternatives (commons, gift economy, communism, sharing economy) as reconfigurations rather than departures is the piece's strongest rhetorical move. It preempts the most common deflections and forces readers to confront the actual proposal.

The three-category organization of objections (circular, misdirected, premature) is clean and effective.

Argument Vulnerabilities

The "we already do this" section is underdeveloped. You assert it, gesture at "normally friendly situations," and move on. This is the strongest empirical hook you have—evidence that share-first is already operative in human life—and it gets two paragraphs. Indigenous societies, family structures, certain commons arrangements, disaster response, open-source communities: there's material here that could ground the principle in observable reality rather than leaving it as pure abstraction.

The "premature" categorization of feasibility objections will read to skeptics as evasion. You're essentially saying "don't ask how until you agree with the what." That's philosophically defensible—principles do precede programs—but it leaves hostile readers an easy exit. They'll say you're hiding behind abstraction to avoid the hard questions. You might strengthen this by acknowledging more directly that the demand for implementation details is often a bad-faith maneuver to avoid engaging the principle, while also signaling that you do take implementation seriously—just not before the principle is established.

The claim that "no one takes this seriously" and that the conceptual space is "completely unexplored" is vulnerable. Some anarchist traditions (Kropotkin's mutual aid, certain strands of anarcho-communism), indigenous knowledge systems, and ecological economists have explored adjacent territory. You could argue they didn't go far enough or framed it differently, but claiming the space is empty invites counterexamples that undermine your credibility. Consider softening to "almost no one" or "not in these terms."

Rhetorical Tension

The piece oscillates between analytical rigor and emotional intensity. "Get down to what the fuck is really going on" and "the insane idea that some people deserve to live like gods while the rest of us crawl like worms" are passionate, but they sit uneasily next to the careful logical work in the Objections section. This isn't necessarily a problem—the piece is an invitation to collaboration, not a peer-reviewed article—but be aware that readers who respond to one register may be put off by the other.

The "vigil" metaphor is evocative in the title and opening but largely disappears. If it's structurally important, it could do more work throughout.

Potential Counterarguments You Don't Address

"Share-first still requires enforcement." If someone does restrict access without necessity, who decides that, and who enforces it? You address the allocation question but not the enforcement question. The objector could say: deprive-first has clear enforcement mechanisms (property law, police). Share-first seems to require some mechanism for determining and enforcing "necessity," which could be just as coercive.

"Necessity is contested." Who defines what counts as a necessary reason to restrict access? This is the same problem that plagues utilitarian ethics. You gesture at "whichever standard of necessity we agree on" but that agreement is doing a lot of work.

Minor Issues

The Russell Ackoff reference ("in Russell Ackoff's words, is not to solve the problem of oppression, but to dissolve it") is good but may be lost on readers unfamiliar with him. Consider whether it needs attribution or can stand as a general principle.

"Our vigil has lasted long enough. We've grieved the ongoing dearth of hope of a world we'd want to live in long enough." The construction "dearth of hope of" is awkward. Consider: "grieved the ongoing absence of hope for a world..."

What's Working

The definition. The alternative formulation. The categorization of objections. The refusal to let reformist alternatives off the hook. The directness of the invitation. The Squid Games image. The confident assertion that this is coherent and worth taking seriously.

What is the most fundamental building block unique to “civilized” societies? 

Language, which predates civilization by hundreds of thousands of years?

Hierarchy, which developed as a societal superstructure? 

The family unit, which predates society itself?

Ownership.

The vigil

For 10,000 - 15,000 years, every society we’ve called “civilized” has been built on ownership—but not only as its foundation. The canonical design of both the structure and dynamics of “civilized” societies revolves around ownership, it’s configured to enable and sustain ownership, and it aims to both manage and sustain the issues ownership incessantly raises. We could well say that society is the unsurprising outgrowth of collectively establishing, perpetuating, and defending ownership. Our social systems would not have gotten off the ground without ownership first being firmly entrenched as our foremost social treasure. In fact, in any case where something seriously threatens established ownership, everything simply stops, all hands on deck to confront the threat—and all else remains halted until the threat has been eliminated. 

No other single societal factor exerts the kind of impact or draws investment with the kind of gravity that belongs to ownership.

We bemoan wealth disparity, poverty, and the injustice and oppression they prove, but we sit, watch, and hope something will magically change—without any hope or intention of changing their true causes. We target symptoms and put in Herculean effort that results in minor change. We protest. We talk. This is the vigil. We do nothing, because we have no interest in changing the premise for all of it.

So, what exactly, in simple terms, do I mean by ownership?

Ownership is a particular kind of legal relationship between a party and a good, giving the “owner” unrestricted right to deprive the good from every other person on Earth. 

Sadly, the near-universal consensus today is that ownership is the only kind of sensible, worthwhile relationship between a party and a good. But strip away the legal overlays, the cultural variations, the philosophical justifications, and—at root—ownership implements a single principle:

First, deprive all—then share only if you want.

If a party owns a good, their relationship to it is absolutely exclusive. No other party can hold the rights to the good held by an owner unless ownership is first transferred, whether by legal or illegal means. The means of establishing ownership matters, ideally and hypothetically—but history shows incontestably that, in practice, the ways and means to ownership don’t matter—unless someone can prove their case to contest it. All that matters is the exclusive status as owner that results. Demonstrate that present status satisfactorily, and questions of provenance instantly become moot. Discovery, seizure, theft—the single thread running through every way and means to ownership is the establishment of legal recognition of the right to universal deprivation, regardless of actual ways and means. Convince the court, and it’s yours. Once established, we ignore ands forget the ways and means, and every single party on Earth becomes excluded from access to, control over, or enjoyment of the good unless the owner grants it to them. 

Most critiques of ownership not only don’t challenge this structure—they don’t even consider it challengeable. At most, they merely reform and/or redistribute it. Collective ownership, commons, public property—these reformulations of ownership only relocate the excluding party from individual to group, from private to state, or vice versa. The underlying principle of universal deprivation remains intact: some party controls, depriving all others except at the controller’s discretion.

For example, “sharing economies” don’t escape the paradigm of universal deprivation. You cannot share—in the typical sense of “share”—what you don’t already own. If you’re not the owner, it’s not yours to share. Joint ownership is just a flavor, not an alternative. “Gift” economies fare no better. Gifting doesn’t escape ownership—it presupposes it.

Sharing in the naturally human sense implies that no one owns, but everyone has access to, control over, and enjoyment of a good. We’ve been so deeply buried under the dogma of ownership, sharing like this seems like a thoroughly unrealistic, absurdly fantastic prospect.

So, there is one structural alternative. Try as you might to find another, but you’ll fail. 

First, share with all—then deprive only when absolutely necessary.

This isn’t a reformation or redistribution of ownership. It’s the eradication of ownership. It’s the displacement of a convention and a practice rooted—both psychologically and historically—in coercion, violence, and supremacism. 

Rooted in inhumanness.

We already do this.

No one seems to have a hope in hell of any such endeavors bearing fruit.

Objections

Every objection to share-first I’ve encountered—objections both to the mere prospect of considering an alternative to deprive-first and to experimenting with share-first specifically—falls into one of three categories: circular, misdirected, or premature.

Before I get to those objections, let’s clear the path of a few misunderstandings on what the universal share-first principle does not require:

technological abundance or post-scarcity conditions.

a mechanism for determining “necessary” to be specified in advance.

anyone’s agreement or buy-in before acknowledging that it’s coherent.

Principles come before programs. “How could that work?” and, “How could that be viable?” and other implementation questions are both premature and irrelevant until we have decided whether the principle itself is sound, coherent, and preferable to the alternative. Circular

“Human nature is selfish.”

“Tragedy of the commons.”

The “commons”—in Hardin’s formulation, for example—is a collectively-owned resource—a different configuration of deprivation, not an alternative to it. Reformed is not replaced. The “tragedy” arises from competing private interests exploiting a shared pool. Under share-first, there are no private interests seeking to deprive others by extracting from a pool. The scenario doesn’t even apply.

“It’s just communism.”

Communism relocates ownership from individuals to the collective or the state. The excluding party changes; the structure doesn’t. Communism is a reconfiguration of deprive-first, not a departure from it.

“Someone has to decide who gets what.”

Under scarcity, yes—allocation decisions must be made. But the question is: what is the default prior to the decision? Deprive-first says: no one has access until someone grants it. Share-first says: everyone has access until necessity requires restriction. The allocation problem exists under both paradigms. 

The real questions are which default we start from and which principle we operate under. 

Before that, the real question is which default and principle we want. 

We currently do not have the slightest bit of a base of data to intelligently compare the alternatives, neither to assess which works better, which is “viable”, or which results in a better outcome.

“People will take advantage.”

Some will, under any system. The question is whether the social structure itself generates and rewards exploitation, or whether it minimizes and isolates it. Will more people take advantage, more often, to greater harm under deprive-first than share-first? Deprive-first systematizes advantage-taking. It makes exclusive accumulation the path to “security” and “power,” which it prioritizes above collective welfare and benefit. Share-first removes that structural incentive. 

What kind of “security” would be required in a society where sharing is the norm, not the exception? What would “power” consist of, if needed at all—if coherent at all?

We need to start at the beginning: the principle is what’s on the table. Is share-first coherent? Is it preferable to deprive-first? These questions come before design and even proof-of-concept “testing”. If the answers are no, show the incoherence. Show the undesirability. If the answer is yes, then we have something to create—together.

Demanding a sketch of the structure before admitting any need for any structure at all isn’t serious inquiry. It’s an excuse to prolong the vigil so that we can avoid the terror of what waits for us outside.

It’s refusal to inquire at all, masquerading as shrewd, skeptical caution.

Collaboration Invitation

What would we gain by such a massive paradigm shift, escaping the deprivation-first Squid Games arena into open air and freedom?

Maybe the better question is: What would we not gain? And what would we that we wouldn’t be happy to get rid of?  Those would make far shorter lists. 

This isn’t about “Utopia”—because Utopia described a thoroughly authoritarian, deprive-first world. It’s not about some Pollyannaish sugar-plum visions of elimination of conflict, scarcity, or difficult decisions dancing in our heads. 

This is about a hard-tacks, structural shift with consequences:

Survival and security would not require accumulation. Under deprive-first, you secure your future by stockpiling and excluding—hoarding what you might need because access to it is never guaranteed. Under share-first, access is the default—so security doesn’t depend on your skills and machinations to deprive others.

Cooperation would not require enforcement. Deprive-first places people in survival competition for access to resources, so cooperation must be enforced or incentivized, going against the grain of the system itself. Under share-first, mutual access is the starting point. Cooperation becomes the path of least resistance, not an achievement wrested from hostile conditions.

“Power” would lose its primary lever. This is a huge one. Our very definition of “power”, for the most part, is domination and control by violence and the threat of violence. The most “powerful” organizations on the planet are States—monopolies on violence that they legitimate for themselves. Controlling others depends on controlling access to what they need. That control presupposes deprivation as the default—without it, there’s nothing to grant or withhold. Flip that default, and coercion loses its structural foundation. This, in Russell Ackoff’s words, is not to solve the problem of oppression, but to dissolve it. You can’t threaten to deprive someone of what they already have access to.

The question “who owns this?” would disappear. That question asks: who has the right to exclude and thereby deprive others? Under share-first, the question becomes: is there a necessary reason to restrict access to this? Maybe that’s a harder question—though I think we’ll find it’s the easier one, by far—but either way, it’s the right one.

It’s too soon to expect a blueprint. Share-first requires a foundation and direction switch. There is no intelligent discussion to be had about design if we can’t even honestly, seriously begin considering the switch.

Our vigil has lasted long enough. We’ve grieved the ongoing dearth of hope for a world we’d want to live in long enough. Enough with the “always with you” cynicism of incessant survival threat, mass oppression and violence, obscene disparities of living conditions and status, and the insane idea that some people deserve to live like gods while the rest of us crawl like worms. 

Our vigil has lasted long enough. We’ve built entire civilizations as psycho-social hospice centers, pretending death defines the culture—ignoring that we deliberately poison both the water and the air.

I’m looking for people unafraid to turn their heads away from the reigning Golden Calf—which on honest analysis was born of paranoid psychosis, which remains to be clearly shown—not to look away from the problem, but to look at it, perhaps for the first time, and to look outward and ahead. 

This is nothing more fantastic or incredible than standing up and walking out of Plato’s Cave.

If you can see the inversion, and you’re interested in honest, open, intelligent conversation about it—I want to hear from you.
