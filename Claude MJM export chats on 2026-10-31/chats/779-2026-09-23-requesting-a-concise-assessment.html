What is Israel's so called safe haven lawThere is no official Israeli law formally named the “Safe Haven Law.” The phrase is used critically (often as “so-called”) mainly in reference to Israel’s Law of Return (1950) and related immigration/extradition practices, particularly claims that they have been exploited by some Jewish individuals accused or convicted of sexual offenses abroad.
The Law of Return
The Law of Return is a foundational statute. It grants Jews (broadly defined to include those with at least one Jewish grandparent, plus certain spouses and converts) the right to immigrate to Israel (aliyah) and obtain citizenship. It was enacted to provide a homeland and refuge for Jews after the Holocaust and centuries of persecution.
It includes an exclusion for a person with a “criminal past, likely to endanger public welfare.” The Interior Ministry (and a special committee in some cases) decides what qualifies, but critics argue the definition and screening have been inconsistently or weakly applied, creating practical loopholes.
Criticisms involving sexual offenders
Advocacy groups (notably Jewish Community Watch) and investigative reporting (including a 2020 CBS News investigation) have documented cases of accused or convicted Jewish sex offenders from the United States and elsewhere moving to Israel. JCW has tracked more than 60 such cases from the US since 2014 (with estimates that the true number is higher); other Israeli and diaspora advocates have cited figures around 100 when including broader Orthodox communities and earlier cases.
Reasons cited for the phenomenon include:

Relatively straightforward access to citizenship or long-term residence under the Law of Return for those who qualify as Jewish.
Once citizenship is obtained, Israel generally does not extradite its own nationals (though exceptions occur under sustained pressure or specific agreements, as in some high-profile cases).
Gaps in sex-offender registration, public notification, and supervision compared with countries such as the US, UK, Canada, and Australia (noted in research by the Knesset Research and Information Center).

Notable examples discussed in reporting include Malka Leifer (former Australian school principal accused of abusing students; she fled to Israel and faced a lengthy extradition battle before being returned and later convicted) and others who entered as tourists or via aliyah and remained for years.
Israeli authorities have denied aliyah in some publicized cases (e.g., certain known abusers), and officials and media (including The Jerusalem Post) have stated that Israel should not serve as a haven for sex criminals. Activists continue to push for stricter pre-immigration screening, a more robust public sex-offender registry, better inter-agency coordination, and clearer statutory definitions of “danger to the public.”
Other uses of “safe haven”

Israel is frequently described rhetorically as a “safe haven” for the Jewish people under the Law of Return—this is the original positive framing.
There are laws and building standards requiring mamad (reinforced safe rooms) in new construction for protection against rockets/missiles; these are sometimes called safe rooms or shelters but are not a “safe haven law.”
Separate issues exist around asylum seekers/infiltrators (under the Prevention of Infiltration Law and Entry into Israel Law) and other topics, but these are unrelated to the “so-called safe haven law” phrasing in the sex-offender context.

In short, the “so-called safe haven law” is not a formal statute. It is a critical label applied mainly to how the Law of Return and related practices have, according to documented cases and advocates, enabled some individuals accused of serious sexual crimes to relocate to Israel and complicate efforts to hold them accountable in their countries of origin.
