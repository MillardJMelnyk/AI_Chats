Meaning of "Confusing X for Y"

when saying "confusing X for Y", which is the false one being taken for the real one?
Claude2025-08-09 14:51 UTC

In the phrase "confusing X for Y," X is the false one (or the incorrect thing) that is being mistaken for Y, which is the real/correct one.

For example:

  • "I confused fool's gold for real gold" - fool's gold is the false thing, real gold is what you mistakenly thought it was
  • "She confused her twin sister for her" - the twin sister is who was actually there, but was mistaken for "her" (the real person being referenced)
  • "Don't confuse correlation for causation" - correlation is the misleading thing that shouldn't be mistaken for causation (the more meaningful relationship)

The structure is: confusing [what you actually encountered] for [what you thought it was].

Claude2025-08-09 14:52 UTC

Actually, no - it's not a completely hard and fast rule. The phrase can be ambiguous and context-dependent.

While my explanation covers the most common usage, "confusing X for Y" can sometimes work both ways:

More common: X is what you encountered, Y is what you mistook it for

  • "I confused him for his brother" (I saw him, thought he was his brother)

Less common but possible: X is what you were looking for, Y is what you actually found

  • In some contexts, people might say "I confused the real diamond for a fake" meaning they had a real diamond but thought it was fake

The ambiguity often gets resolved by:

  • Context clues
  • Additional words ("mistakenly confused," "accidentally confused")
  • The specific nouns used (some combinations are more naturally interpreted one way)

So while there's a strong tendency toward the pattern I described first, it's not absolute. When in doubt, the surrounding context usually makes the intended meaning clear.

Claude2025-08-09 14:55 UTC

Ah, interesting! "Confusing X with Y" does work differently than "confusing X for Y."

With "confusing X with Y," the relationship is more symmetrical - it typically means you're mixing up two things without necessarily implying that one is "real" and the other is a mistaken interpretation. You're just failing to distinguish between them properly.

Examples:

  • "I always confuse John with his twin brother" - could mean you mistake John for his brother OR mistake his brother for John
  • "Don't confuse correlation with causation" - you're mixing up two distinct concepts
  • "I confused salt with sugar while cooking" - you grabbed one when you meant to grab the other

So while "confusing X for Y" has that directional pattern (X is present, Y is the misidentification), "confusing X with Y" is more about failing to properly distinguish between two things, without a clear directional implication about which one you actually encountered.

The "with" version suggests mutual confusion or mixing up, while the "for" version suggests mistaken identity in a specific direction.
