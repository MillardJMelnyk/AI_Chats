Millard2025-06-03 16:45 UTC
The as-experienced artifacts of our interactions with real-world phenomena do not carry any built-in connection to what we experienced. We experienced something, yes, leaving us with experiential artifacts: the memories, impressions, and emotions resulting from an experience of certain real-world phenomena. We associate these artifacts to the real world phenomena we experienced--*we* do that--but no inherent guarantees exist that our associations correspond properly, accurately, or functionally to the phenomena we connect them to. It takes additional work completely apart from the experience itself--work that *we* must do--to ensure that the as-experienced phenomena in our heads resemble and actually refer to the real-world, as-is phenomena we associate them with. This strikes me as a fundamental insight about the epistemic gap between experience and reality. You're highlighting something crucial: that the phenomenological content of our experience - those memories, impressions, and emotions - doesn't come with built-in verification stamps saying "this accurately represents external reality."

The work you mention - that additional effort to establish correspondence between our experiential artifacts and actual phenomena - seems to be what we might call the work of knowledge. This connects to some deep philosophical questions. When I have what feels like an experience of understanding your words, for instance, there's no inherent guarantee that my internal process actually corresponds to what you meant to convey. The meaning I construct might be quite different from your intended meaning, even if my experience feels coherent and complete to me.

What's particularly interesting is how this applies to memory over time. Even experiences we're confident about can drift from their original referents without us noticing. Our experiential artifacts can maintain their felt sense of connection to reality while potentially becoming increasingly detached from the actual phenomena they supposedly represent.

Do you see this gap as something we can ever fully bridge, or is it more about developing better practices for maintaining approximate correspondence? And I'm curious whether you think this has implications for how we should approach knowledge claims more generally.

Claude2025-06-03 16:49 UTC

A few significant criticisms come to mind:

The verification problem: If our experiential artifacts don't inherently connect to reality, then the "additional work" to verify correspondence faces the same problem. How do we know our verification processes themselves accurately connect to reality? This could lead to infinite regress - we'd need to verify our verification methods, then verify those verifications, and so on.

Pragmatic objection: While philosophically interesting, this view might be practically paralyzing. If we truly can't trust the basic correspondence between experience and reality, how do we function? Our ability to navigate the world suggests some reliable connection exists, even if imperfect.

Evolutionary argument: Natural selection would presumably favor organisms whose experiential artifacts correspond reasonably well to reality. Creatures whose internal representations were completely divorced from external phenomena wouldn't survive long. This suggests some built-in reliability, even if not perfect.

The coherence challenge: Your view seems to assume we can meaningfully distinguish between "experiential artifacts" and "real-world phenomena" - but if all our access to the world is through experience, this distinction might be incoherent. We might not be able to step outside experience to make this comparison.

Degrees vs. binaries: Perhaps the issue isn't that correspondence is absent and must be built from scratch, but that it exists in degrees and can be improved. This would make the problem more tractable without dismissing your core insight.

Which of these resonates as the strongest challenge to your framework?
