Kathryn Schulz covers a phenomenon in her book *Being Wrong -- Adventures in the Margin of Error* where things that are actually quite h far away appear much nearer. Explorers looking for a northwest passage were taken in by it. What is it called?
Claude2025-12-02 22:21 UTC

I need to search for the specific term Schulz uses in her book for this phenomenon.

Superior mirage (also called an Arctic mirage).

Schulz also refers to it as a "Fata Morgana" in the specific context of the Crocker Land deception—a type of superior mirage where atmospheric refraction makes distant objects appear much closer and distorted into mountainous forms.

Millard2025-12-02 22:28 UTC
Does it actually enable features that are geometrically below the horizon to appear as if they're closer than the righhorizon?
Claude2025-12-02 22:28 UTC

Yes. Superior mirages occur when cold air near the surface is topped by warmer air (temperature inversion). Light rays bend downward as they pass through these density layers, effectively following Earth's curvature more than usual. This allows objects that are geometrically beyond the horizon—hidden by Earth's curvature—to become visible.

The phenomenon both:

  1. Brings sub-horizon objects into view (extends visual range beyond geometric horizon)
  2. Distorts apparent distance, often making them appear closer than they actually are

In Ross's 1818 case: mountains 200 miles away (well below his geometric horizon) appeared as nearby peaks ~27 miles distant. The light path curved enough to bring them into view, while the atmospheric distortion compressed the perceived distance.
