Based on the declassified documents and historical evidence, the U.S. role in the Rwandan genocide was not one of "fomenting" or instigating the genocide, but rather one of deliberate inaction and preventing international intervention. Here's what the evidence shows:

The U.S. Role: Inaction Rather Than Instigation

The U.S. did not foment or cause the Rwandan genocide. The genocide was planned and executed by Rwandan extremist Hutu groups, particularly around the government of President Juvénal Habyarimana. However, the U.S. response was characterized by:

1. Deliberate Prevention of UN Intervention The U.S. used its influence at the United Nations to discourage a robust UN response, instructing diplomats that "the international community must give highest priority to full, orderly withdrawal of all UNAMIR personnel as soon as possible" and that "we will oppose any effort at this time to preserve a UNAMIR presence in Rwanda."

2. Early Knowledge and Warning Signs Ignored UN Force Commander Romeo Dallaire had warned in January 1994 of arms caches, plots to assassinate Belgian peacekeepers, and lists of Tutsis to be killed, but UN officials refused his request to raid the weapons caches.

3. Avoiding the "Genocide" Label The U.S. government engaged in semantic gymnastics to avoid using the word "genocide" for weeks, knowing that officially acknowledging genocide would create pressure for intervention under international law. Internal State Department memos show officials discussing how "Genocide finding could commit USG to do something" and debating whether to use formulations like "acts of genocide have occurred" versus "genocide has occurred."

4. Intelligence Agencies Understood the Scope Pentagon analysts concluded early on that "if the peace process fails, a massive bloodbath (hundreds of thousands of deaths) will ensue" and that "the US will not get involved until peace is restored." Intelligence reports from May 1994 clearly identified that "the violence is directed by high-level officials within the interim government" and that the army was "pursuing a genocide to destroy the leadership of the Tutsi community."

5. Blocking Practical Interventions The U.S. even opposed relatively low-cost measures like jamming the extremist radio broadcasts that were coordinating the killings, with Pentagon officials calling such efforts "ineffective and expensive."

Historical Context

The roots of the genocide lie in Rwanda's colonial history under Belgian rule, which institutionalized ethnic divisions between Hutus and Tutsis, and the 1990-1994 civil war between the government and the Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF). The immediate trigger was the shooting down of President Habyarimana's plane on April 6, 1994.

The U.S. failure was not in causing the genocide, but in deliberately choosing not to prevent it despite having clear advance warning and the capability to intervene or support international intervention. This represented a policy choice following the Somalia intervention disaster in 1993, where the Clinton administration had become deeply reluctant to engage in African conflicts.
