You're asking about a grammatical choice between "zero tolerance of atrocities" and "zero tolerance toward atrocities." Both prepositions work, though they carry slightly different connotations:

"Zero tolerance of atrocities" suggests the atrocities themselves are what cannot be tolerated - it focuses on the acts as inherently intolerable.

"Zero tolerance toward atrocities" emphasizes the directional stance or attitude one takes in relation to atrocities - it's about the orientation of one's response.

In practice, both are widely used and understood. "Zero tolerance of" is more common in formal policy language, while "toward" might feel slightly more active or directional.

Your broader point about the erosion of moral standards being itself a form of evil touches on an important ethical concept - that moral relativism or the gradual acceptance of previously unacceptable acts can constitute its own form of wrongdoing. This idea has been explored by various philosophers and ethicists who argue that societies have an obligation to maintain certain moral boundaries.

Claude2025-08-18 20:18 UTC

"Zero tolerance of atrocities" works better in your phrase.

The construction "zero tolerance of" is the standard, established idiom. It's what readers expect and what sounds most natural. While "toward" is grammatically possible, it creates unnecessary friction in an otherwise powerful statement.

Your phrase has strong rhetorical impact - "zero tolerance of" maintains that flow and keeps the focus on your main argument about moral erosion rather than making readers pause over an unusual preposition choice.
