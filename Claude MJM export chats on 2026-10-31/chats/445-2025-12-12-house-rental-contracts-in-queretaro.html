No, it is not a crime.

Being an undocumented migrant is not a crime. The UN Committee on the Rights of Migrant Workers has stated that irregular migration can only be an administrative violation, and therefore no criminal sanction can be imposed.

Under Mexican law, "in no case does an irregular migratory status, by itself, constitute a crime, nor will the commission of offenses be prejudged against a migrant for being in an undocumented condition."

The Ley de Migración establishes that irregular migration is NOT a crime, but rather an administrative violation.

Consequences:

  • Administrative fine (variable, typically payable at departure)
  • Possible detention in estación migratoria (immigration facility) for processing
  • Potential deportation or retorno asistido (assisted return) in extended cases
  • Possible impact on future entry decisions (at officer discretion)

Not consequences:

  • Criminal prosecution
  • Criminal record
  • Imprisonment (for overstay alone)

explain "The risk is that the pagaré is enforceable independently via juicio ejecutivo mercantil even if you dispute the underlying damage claim in the lease context."
Claude2025-12-12 22:10 UTC

A pagaré is a título de crédito (negotiable instrument) governed by the Ley General de Títulos y Operaciones de Crédito, not civil contract law.

What this means procedurally:

  1. Juicio ejecutivo mercantil is a fast-track commercial collection proceeding. The pagaré itself is sufficient proof of debt. The court can order asset seizure and payment without first proving the underlying obligation.

  2. In a lease dispute over damages, you would normally argue: "I didn't cause damage" or "The damage was pre-existing" or "The claimed amount is inflated." These are factual defenses requiring evidence, witnesses, inspection reports, etc.

  3. In a juicio ejecutivo mercantil on the pagaré, the landlord doesn't need to prove damage occurred. The signed pagaré is the debt. Your defenses are limited to defects in the instrument itself (forgery, alteration, payment already made, prescription).

The practical problem:

You sign a pagaré for $55,000 as "damage security." A year later, the landlord claims $30,000 in damages you dispute. Instead of suing you under the lease (where you could defend on the merits), the landlord takes the pagaré to court. The judge sees a valid signed instrument and orders collection. Your argument that "the damage didn't happen" is largely irrelevant to the pagaré proceeding.

You would need to file a separate civil action to recover the money—after already paying.

Isn't there renter's insurance available in Mexico?
Claude2025-12-12 23:09 UTC

The Mexican market differs significantly from the US model.

What exists:

  1. Seguro de arrendamiento / Póliza jurídica — Protects the landlord, not the tenant. Covers rent default, legal fees for eviction, sometimes unpaid utilities. The premium is paid by the landlord, who is the insured and beneficiary.

  2. Seguro de hogar para inquilinos — Protects the tenant's belongings (furniture, electronics, clothing) against fire, flood, earthquake, theft. Does not cover the structure or damage to the landlord's property.

  3. Combined product (new, late 2024) — MoradaUno formed an alliance with Allianz in the last quarter of 2024 to create "Arrendamiento Uno," described as the first insurance in Mexico designed 100% for both landlords and tenants, including home coverage and civil liability that protects both parties.

The gap:

In Mexico, only 5.5% of dwellings have any type of insurance or protection, according to AMIS figures. There is no established equivalent to US-style renter's insurance with liability coverage for damage to the landlord's property as a standard product.

Relevance to your situation:

A responsabilidad civil (liability) policy covering damage you cause to the property could theoretically replace the pagaré function—but this is not standard practice in Mexico, and landlords are unlikely to accept it in lieu of traditional guarantees (deposit + pagaré + fiador).
