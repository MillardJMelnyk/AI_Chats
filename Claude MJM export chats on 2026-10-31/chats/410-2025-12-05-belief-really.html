The difference between a a belief and a mere abstraction lies in their durability. Even the word “abstraction” connotes something immaterial, iffy, provisional, and derived. An abstraction is a kind of “thing” that has a kind of quasi-existence—enough so that our minds accept it as a mental proxy for whatever it refers to. It might or might not adequately represent what it refers to, depending on our purposes for it. 

A photo of a loved one is not the loved one, but it proxies for them enough to evoke feelings for them. We might love the photo, but we don’t confuse our love for the photo with our love for our loved one it represents. The photo exists, so we could say the image of our loved one exists, but we don’t confuse the existence of an image with the existence of its subject. 

Someone might spite us by drawing an absurd goatee on the photo of our loved one’s face, but that would have no effect whatsoever on our loved one’s actual face. The photo could be torn up or mutilated or burned without our loved one feeling the slightest discomfort. 

All the discomfort over these actions would be ours. 

Still, we wouldn’t confuse that discomfort with the discomfort we’d feel if someone actually harmed our loved one. 

None of this is especially complicated—we just don’t normally think about things at this level of detail.

Criticisms:

  1. "Purpose" is ungrounded. You claim the "preeminent purpose" of belief is separability from reality. Purpose implies intent or design. Whose? This isn't specified. You may mean function, but that's a different claim requiring different support.

  2. The belief/abstraction distinction is gestural, not operational. "Durability" is the proposed differentiator, but you provide no threshold, metric, or test. At what point does an abstraction become a belief? Without operationalization, this is unfalsifiable.

  3. "All beliefs are abstractions" is asserted, not argued. Is "I believe there's a wall behind me" an abstraction in the same sense as the word "elephant"? The former is a propositional attitude about immediate reality; the latter is a semantic symbol. You've conflated categories.

  4. The "privileged abstraction" framing is circular. You ask why beliefs generate conflict while abstractions don't—but if conflict-generation is what distinguishes beliefs, then you've defined beliefs by conflict-generation. The argument becomes: "Beliefs cause conflict because they're the kind of abstractions that cause conflict."

  5. Your empirical claim is false. "When was the last time you got in an argument over an abstraction?" People argue constantly over abstractions: what counts as "art," "racism," "justice," "terrorism." These disputes are definitional/categorical—pure abstraction warfare.

  6. The photo analogy undermines your thesis. You argue we don't confuse photo-damage with person-damage. But then belief-attack should likewise be distinguishable from reality-attack. Your analogy predicts beliefs should behave like other abstractions, not differently.

  7. No mechanism provided. You identify that beliefs generate "fireworks" but never explain why. What confers the privilege? Identity investment? Social signaling? Without mechanism, you've described a phenomenon, not explained it.

  8. "Fixed" misdescribes belief. Beliefs update. Bayesian epistemology models beliefs as probability distributions that shift with evidence. Your characterization describes pathological or rigid belief, then generalizes to all belief.

Core problem: The document asserts a distinction (belief vs. abstraction) without specifying necessary and sufficient conditions for membership in either category.

But "state of affairs" is itself philosophical jargon that may carry unwanted connotations from analytic philosophy.

"Actuality" or "the actual" might split the difference—grounded, direct, but not colloquial.

Millard2025-12-08 14:37 UTC
Are all abstractions representative, referring to something in actuality or in the abstract to which they bear a referential relationship?
Claude2025-12-08 14:37 UTC

No. Formal constructs (mathematical objects, logical operators, rules of games) don't refer to actuality or other abstractions. They're stipulated into existence, not derived from anything they represent.

Millard2025-12-08 14:38 UTC
are all those constructs axioms?
Claude2025-12-08 14:38 UTC

No. Axioms are foundational stipulations. But formal constructs also include definitions, theorems (derived from axioms), operators, and rules. Not all are axiomatic—some are derived, some are operational.

Millard2025-12-08 14:40 UTC
That seems like a hugely important distinction. Which thinkers have made it clear?
Claude2025-12-08 14:41 UTC

I'm not confident any thinker has made this distinction clearly and directly. Closest candidates:

  • Vaihinger ("Philosophy of As If") — treated many concepts as useful fictions, but didn't cleanly separate referential from imaginary
  • Logical positivists — analytic/synthetic distinction, but that's about truth-conditions, not referentiality
  • Fictionalists in philosophy of mathematics (Field) — treat math as non-referring, but as a polemic position, not a general taxonomy

The distinction may be obvious enough that it's been assumed rather than articulated—or it's been obscured by debates (nominalism vs. realism, analytic vs. synthetic) that frame the question differently.
