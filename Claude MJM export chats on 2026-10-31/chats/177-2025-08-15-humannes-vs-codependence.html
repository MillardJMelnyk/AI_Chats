Just hysterical
Gilligan would say that shame, disrespect, or lack of self-respect give rise to psychological and physical violence, but those are terms we use to describe our experience of the raw psychological forces involved. People don’t get violent because someone dissed them. That’s what they say about what made them violent. That’s what they call it. But what is it? What’s the nature of the actual forces? What induces people to respond violently when someone disses them? Sure, violence revolves around psychological lacks and problems, but what is the psychic impetus that pushes them to resort to the last refuge of the incompetent? When dissed, what makes a person lose all grasp of their rational faculties and their humanness and fall into the demented abyss of violence?
I can capture the raw motive drive in a single word: hysteria.
No matter what “evil” you look at—violence, seduction, extortion, fraud, exploitation, dehumanization, mass murder (“war”), mass rape (“war”), mass mayhem (“war”), massive and wanton destruction (“war”), genocide, ethnocide, orchestrated starvation, poverty (completely orchestrated), take your pick—the driving force behind them all, the single energetic impetus which, if eliminated, would rip down the sails that propel them and pop the balloons that buoy them is: hysteria. 
Don't take me to mean hysteria as histrionics. I'm talking about a far deeper and primal form of hysteria, one that drives us away from sanity and humanness on levels well beneath the floor of consciousness, whether it induces hysterical behavior or not.
Whether megalomaniacal or existentially terrified, no one gets violent until they become hysterical. And no one does evil until they've gotten violent.
Eliminate hysteria, and you’ll have blown away the fogs of frantic desperation on which “evil” gets projected.
Everything that has merited the label “evil”, as far as I’ve been able to ascertain after almost 60 years of investigation, (it’s been a lifelong quest for understanding,) resulted wholly-and-only from hysteria: meaning haywire subliminal psychological processes that bear no connection to what’s really going on in the here and now but erupt into consciousness with such immanent and irresistible force that we take their manifestations as eminently, self-evidently, unquestionably real and true merely because they erupted.
Hysteria puts us into a frantic, driven mode where the detachment required for self-awareness, reflection, critical thinking, and judgment is impossible. Self-awareness becomes self-cluelessness, reflection becomes projection, critical thinking becomes infantilistic tell-me-what-to-do, judgment becomes knee-jerk, and the only question we’re able to entertain is, “How high?” when our hysteria screams, “JUMP!”
Hysteria, in this reclaimed sense that has shitall to do with its historical usage, is illusionary by definition—delusionary, for the most part. The idea includes effects of psychoactive substances that alter our perception, mood, or cognition in such a way as to create fear and panic.
Replace every usage of “evil” with “hysteria” in this sense and not only won’t you lose anything meaningful or significant, but you’ll demystify the matter and open the door to a far saner, more effective understanding of what’s going on than “evil” ever afforded.
Why not include broken-from-reality illusions in the set of all things hysterical when they induce euphoric, enjoyable experience, including those chemically induced? Simple reason: no fear and panic.
Since English has no umbrella term counterpart to “hysteria” for positive, enjoyable mental states that are similarly disconnected and distorted, (a telling fact,) I’ll call this counterpart to hysteria hyperia: a state of euphoric rupture where expansive, reality-distorting neuroprocesses alter or override perception. Think of it as a rapturous rupture from reality.
Oh, the tales that huge linguistic omissions tell about the mentality of the culture that limps along despite them!
Hyperia’s open, embracing, and (often) one-with-the-universe enthrall is the antithesis of hysteria’s constrictive fear and panic. Hysteria closes us in on ourselves in defense against perceived calamity with no thought about the credibility of the “self-evident” perception. Although similarly credulous, hyperia goes the other direction, motivating us towards exploration, absorption, embrace, connection, and a sense of immediate oneness, even if only with ourselves, tending toward and often transcending conventional boundaries.
The characteristic effect of hyperical experience—an MDMA/ecstasy trip, for example—is to open us up to life and the world and engage in them once we’ve come down: precisely the opposite of the effect of hysterical experience.
Hysteria and hyperia both involve some degree of break from reality, some degree of perceptual distortion—but, by quite different neurological means, take us in opposite directions. Beyond that, they bear each other little resemblance. In terms of “evil”, they couldn’t be more different: “evil” (as a malevolent, destructive force tending towards calamity) is inextricably linked with hysteria. You can look far and wide in hyperical human experience and nary find a trace of it.
Occult?
I consider “occult” as neutral in terms of good/evil, in the same way that “darkness” is neutral. The same occult prospect that scares and repels some people—implying threat, potential danger/harm, and infection by the malevolent—affects others oppositely: implying wonder, welcome potential, enticing and beckoning them to explore and engage, often irresistibly. We climb mountains and spelunk claustrophobic spaces and foray into the most occult of all—space—“just because it’s there”. Our sense that the occult is sinister or that “evil” lurks “out there in it” is hysterical. Once we go beyond fantasy and actually enter into the unclear, obscure, mysterious, ineffable, of which we have only slight, vague awareness, taking action to engage with it with the purpose of enlightening it, discovering it, clarifying it, revealing it, what we find in “the occult” turns out to be overwhelmingly good.
The occult is a huge part of all our lives. I think of it as twilight, or the dawn of initial awareness before actual encounter. We are constantly affected and must deal with real things and forces—real phenomena—which to us are unclear, obscure, mysterious, ineffable, operating beyond our horizon of clear perception, which is all that “occult” actually means.
The rest of the stuff we’ve impregnated the term with (thanks so much, religion!) is not real at all—so far as we know—and I’ve never seen a non-hysterical, functional, beneficial reason convincing enough to regard any of that shit to be real.
I’ll get deeper into the occult later, but for my purposes at this point, the occult as I have it in mind includes only real phenomena. Otherwise, by definition, there exists no such phenomenon to include. Our awareness (or lack of it) about real phenomena has no bearing on the question of whether or not they’re real. If they’re not real, they can’t be found in the occult because there’s nothing to find. In other words: being unreal, they’re not occult—they’re imaginary.
I use “occult” to refer to the hinterlands of our awareness of reality, not the realites we’re barely aware of. The occult is not a realm that’s “out there” containing some weird, iffy, special kind of stuff. It's just the dusk/dawn between awareness and obliviousness. It's a cognitive zone within us, the perceiving subjects, completely in our heads—not a characteristic or context of phenomena we’re perceiving. Things in our murky mental zones are dark, fuzzy, hardly discernible, i.e., occulted—not because of what they are, but because of our meager, feeble awareness and grasp of what they are—which says plenty about us but nothing about them. But we’re not oblivious to them. We’re aware that something seems to be there.
Both questions of whether, in fact, something’s there and questions of whether it does, in fact, resemble the fuzzy, obscured silhouettes we can barely make out, are wholly irrelevant to the cognitive state into which impressions of strange, barely discernible phenomena put our minds. The things as we hold them in that mental zone are not “occult things”, because at different points any and all of them move through our dusk into forgetfulness or through our dawn into consciousness as the self-same phenomena both before and after. It’s a cognitive foible to apply the label “occult” to them, as if it defined or described objects of our perception instead of our own state of ignorance of them—as if there were entities and forces which by nature are unclear, obscure, mysterious, and ineffable. No—that’s an unnecessary, unjustified leap. They’re unclear, obscure, mysterious, and ineffable to us. We designate them as “occult”—not by virtue of their nature, but by virtue that we’re unfamiliar, unclear, or lack informative awareness about them, whatever the reason for that might be.
The rest of the stuff that people typically think resides “in the occult”, then, resides nowhere at all. It’s unreal, fantasized, delusionary, i.e., superstitious. The important question isn’t whether something “in the occult” is real or not, but how we paint what we think is there. That is what affects us, there or not. Real or not, we are the ones who paint it with superstition or refrain from painting it and bear the dissonance of reserving judgment. Coupled with the sense of threat, already mentioned, which vague awareness of the nebulous, darkened, resistant to clarity always conjures in codependent minds, in turn triggering some degree of fear and, when it’s gone far enough, panic: it all comes down to hysteria.
Anatomy of a freak-out
Hysteria involves three functional components: dissociation, delusion, and fear.
Dissociation results from a self-preserving need to detach from reality. It involves a sense or realization of two things, regardless of whether they’re accurate or have any factual basis: imminent threat coupled with our sense of incompetence to deal with the threat successfully.
No fear-inducing threat? No dissociation. Competence present? No dissociation. People who feel competent to deal with threat don’t dissociate, they engage—like an expert does when challenged about their expertise. They might strategically or tactically choose not to engage with a particular risky, dangerous matter at a particular place and time… but when they are interested—whatever extreme sport or exploration or combat or personal issue it might be, from boxing and martial arts to plumbing ocean depths to space exploration to financial trading to gambling to facing romantic failure to dealing with rejection to surviving social erasure—they demonstrate that competent people love to face the risk/danger of challenges when they want to achieve or overcome or “win” and feel competent to do it.
Delusion is the natural result of having dissociated. Just like how your eye fills in the blind spot where your optic nerves connect to your retinas, and like we instinctively (and compulsively) fill in gaps in conversation or in our grasp of what’s going on—even if it takes making baseless assumptions to do it—our minds don’t deal very well with “nothing”. So, robbed of a picture of what’s really going on by our detachment from it: our minds make up their own images, insert them, and then pretend that the imaginary is as real as the original we Photoshopped it into.
When we get hysterical, dissociated from what’s really going on and, in turn, become deluded, driven by fear and panic, we do stupid, crazy, sometimes extremely harmful things. But we’re not being driven by something real—rather, we’re driven by something utterly unreal, completely imaginary, immaterial, and obscenely irrelevant to what’s really going on. The really-real
Instead of striking “evil” from our lexicon, I’ve redeemed and resurrected it. Otherwise, a huge swath of human experience would stand naked, lexically invisible, excluded from conversation and discussion for mere lack of a name.
