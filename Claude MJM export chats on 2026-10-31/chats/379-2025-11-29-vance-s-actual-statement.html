At a Turning Point USA event on October 29, 2025, Vance made these statements:

"When the Settlers came to the new world, they found a very widespread child sacrifice...There is a reason why Christian civilization ended the practice of child sacrifice all over the world, and it's one of the great accomplishments of Christian civilization."

He also stated: "What did the Christian settlers find when they came to the new world? They found a lot of civilizations that were murdering babies in weird religious rituals. It was Christianity that said, 'We don't kill children just because they're somehow inconvenient to people.'"

What experts say:

Archaeologists and historians describe no evidence of anything like "widespread child sacrifice" as a defining feature of North American Indigenous life. When human sacrifice appears in the record, it is rare, localized, and nothing like the sweeping caricature Vance describes.

Archaeological evidence has revealed very few unambiguous examples of human sacrifice among North American Indigenous peoples—the most generous estimates suggest a few thousand over several centuries. Human sacrifice in North America was generally unheard of.

The image's factual claim about what Vance said is accurate. The historical claim about widespread child sacrifice is disputed by archaeologists and historians.

Claude2025-11-29 14:24 UTC

Yes, that's the geographic distinction the sources make. While there is archaeological evidence of widespread child sacrifice in Mesoamerican cultures (such as Aztec, Maya) and some South American cultures, there is little evidence of this in North American indigenous societies.

Human sacrifice in North America was generally unheard of, and the few locations where it has been hypothesized are archaeologically ambiguous at best.

Even in Mesoamerica where it did occur, the sources emphasize it was exaggerated by Spanish colonial accounts. Many of those sources exaggerated what they saw to paint a picture of tens, perhaps hundreds of thousands of victims, much of which was an intentional effort to justify brutalizing, converting and conquering indigenous nations. Archaeological evidence has revealed very few unambiguous examples—the most generous estimates suggest a few thousand over several centuries.

So Vance's claim conflates geographically distinct regions and inflates the actual practice even where it did exist.
