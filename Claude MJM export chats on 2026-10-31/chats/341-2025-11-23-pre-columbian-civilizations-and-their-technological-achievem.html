I'll search for detailed information on pre-Columbian civilizations to provide accurate population estimates and social structure details.

Based on the searches, here's what the archaeological evidence shows:

Major Pre-Columbian American Civilizations

Cahokia (Mississippian Culture)

  • Population: Peak 10,000-20,000 (c. 1100-1200 CE)
  • Ruling echelon: Yes - hierarchical society with chiefs, nobles, priests
  • Warfare: Yes - evidence of defensive palisades, warfare with neighboring groups, human sacrifice of 53 young women at Mound 72
  • Human sacrifice: Yes - documented mass burials, ritual sacrifices

Teotihuacan

  • Population: Peak 100,000-200,000 (c. 250-600 CE)
  • Ruling echelon: Yes - priests held most power, religious leaders were political leaders, clear social stratification
  • Warfare: Yes - sacrifices of captured warriors, evidence of military activity
  • Human sacrifice: Yes - approximately 200 people sacrificed at Feathered Serpent Pyramid, prisoners of war sacrificed

Tenochtitlan (Aztec/Mexica)

  • Population: 140,000-200,000+ at peak (early 1500s)
  • Ruling echelon: Yes - rigid hierarchy: emperor (Huey Tlatoani), nobles (pipiltin), priests, warriors, commoners (macehualtin), slaves
  • Warfare: Yes - militaristic empire, constant warfare for expansion and captives
  • Human sacrifice: Yes - extensive practice, estimated thousands to tens of thousands annually

Cusco/Inca Empire

  • Population: Empire ~10 million; capital city exact population unclear, 40,000 Inca governed the empire
  • Ruling echelon: Yes - absolute ruler (Sapa Inca), nobles (panaqa), strict hierarchy
  • Warfare: Yes - military conquest was primary means of expansion
  • Human sacrifice: Yes - capacocha (child sacrifice), though less frequent than Aztecs; up to 4,000 killed upon death of Huayna Capac

Maya Civilization (multiple city-states)

  • Population: Peak ~2 million total; individual cities 5,000-50,000 each
  • Ruling echelon: Yes - hereditary kings (k'uhul ahaw), nobles, priests, warriors; commoners ~90% of population
  • Warfare: Yes - constant inter-city warfare, taking of captives
  • Human sacrifice: Yes - prisoners of war, nobility, ritual decapitation, heart extraction

Other significant civilizations mentioned:

  • Moche (Peru, c. 100-800 CE): Had rulers, practiced human sacrifice
  • Chimor/Chan Chan (Peru, c. 900-1470 CE): Had ruling class, practiced warfare
  • Tiwanaku (Bolivia, c. 300-1000 CE): Had elite class, evidence of human sacrifice

Regarding the Specific Claims:

Irrigation: ✓ YES - documented across all major civilizations

Sewage systems: PARTIAL - Tenochtitlan had sophisticated systems; others had varying levels

Metallurgy: ✓ YES - particularly South American civilizations (gold, copper, bronze)

Economic systems: ✓ YES - all had complex trade networks and economic systems

"Forms of electricity": ✗ NO archaeological evidence - The only ancient "battery" claim is the Baghdad Battery from Iraq (not Americas), and this interpretation is rejected by archaeologists

"Astrological, scientific and medical knowledge thousands of years ahead of Europe": EXAGGERATION - These civilizations had sophisticated astronomical calendars and medical knowledge, but "thousands of years ahead" is not supported. Knowledge systems were different, not categorically superior or inferior.

"Knowledge of energies akin to nuclear power": ✗ NO archaeological or credible evidence

Summary:

Every major pre-Columbian civilization listed had:

  • ✓ Hierarchical ruling classes with power concentrated in elites
  • ✓ Engaged in warfare
  • ✓ Practiced human sacrifice (frequency varied)

None operated on principles of "cooperation and humility" without hierarchy, warfare, or coercion, contrary to your friend's claims.

You're absolutely correct. Let me provide the updated information on both Cusco and Quito:

Cusco (Inca Empire Capital)

  • Population: Capital of an empire of ~10 million people; ~40,000 Inca nobles/administrators governed the empire
  • Ruling echelon: YES - Absolute monarch (Sapa Inca treated as divine), nobility (panaqa), strict hierarchical system
  • Warfare: YES - Military conquest was the primary means of Inca expansion from 1438-1532 CE
  • Human sacrifice: YES - Capacocha (child sacrifice) ceremonies, sacrifice of retainers (up to 4,000 upon death of Huayna Capac in 1527), ritual sacrifices for state events

Quito (Northern Inca Capital)

  • Population: Pre-Columbian population figures not well documented; became second Inca capital under Huayna Capac
  • Pre-Inca period: Inhabited by Quitu people, then Kingdom of Quito (~980 CE), conquered by Inca 1463-1487
  • Ruling echelon: YES - First under local kingdoms, then incorporated into Inca hierarchical system after conquest
  • Warfare: YES - The Quitu and Cañari people fought "fierce resistance" against Inca conquest; after incorporation, subject to Inca military system
  • Human sacrifice: YES - After Inca conquest, subject to same capacocha and other sacrificial practices as rest of empire

Key Point:

Both Cusco and Quito operated under the Inca imperial system which featured:

  • Absolute monarchy with divine claims
  • Rigid social hierarchy
  • Military expansion through conquest
  • Human sacrifice (though less frequent than Aztecs)

The claim that these societies operated through "cooperation and humility" without "slavery, genocide, colonization or theft of land and resources" is contradicted by archaeological and historical evidence - the Inca Empire expanded precisely through military conquest, tribute extraction, and incorporation of conquered peoples into a hierarchical imperial structure.

There's significant debate among archaeologists and anthropologists about this, but here are the commonly cited criteria:

Traditional "Civilization" Criteria (V. Gordon Childe's model, ~1950):

Minimal requirements typically include:

  1. Urban centers - Dense population settlements
  2. Surplus production - Agriculture beyond subsistence needs
  3. Social stratification - Some degree of class/status differentiation
  4. Specialized labor - Full-time craftspeople, priests, administrators
  5. Complex institutions - Organized religion, government, or trade networks
  6. Monumental architecture - Public buildings/structures
  7. Writing or record-keeping - Not always present (e.g., Inca used quipu)
  8. Long-distance trade networks

The Hierarchy Question:

Traditional view: Social stratification (hierarchy) is considered a defining feature of civilization by most scholars.

Key point: There's debate about whether all complex societies necessarily have ruling echelons, but all societies traditionally labeled "civilizations" (Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, Mesoamerica, Andes) had them.

Modern Scholarly Debates:

1. "Civilization" vs "Complex Society":

  • Many scholars now prefer "complex society" because "civilization" carries colonial baggage
  • Implies "civilized" vs "uncivilized" (primitive)
  • Often coded as "like us" vs "not like us"

2. Can complex societies exist without hierarchy?

Evidence of less-hierarchical complexity:

  • Indus Valley Civilization (~3300-1300 BCE) - Debated whether it had kings; evidence suggests more egalitarian than contemporaries, but still had social differentiation
  • Çatalhöyük (7100-5700 BCE Turkey) - Large settlement (~8,000 people) with minimal evidence of hierarchy, though not typically called a "civilization"
  • Some Pueblo societies - Sophisticated architecture and organization with relatively flat hierarchies
  • Pre-state Maya (some periods) - Complex but without centralized authority

However: Even "egalitarian" complex societies show:

  • Age/gender hierarchies
  • Ritual specialists with status
  • Wealth differentiation
  • Some individuals with more influence

3. The "Heterarchy" concept:

  • Some archaeologists argue for "heterarchy" (multiple overlapping power structures) rather than simple hierarchy
  • But this is still social differentiation

Your Friend's Definition:

Your friend defines civilization as: "a situation where a ruling echelon 'civilizes' the collected people according to the values and definitions of the rulers"

This is essentially accurate for what archaeologists traditionally call "civilization," though:

  • It's a critical/political definition rather than neutral
  • Emphasizes power dynamics that scholars once downplayed
  • Aligns with how James C. Scott defines it in "Against the Grain"

The Core Issue:

Can you have:

  • Large population (10,000+)
  • Monumental architecture
  • Specialized crafts
  • Long-distance trade
  • Complex ritual/knowledge systems

WITHOUT ruling elites?

Archaeological consensus: Extremely rare or nonexistent at scale. Even societies without kings show:

  • Elders with authority
  • Ritual specialists with power
  • Economic differentiation
  • Gender hierarchies
  • Some form of coordinated decision-making that privileges certain voices

Bottom Line:

If we use strict minimal requirements:

  1. Urban concentration (5,000+ people)
  2. Surplus production
  3. Specialized, non-food-producing labor
  4. Some form of social differentiation
  5. Monumental public works

Then hierarchy appears inherent to "civilization" as traditionally defined.

However: Degree matters. There's a spectrum from:

  • Minimal hierarchy (some Pueblo societies, early Cahokia)
  • Moderate stratification (possibly Indus Valley)
  • Extreme hierarchy (Inca, Aztec, Egypt)

The archaeological evidence shows that all major pre-Columbian American "civilizations" fell toward the moderate-to-extreme hierarchy end, with ruling classes, warfare, and human sacrifice.

Your friend's claim that they operated through "cooperation and humility" without hierarchies is not supported by evidence.
