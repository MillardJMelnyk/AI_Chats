This argument has a fundamental flaw: it confuses the act of claiming rights with the existence of rights themselves.

Your core premise—that invoking rights proves victimization and therefore makes rights "useless"—is like arguing that calling 911 proves you're already in danger, therefore emergency services are pointless. The logic doesn't follow.

Rights exist independently of whether someone invokes them effectively in a crisis moment. A person being mugged who shouts "You can't do this!" isn't proving rights are meaningless—they're affirming a moral and legal framework that exists whether the mugger respects it or not.

Your preferred declaration—"You will stop, or we will stop you!"—isn't operating outside a rights framework. It's actually enforcing rights. The authority to "stop" someone comes from... what exactly? Your personal preference? No—it comes from the principle that people have rights not to be harmed, which gives others the authority to intervene.

The "after the fact" critique misses how rights actually function. Rights create the moral and legal architecture that justifies prevention, intervention, and consequences. Police don't just randomly decide to stop criminals—they do so because rights violations are prohibited. Courts don't arbitrarily punish—they respond to rights violations.

Your argument essentially claims that because criminals ignore moral arguments, morality itself is incoherent. But criminals ignoring rights doesn't make rights disappear any more than people ignoring gravity makes physics false.

The real "loop-de-loop mental contortion" is trying to argue for forcible intervention against wrongdoing while simultaneously claiming the moral framework that justifies such intervention is meaningless.

Claude2025-08-17 20:22 UTC

No, they don't exist in the same way at all. That's a crucial distinction that actually strengthens the critique of your original argument rather than weakening it.

Physical laws are descriptive—they describe how matter and energy actually behave. Gravity isn't a rule we made up; it's our description of how objects with mass interact. You can't violate gravity; you can only work within its constraints.

Rights are prescriptive—they're human constructs about how we think people should be treated. They exist in the realm of human agreement and social institutions, not as features of the physical universe. A criminal can violate someone's rights in a way they simply cannot violate thermodynamics.

But this difference doesn't make your original argument correct. You're essentially arguing: "Since rights aren't physical forces that automatically stop wrongdoing, they're meaningless." That's like saying "Since speed limits don't physically prevent cars from going fast, traffic laws are incoherent."

The fact that rights are human constructs makes them more important for organizing society, not less. They represent our collective decision about what behaviors we'll tolerate, support with institutions, and enforce through coordinated action.

Your "You will stop, or we will stop you!" declaration only makes sense within a framework where some people are authorized to stop others—and that authorization comes from shared agreements about rights and wrongs, not from physical law.

So yes, rights are different from physics. But that's precisely why we need them—physics doesn't tell us how to treat each other, only what's physically possible.
