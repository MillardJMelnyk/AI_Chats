I can see you're trying to express a complex idea about widespread psychological or social phenomena. Let me offer some thoughts on the terminology:

"Universal form of psychosis" - This is where the terminology becomes problematic. In clinical terms, psychosis refers to a specific set of symptoms involving a loss of contact with reality (hallucinations, delusions, severely disorganized thinking). It's not something that can be "universal" in the way you're describing, as it's a medical condition affecting individuals.

You might be reaching for terms like:

  • "Collective delusion" or "mass delusion"
  • "Social conditioning"
  • "Cultural mythology" or "dominant narrative"
  • "Ideological hegemony"
  • "False consciousness" (from Marxist theory)

"Deliberately generalized and normalized and inculturated" - These terms work better together. "Inculturated" means taught as part of culture, "normalized" means made to seem normal/acceptable, and "deliberately" suggests intentional design.

What you seem to be describing sounds more like a critique of how certain beliefs, values, or ways of thinking become so embedded in society that people accept them without question - even when they might be harmful or false. Thinkers like Noam Chomsky, Antonio Gramsci, or Michel Foucault have explored similar ideas about how power structures shape collective thinking.

What specific phenomenon were you trying to describe? That might help clarify the most precise terminology.

So is the correct term inculturated or enculturated?
Claude2025-09-03 17:12 UTC

Both terms exist, but "enculturated" is the more standard and widely used term in academic contexts.

Enculturation is the established anthropological/sociological term for the process by which individuals learn and internalize the culture they're born into - the norms, values, beliefs, and behaviors of their society.

Inculturated is less common and more specific to certain contexts (sometimes used in religious studies or when emphasizing the deliberate "inculcating" or instilling of specific beliefs).

So for your analysis of how racism and misogyny become psychologically embedded through cultural transmission, "enculturated racism and misogyny" would be the more precise terminology.

The word captures exactly what you're describing - these aren't just personal prejudices people develop, but systematic distortions that get transmitted through the normal process of cultural learning, making them seem natural and invisible to those who internalize them.
