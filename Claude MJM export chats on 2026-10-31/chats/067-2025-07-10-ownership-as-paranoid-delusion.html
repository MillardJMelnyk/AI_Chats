However, here are the closest matches I can identify:

Kevin Carson comes closest to your criteria. He wrote "Capitalist Nursery Fables: The Tragedy of Private Property, and the Farce of Its Defense" and takes a more radical anti-property stance than most. Carson argues that private property as currently constituted is fundamentally illegitimate and based on historical violence and state coercion. While he doesn't explicitly frame it as "paranoid delusion," his critique is quite thoroughgoing.

David Bollier focuses on commons-based alternatives but doesn't reject ownership as fundamentally perverse - he's more interested in expanding commons and collective ownership models rather than rejecting the concept entirely.

The reality is that most contemporary thinkers in this space - whether they're commons theorists, anarcho-communists, or post-capitalist philosophers - tend to maintain some conceptual framework for use rights, possession, or stewardship rather than completely rejecting all forms of ownership as delusional. They're more likely to critique specific forms of property (like absentee ownership or intellectual property) rather than ownership per se.

Your criteria are quite specific and radical - finding someone who views ownership itself as a paranoid delusion rather than just critiquing particular property regimes is genuinely rare in contemporary discourse.

Claude2025-07-10 14:52 UTC

Actually, there are some contemporary thinkers who do explore the psychological pathology of ownership, though they may not frame it exactly as "paranoid delusion."

Byung-Chul Han in his work on neoliberalism discusses how the obsession with ownership and accumulation reflects deeper psychological disorders of modern subjectivity - the inability to relate to others or the world without possessing and controlling.

Franco "Bifo" Berardi has written about how capitalist property relations create forms of mental pathology, particularly around the compulsive need to own and accumulate as a defense against anxiety and social fragmentation.

Mark Fisher (before his death in 2017) explored how capitalist realism creates psychological conditions where people cannot imagine relating to the world except through ownership and consumption - he saw this as a form of collective mental illness.

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari (though Guattari died in 1992, their work remains influential) analyzed capitalism as producing "schizophrenic" desires for possession that are fundamentally at odds with human flourishing.

Some contemporary eco-psychologists like David Loy have written about the psychological roots of the ownership mindset as a form of "hungry ghost" mentality - never satisfied, always grasping, rooted in fundamental insecurity about one's place in the world.

But you're right that few frame it as directly as you're describing - as a form of paranoid delusion where the very concept of exclusive ownership represents a pathological break from reality. Most still engage with ownership as a social institution to be critiqued rather than a psychological pathology to be diagnosed.

Claude2025-07-10 14:57 UTC

Given the radical nature of your thesis, here are the most effective approaches:

Start with clinical precision. Define your terms clearly - what constitutes "delusional" and "paranoid" in this context, drawing from established psychological/psychiatric frameworks. This prevents dismissal as mere polemic and forces engagement with your actual argument.

Build the logical chain systematically:

  1. Ownership requires believing others will deprive you of goods
  2. This belief is typically held without evidence (delusional)
  3. The belief becomes so consuming that sharing becomes unthinkable (paranoid)
  4. Therefore ownership is a paranoid delusion institutionalized

Use concrete examples. Show how people defend property they'll never use, against threats that don't exist, while surrounded by abundance. The billionaire hoarding wealth while others starve is your clearest case study.

Anticipate the strongest objections. People will argue scarcity is real, that property enables planning, that sharing leads to tragedy of commons. Have devastating responses ready.

Choose your venue strategically. This won't get published in mainstream economics journals. Consider philosophy journals focused on political philosophy, critical theory publications, or radical anthropology venues where the framework for questioning fundamental assumptions already exists.

Frame it as diagnostic, not prescriptive. Present it as identifying a psychological phenomenon, not initially as a political program. This makes it harder to dismiss as utopian.

Consider a book rather than articles. This thesis needs space to develop fully and address inevitable counterarguments comprehensively.

The key is treating this as serious psychological analysis, not political rhetoric.
