Claude2026-01-07 19:30 UTC

#1 — Template structure definition

  1. DSL (domain-specific language): Define a grammar for specifying elements, relationships (crossover, divergence, proximity), and parameters. Flexible, versionable, human-readable.

  2. Modular plugin architecture: Each element type (indicator, price action, comparison) is a discrete component. Relationships are configurable connectors. Extensible without rewriting core logic.

#2 — Shape representation and matching

  1. Normalized feature vectors + distance metrics: Normalize each element to comparable scale (z-score or min-max), concatenate into vector, measure similarity via Euclidean or cosine distance. Simple, fast, interpretable.

  2. Dynamic Time Warping (DTW): Handles temporal distortion—matches shapes even if stretched/compressed in time. Computationally heavier but captures "looks like" better than rigid alignment.

#3 — Indicator line derivation

  1. Match-weighted aggregation: For each historical match, record subsequent direction and magnitude. Weight contribution by match quality (closer match = higher weight). Aggregate to produce probability and expected magnitude.

  2. Windowed outcome measurement: Define fixed forward window (e.g., N bars). Measure direction and magnitude within that window. Consistent measurement frame across all matches.

Questions or continue?

Millard2026-01-07 19:59 UTC
Let's put a pin in those a moment. Charts are geometric. Isn't there already extremely sophisticated math for matching, measuring, evaluating, and comparing sets of geometric shapes with each other?
Claude2026-01-07 19:59 UTC

Yes. Relevant fields:

  1. Procrustes analysis: Optimal alignment of shapes, removes translation/rotation/scale to compare pure form.

  2. Fourier descriptors: Represent shapes as frequency components. Compact, rotation/scale invariant, good for comparing curve profiles.

  3. Fréchet distance: "Dog-walking distance" for curves—measures similarity accounting for flow along the path. More appropriate for time-ordered data than Hausdorff.

  4. Shape context: Histogram-based descriptor of point relationships. Robust to deformation.

  5. Moment invariants: Statistical shape descriptors invariant to geometric transforms.

  6. Persistent homology (TDA): Topological features that persist across scales. Captures structural properties, ignores noise.

These are mature, with optimized implementations.

Fréchet or Fourier descriptors likely fit best for chart curves. DTW is essentially the discrete time-series cousin of Fréchet.

Want to explore one of these as the matching backbone?
